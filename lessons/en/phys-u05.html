Temperature and the Kelvin Scale

What you will learn
  • Understand what temperature really measures
  • Convert between Celsius and kelvin
  • Know why gas laws need kelvin

What does temperature mean?

The particles (atoms or molecules) of any substance are always moving. Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of these particles: the faster they move, the higher the temperature.

Temperature is not heat. Heat is energy that flows from the hotter body to the colder one, while temperature describes the state of the particles inside one body.

The Celsius scale and the kelvin scale

On the Celsius scale water freezes at 0∘C and boils at 100∘C. The kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, the coldest possible temperature, where particle motion almost stops.

Absolute zero is −273∘C, so we get kelvin by adding 273 to the Celsius value. One kelvin is the same size as one Celsius degree.

  • A temperature difference is the same on both scales: a rise of 10∘C equals a rise of 10K.
  • A kelvin temperature is never negative.

Why use kelvin for gases?

The kinetic energy of a gas's particles sets its volume and pressure: at constant pressure the volume grows as this energy grows, and at constant volume the pressure does. That energy is zero at absolute zero, so volume or pressure is proportional to the kelvin temperature, not the Celsius one. With Celsius, 0∘C would wrongly give a gas of zero volume.

Laws and rules

Converting between scalesT=t+273t is the Celsius temperature in ∘C and T the kelvin temperature in K. To go back, subtract 273.
Temperature changeΔT=ΔtA difference between two temperatures does not change with the conversion, so never add 273 to a difference.

Worked examples

Convert 47∘C to kelvin.
Show the solution
  1. Use the rule: kelvin = Celsius + 273.
  2. Substitute: 47+273=320.
Answer: 320K.
The temperature of some water rises from 20∘C to 70∘C. What is the rise in kelvin?
Show the solution
  1. Find the difference in Celsius: 70−20=50∘C.
  2. The same difference holds in kelvin, so we do not add 273 to a difference.
Answer: 50K.
A gas is at 250K. What is that in Celsius? If its temperature rises from 27∘C to 54∘C, does the kinetic energy of its particles double?
Show the solution
  1. For the first part, subtract: 250−273=−23∘C.
  2. For the second part, convert to kelvin: 27+273=300 and 54+273=327.
  3. The ratio is 300327​=1.09, an increase of only 9%.
  4. Kinetic energy is proportional to kelvin, so it does not double when the Celsius value doubles.
Answer: −23∘C, and no, it does not double (about 9% more).
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting to convert: reading 27∘C as 27K. The correct value is 300K.
  • Adding 273 to a temperature difference. A change is the same on both scales.
  • Thinking that double the Celsius value means double the energy. Ratios must be taken in kelvin.
  • Mixing up heat and temperature: heat is energy in transit, temperature measures average kinetic energy.
Summary
  • Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of particles.
  • Kelvin = Celsius + 273.
  • A temperature difference is the same on both scales.
  • Gas laws need kelvin because absolute zero is the true zero.

Heat Quantity and Specific Heat

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Calculate the heat needed to change a body's temperature
  • Compare materials by their specific heat
  • Solve mixing and calorimeter problems with: heat lost by hot = heat gained by cold

Specific heat

The specific heat of a material is the heat needed to raise the temperature of 1kg of it by 1K. Its unit is J/(kg⋅K).

Water has a large specific heat (4200J/(kg⋅K)), so it needs a lot of heat to warm up and cools slowly. Metals have small specific heats, so they warm up quickly with the same heat.

Heat quantity

The heat needed grows with the mass, with the specific heat, and with the temperature change. So we multiply the three.

If two bodies have equal mass and receive equal heat, the one with the smaller specific heat gets hotter.

Mixing and the calorimeter

When two bodies are mixed in an insulated container no heat is lost. The hot body loses heat and the cold one gains exactly the same amount until both reach the same equilibrium temperature.

A calorimeter is an insulated container holding hot metal and water, so we can find the metal's specific heat from the heat the water gained.

  • Write the heat lost by the hot body: mass × specific heat × (its start temperature − equilibrium temperature).
  • Write the heat gained by the cold body: mass × specific heat × (equilibrium temperature − its start temperature).
  • Set them equal and solve. If the substance is the same, the specific heat cancels.

Laws and rules

Heat quantityQ=mcΔTQ is heat in J, m mass in kg, c specific heat in J/(kg⋅K), and ΔT the temperature change in K (or ∘C).
Thermal equilibriumQlost​=Qgained​Valid in an insulated container that exchanges no heat with the surroundings; both sides in J.

Worked examples

How many joules are needed to heat 2kg of water from 20∘C to 45∘C? (specific heat of water 4200J/(kg⋅K))
Show the solution
  1. Temperature change: 45−20=25K.
  2. Substitute: 2×4200×25.
  3. 2×25=50, then 50×4200=210000.
Answer: 2.1×105J.
1kg of water at 90∘C is mixed with 2kg of water at 30∘C in an insulated container. Find the equilibrium temperature.
Show the solution
  1. It is the same water, so the specific heat cancels from both sides.
  2. Let the final temperature be x. Lost: 1×(90−x), gained: 2×(x−30).
  3. Set equal: 90−x=2x−60.
  4. Add: 150=3x, so x=50.
  5. Check: the hot water loses 40 degrees and the cold gains 20 degrees with twice the mass, so the heats match.
Answer: 50∘C.
A 0.5kg piece of metal cools from 100∘C to 30∘C in an insulated calorimeter, and the water gains 8400J. Find the metal's specific heat.
Show the solution
  1. Drop in temperature: 100−30=70K.
  2. The heat lost by the metal equals what the water gained: 8400J.
  3. 8400=0.5×c×70, so 8400=35c.
  4. c=358400​=240.
  5. Check: 0.5×240×70=8400.
Answer: 240J/(kg⋅K).
Common mistakes
  • Using the equilibrium temperature in the formula instead of the difference. You always need the change: final − initial.
  • Forgetting the masses when mixing. Heat depends on mass, so the answer is not simply halfway.
  • Assuming the equilibrium temperature is the plain average of the two. That is true only if the masses are equal and the substance is the same.
  • Mixing joules with kilojoules: check the unit of the specific heat before substituting.
Summary
  • Q=mcΔT.
  • A larger specific heat means slower warming for the same heat.
  • In an insulated container: heat lost = heat gained.
  • The equilibrium temperature lies between the two and closer to the more massive body when the substance is the same.

Changes of State and Latent Heat

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Explain why temperature stays constant during melting and boiling
  • Calculate the heat needed for a change of state
  • Combine several stages in one problem

Where does the heat go?

When we heat ice at 0∘C its temperature stops rising even though heating continues. The heat does not raise the temperature here; it breaks the bonds between particles and turns ice into water.

The same happens in boiling: the temperature stays at 100∘C while water turns to steam, because the heat is used to free the particles from each other.

Latent heat

Latent heat is the heat needed to change 1kg of a substance from one state to another without changing its temperature. For melting it is the latent heat of fusion, for boiling the latent heat of vaporization.

The heat of vaporization is much larger than that of fusion, because vaporizing separates the particles completely. That is why steam burns worse than water at the same temperature: when it condenses on skin it releases its latent heat on top of the heat from cooling.

The reverse also holds: freezing and condensation release the same latent heat per kilogram to the surroundings.

A multi-stage problem

To heat ice until it becomes warm water we split the problem into stages and add their heats: warming the ice to 0∘C, then melting, then warming the water. In a change-of-state stage use latent heat; in a temperature-change stage use specific heat.

Laws and rules

Heat to meltQ=mLf​Q in J, m in kg, Lf​ the latent heat of fusion in J/kg. For ice: 3.3×105J/kg.
Heat to vaporizeQ=mLv​Lv​ is the latent heat of vaporization; for boiling water 2.3×106J/kg. The temperature stays constant during vaporization.

Worked examples

How many joules are needed to melt 2kg of ice at 0∘C? (latent heat of fusion 3.3×105J/kg)
Show the solution
  1. The ice is already at its melting point, so only latent heat is involved.
  2. 2×3.3×105=6.6×105.
Answer: 6.6×105J.
How many joules are needed to vaporize 0.5kg of boiling water at 100∘C? (latent heat of vaporization 2.3×106J/kg)
Show the solution
  1. The temperature is constant during vaporization, so no specific heat is needed.
  2. 0.5×2.3×106=1.15×106.
Answer: 1.15×106J.
How many joules are needed to turn 1kg of ice at 0∘C into water at 10∘C? (specific heat of water 4200J/(kg⋅K))
Show the solution
  1. Stage 1 (melting): 1×3.3×105=330000J.
  2. Stage 2 (warming the water from 0 to 10∘C): 1×4200×10=42000J.
  3. Add the stages: 330000+42000=372000.
Answer: 3.72×105J.
Common mistakes
  • Using specific heat during a change of state. During melting and boiling the temperature does not change.
  • Skipping a stage in a long problem: sketch the stages in order first.
  • Thinking the heat added during melting is lost. It becomes energy stored in the bonds.
  • Believing steam is hotter than boiling water: both are at 100∘C, but steam carries extra latent heat.
Summary
  • During a change of state the temperature stays constant.
  • The heat in that stage is Q=mL.
  • The heat of vaporization is larger than the heat of fusion.
  • In multi-stage problems add the heat of each stage.

Modes of Heat Transfer

What you will learn
  • Tell conduction, convection and radiation apart
  • Explain everyday effects with the right mode
  • Understand how thermal insulation works

Three modes

Heat always flows from the hotter to the colder place by three modes: conduction in solids, convection in liquids and gases, and radiation, which needs no medium.

That is how the Sun's heat reaches us across empty space: by radiation, which conduction and convection cannot do.

Conduction and convection

In conduction fast-moving particles collide with their neighbours and pass energy on without the material itself moving. Metals conduct well because they have free electrons, while wood and air are poor conductors.

In convection a hot lump of fluid rises because it is less dense, and cold, denser fluid sinks to take its place, creating convection currents. That is how room air is warmed from a heater at the bottom.

Thermal insulation

An insulator is a poor conductor that slows heat transfer. Air trapped in small pockets, as in wool, cork and the gap of a double-glazed window, blocks conduction and prevents convection currents.

The rate of heat flow by conduction grows with the area, the temperature difference and the conductivity, and falls as the thickness increases.

Laws and rules

Rate of conductionP=dkAΔT​P is the rate of heat flow in watts (J/s), k the conductivity in W/(m⋅K), A the area in m2, ΔT the temperature difference across the faces in K, and d the thickness in m.

Worked examples

Name the main mode of transfer in each case: (a) a metal spoon in hot soup, (b) water heated in a pot from below, (c) the warmth of the Sun on your face.
Show the solution
  1. (a) A spoon is a solid, so the transfer is by conduction.
  2. (b) Water is a fluid: hot water rises and cold water sinks, so it is convection.
  3. (c) Between the Sun and Earth is almost empty space, so it is radiation.
Answer: (a) conduction, (b) convection, (c) radiation.
Why does a metal handle feel colder than a wooden handle in the same room?
Show the solution
  1. Both are at the same room temperature.
  2. Metal is a good conductor, so it draws heat away from your hand quickly.
  3. Wood is a poor conductor, so it draws only a little.
  4. What you feel is how fast heat leaves your hand, not the temperature.
Answer: Because metal conducts heat away from the hand faster than wood does.
A wall has area 10m2, thickness 0.2m, conductivity 0.8W/(m⋅K) and a temperature difference of 20K across its faces. Find the rate of heat flow through it. What does the rate become if the thickness is doubled?
Show the solution
  1. Substitute: 0.20.8×10×20​.
  2. Numerator: 0.8×10×20=160.
  3. Rate =0.2160​=800W.
  4. The rate is inversely proportional to the thickness, so with 0.4m (double) it is halved: 2800​=400W.
Answer: 800W, then 400W after doubling the thickness.
Common mistakes
  • Saying convection happens in metals. Convection is only for fluids (liquids and gases).
  • Thinking radiation needs air. Radiation travels through a vacuum.
  • Believing an insulator "blocks cold". It only slows heat leaving or entering.
  • Judging by the feel of your hand: something that feels colder may simply be a better conductor, not a lower temperature.
Summary
  • Conduction in solids, convection in fluids, radiation with or without a medium.
  • Metals conduct well; trapped air insulates well.
  • Convection comes from the density difference between hot and cold fluid.
  • Conduction falls with thickness and grows with temperature difference.

Thermal Expansion

What you will learn
  • Explain why materials expand when heated
  • Calculate the increase in length of a rod
  • Know what happens to a hole in a plate when it is heated

Why do materials expand?

When heated, the particles vibrate with a larger amplitude, so the average distance between them grows and the whole body gets bigger. That is why gaps are left between rail sections and bridge parts, so they do not buckle in summer.

Linear expansion

The increase in length of a rod is proportional to its original length and to the rise in temperature. The coefficient of linear expansion is a constant of each material and tells how much it expands: aluminium has a larger one than iron.

If the body is cooled it shrinks by the same amount. The coefficient is very small, so expansion is noticeable only for long objects or large temperature changes.

Expansion of area and volume

A plate expands in length and width together, so its area grows by about twice the fractional increase in length, and a solid's volume by about three times it.

Liquids expand more than solids for the same heating, which is why mercury rises in a thermometer.

Expansion of a hole

If we heat a plate that has a hole, the hole gets bigger, not smaller. Imagine the hole filled with the same material: heating it would expand it in proportion, so the hole expands by the same ratio as the plate. In the same way the diameter of a metal ring grows when heated.

Laws and rules

Linear expansionΔL=αL0​ΔTα is the coefficient of linear expansion in 1/∘C (or 1/K), L0​ the original length in m, and ΔT the temperature change in K; ΔL comes out in m.

Worked examples

A 10m rod with α=1.2×10−5 per ∘C is heated by 50∘C. By how much does its length increase?
Show the solution
  1. Substitute: 1.2×10−5×10×50.
  2. 10×50=500, then 1.2×10−5×500=6×10−3m.
  3. Convert to millimetres: 6×10−3m=6mm.
Answer: 6mm.
A rail 20m long with α=1.2×10−5 per ∘C warms from 10∘C to 40∘C. How much longer does it become?
Show the solution
  1. Temperature change: 40−10=30∘C.
  2. 1.2×10−5×20×30.
  3. 20×30=600, then 1.2×10−5×600=7.2×10−3m=7.2mm.
Answer: 7.2mm.
A metal plate has a hole of diameter 10cm and α=1.2×10−5 per ∘C. It is heated by 100∘C. What happens to the hole's diameter, and what is it now?
Show the solution
  1. The hole expands as if it were made of the same material, so its diameter grows.
  2. Increase =1.2×10−5×10×100=1.2×10−2cm.
  3. New diameter =10+0.012=10.012cm.
Answer: The diameter grows to 10.012cm.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the hole shrinks because the material expands into it. The hole always grows with the plate.
  • Forgetting unit conversion: the answer is in metres and needs converting to millimetres if asked.
  • Using the final length instead of the original length in the formula.
  • Confusing expansion with gaining mass: the mass does not change, only the density falls.
Summary
  • Heating increases particle spacing, so materials expand.
  • ΔL=αL0​ΔT.
  • A hole in a plate gets bigger when heated.
  • Cooling reverses expansion: shrinking by the same amount.

The Gas Laws

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Apply Boyle's law and Charles's law
  • Use the combined gas law when all three variables change
  • Always remember to convert temperature to kelvin

What controls a gas?

The state of a fixed amount of gas is described by three quantities: pressure, volume and kelvin temperature. Pressure comes from particles hitting the container walls, volume is the space of the container, and temperature measures kinetic energy.

Boyle's law (temperature constant)

At constant temperature, a smaller volume makes the particles hit the walls more often, so the pressure rises. Halving the volume doubles the pressure, so pressure × volume stays constant.

The relation between pressure and volume is inverse, and its graph is a falling curve.

Charles's law (pressure constant)

At constant pressure a gas expands when heated: its volume is directly proportional to its kelvin temperature. Doubling the kelvin temperature doubles the volume.

The graph of volume against kelvin is a straight line from zero. Likewise at constant volume, pressure is directly proportional to kelvin, so a tyre at 200kPa at 27∘C reaches 240kPa when warmed to 87∘C.

The combined gas law

When all three quantities change together we merge the laws into one: pressure × volume ÷ kelvin temperature is constant for a fixed amount of gas. Write it for the initial and final states and set them equal.

  • Convert every temperature to kelvin.
  • Write the law for the two states: initial and final.
  • Substitute what is known and solve for the unknown.

Laws and rules

Boyle's law (T constant)P1​V1​=P2​V2​Pressure in any unit (for example kPa) and volume in any unit (for example L), as long as both states use the same units.
Charles's law (P constant)T1​V1​​=T2​V2​​Temperatures in kelvin, never Celsius. Volume in the same unit on both sides.
Pressure law (V constant)T1​P1​​=T2​P2​​Temperatures in kelvin; pressure in the same unit on both sides.
Combined gas lawT1​P1​V1​​=T2​P2​V2​​Fixed amount of gas, temperatures in kelvin. At constant volume TP​ stays constant.

Worked examples

A gas at 100kPa and 6L is compressed to 2L at constant temperature. What is the new pressure?
Show the solution
  1. Boyle's law: 100×6=P2​×2.
  2. 600=2P2​.
  3. New pressure =2600​=300kPa.
  4. Check: the volume became one third, so the pressure is three times as large.
Answer: 300kPa.
A gas has volume 2L at 27∘C and constant pressure. It is heated to 177∘C. What is its new volume?
Show the solution
  1. Convert to kelvin: 27+273=300 and 177+273=450.
  2. Charles's law: 3002​=450V2​​.
  3. New volume =3002×450​=3L.
Answer: 3L.
A gas at 100kPa, 6L and 300K is compressed to 3L and heated to 600K. What is its final pressure?
Show the solution
  1. Write the combined law: 300100×6​=600P2​×3​.
  2. Left side: 300600​=2.
  3. 2=600P2​×3​, so 3P2​=1200.
  4. Final pressure =31200​=400kPa.
  5. Check: halving the volume doubles the pressure, doubling the kelvin temperature doubles it again, so 100×4=400.
Answer: 400kPa.
Common mistakes
  • Using Celsius in Charles's law or the combined law. Always convert to kelvin.
  • Mixing the inverse relation (Boyle: volume and pressure) with the direct one (Charles: volume and temperature).
  • Thinking that doubling the Celsius value doubles the volume.
  • Changing the unit of volume or pressure between the two states without converting.
Summary
  • Boyle: PV is constant at constant temperature.
  • Charles: V/T is constant at constant pressure.
  • At constant volume: P/T is constant.
  • The combined law merges them, and kelvin is always required.

The First Law of Thermodynamics and Engines

What you will learn
  • Apply the first law with correct signs
  • Tell an adiabatic process from an isothermal one
  • Calculate the efficiency of a heat engine

The first law: conservation of energy

The internal energy of a gas is the total kinetic energy of its particles. It changes in two ways: by adding heat or by work. The first law says that the heat a gas absorbs is partly turned into work done by the gas, and the rest raises its internal energy.

Energy is neither created nor destroyed, only converted.

The signs

By convention heat is positive when the gas absorbs it and negative when it loses it. Work is positive when the gas does work on the surroundings (it expands) and negative when work is done on it (it is compressed).

If work is done on the gas its internal energy rises, and if the gas loses heat its internal energy falls.

Two special processes

An isothermal process happens at constant temperature, so the internal energy does not change. Then the heat absorbed equals exactly the work done by the gas.

An adiabatic process has no heat exchange with the surroundings, so the heat is zero. If the gas expands and does work, its internal energy falls by the amount of work and its temperature drops.

If the volume stays constant the gas does no work (the work is zero), so all the heat absorbed raises its internal energy.

The heat engine

A heat engine absorbs heat from a hot reservoir, turns part of it into useful work, and rejects the rest to a cold reservoir. The work is the difference between the heat absorbed and the heat rejected.

Efficiency is the ratio of useful work to heat absorbed, and it is always below 100% because some heat is rejected. The greatest possible efficiency grows as the gap between the hot and cold reservoir temperatures widens.

Laws and rules

First lawΔU=Q−WAll in joules. Q is positive when the gas absorbs heat, and W is positive when the gas does work.
Isothermal processQ=WThe temperature is constant, so ΔU=0.
Adiabatic processΔU=−WNo heat exchange, so Q=0.
Engine efficiencye=Qh​W​×100%W=Qh​−Qc​, with Qh​ the heat absorbed and Qc​ the heat rejected, in joules.

Worked examples

A gas absorbs 500J of heat and does 200J of work on its surroundings. How much does its internal energy change?
Show the solution
  1. Heat is absorbed, so Q=+500J. The gas does work, so W=+200J.
  2. ΔU=500−200.
  3. The result is +300J, so the internal energy rises.
Answer: It rises by 300J.
100J of work is done on a gas to compress it, and the gas loses 30J of heat. How much does its internal energy change?
Show the solution
  1. The gas loses heat, so Q=−30J.
  2. Work is done on the gas, so W=−100J.
  3. ΔU=−30−(−100)=−30+100=+70.
Answer: It rises by 70J.
A heat engine absorbs 800J from the hot reservoir and rejects 600J to the cold one. Find its work and its efficiency.
Show the solution
  1. Work =800−600=200J.
  2. Efficiency =800200​×100%=25%.
  3. Check: the ratio is below 100%, as it must be.
Answer: Work 200J, efficiency 25%.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up the sign of work: positive work is work done by the gas. If the gas is compressed, the work is negative.
  • Thinking a gas always warms when heat is added. In an isothermal process all the heat becomes work and the temperature is constant.
  • Assuming an adiabatic process has constant temperature. It has no heat exchange, and the temperature does change.
  • Dividing the work by the heat rejected instead of the heat absorbed when finding efficiency.
Summary
  • ΔU=Q−W.
  • Isothermal: the temperature is constant, so ΔU=0.
  • Adiabatic: Q=0.
  • Efficiency =Qh​W​, always below 100%.
