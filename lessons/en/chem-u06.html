Energy in Reactions: Exothermic and Endothermic

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell exothermic from endothermic reactions by what the surroundings do.
  • Link the reaction type to the sign of ΔH and to the energy diagram.
  • Calculate heat released or absorbed from a thermochemical equation and moles.

System and surroundings

The system is the reacting substances themselves; the surroundings are everything around them: the water in the beaker, the air, your hand. Energy is never destroyed or created; it only moves between system and surroundings.

So if the surroundings gain energy, the system lost exactly that much, and if the surroundings lose energy, the system gained it.

Exothermic and endothermic reactions

Practical rule: look at the surroundings, not at the reaction. Surroundings warmer means exothermic; surroundings colder means endothermic.

  • Exothermic: releases energy to the surroundings, so the beaker or the air around it gets warmer (burning is an example).
  • Endothermic: absorbs energy from the surroundings, so what is around it gets colder (a cold pack is an example).

Enthalpy and the sign of its change

Enthalpy is the energy stored in the reacting substances. What matters is its change: energy of the products minus energy of the reactants.

In an exothermic reaction the products end up with less energy than the reactants because the difference went out to the surroundings, so ΔH is negative. In an endothermic reaction the products rise, so ΔH is positive.

On an energy diagram read the direction from the reactants level to the products level: going down means exothermic, going up means endothermic.

Thermochemical equations

A thermochemical equation is a balanced equation written together with ΔH, and that value belongs to exactly the number of moles written in the equation.

If a different number of moles reacts, scale the value by the ratio of moles. If you reverse the equation, the value stays the same but its sign flips.

Laws and rules

Change in enthalpyΔH=Hproducts​−Hreactants​Energy in kJ. A negative result means exothermic; a positive result means endothermic.
Heat from molesq=ΔHper mol​×nq in kJ, ΔH per mole in kJ/mol, n in mol. n=mass÷molar mass (g divided by g/mol).

Worked examples

The reactants hold 80 kJ and the products hold 50 kJ. Find ΔH and the reaction type.
Show the solution
  1. Write the law: ΔH=Hproducts​−Hreactants​.
  2. Substitute: ΔH=50−80=−30 kJ.
  3. The sign is negative: the products have less energy and the difference went to the surroundings.
Answer: ΔH=−30 kJ, so the reaction is exothermic.
An exothermic reaction has ΔH=−100 kJ/mol. How many kJ are released when 0.25 mol reacts?
Show the solution
  1. Use q=ΔHper mol​×n.
  2. Use the magnitude because the question asks how much is released: q=100×0.25=25 kJ.
  3. Check: a quarter of a mole gives a quarter of 100 kJ, which is 25.
Answer: 25 kJ are released.
For 2HX2​+OX2​​2HX2​O, ΔH=−572 kJ. How many kJ are released when 8 g of hydrogen burns? (Molar mass of HX2​ is 2 g/mol)
Show the solution
  1. The value 572 belongs to 2 mol of HX2​ as written.
  2. Moles of hydrogen: 28​=4 mol.
  3. Ratio of what reacted to what is written: 24​=2, twice the equation.
  4. Heat: 572×2=1144 kJ.
Answer: 1144 kJ are released.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking exothermic means ΔH is positive because heat "increased". The heat increased in the surroundings; the system lost energy, so ΔH is negative.
  • Using the value from the thermochemical equation without checking the real number of moles. The value belongs only to the moles written; convert the mass to moles and compare with the equation.
  • Reversing the equation but keeping the sign. Reversing flips the sign: an exothermic reaction becomes endothermic with the same value.
Summary
  • Surroundings warm up: exothermic. Surroundings cool down: endothermic.
  • ΔH = energy of products - energy of reactants: negative for exothermic, positive for endothermic.
  • On a diagram, going down from reactants to products means exothermic.
  • Heat = ΔH per mole × moles, and reversing the equation flips the sign.

Specific Heat

What you will learn
  • Understand why materials heat up at different speeds.
  • Use heat = mass × specific heat × temperature change.
  • Solve for any unknown: heat, mass, temperature change or specific heat.
  • Link the heat from burning a fuel to warming water.

What is specific heat?

Specific heat is the heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1∘C. Its unit is J/(g⋅∘C).

Keep the two ideas apart: heat is energy in transfer and is measured in joules, while temperature measures how hot the substance is and is measured in degrees Celsius.

Water has a relatively large specific heat (about 4.18J/(g⋅∘C)), while metals have small ones (aluminium about 0.9).

What does the value tell you?

A large value means the substance needs a lot of energy for each degree, so it heats and cools slowly. That is why coastal cities have milder weather.

A small value means little energy changes the temperature a lot, so it heats quickly, like a metal pan on a flame.

How do we calculate?

The heat transferred is proportional to the mass, to the specific heat and to the temperature change, which is the difference: final temperature minus initial temperature.

We take the size of that difference when we calculate the heat absorbed or lost. When the unknown is not the heat, rearrange by dividing by the remaining factors, then check that the units cancel.

Fuel and water

When a fuel burns under a container of water, the heat released passes to the water. If nothing escapes to the air, the heat the fuel lost equals the heat the water gained.

First find the heat from ΔH and the moles (in kJ), then convert it to joules by multiplying by 1000 before using a specific heat given in joules.

Laws and rules

Specific heat lawq=m×c×ΔTq heat in J, m mass in g, c specific heat in J/(g⋅∘C), ΔT temperature change in ∘C.
Finding the temperature changeΔT=m×cq​Divide the heat by the product of mass and specific heat; the result is in ∘C.
Finding the specific heatc=m×ΔTq​Heat in J and mass in g; the result is in J/(g⋅∘C).

Worked examples

The specific heat of water is 4J/(g⋅∘C) (rounded). How many joules does 100 g of water absorb when it warms by 10∘C?
Show the solution
  1. Write: q=m×c×ΔT.
  2. Substitute: q=100×4×10.
  3. Compute: 100×4=400, then 400×10=4000 J.
Answer: 4000 J.
A 50 g metal piece absorbs 900 J and warms from 20∘C to 40∘C. What is its specific heat? Which warms faster for the same mass: this metal or water?
Show the solution
  1. The rise: 40−20=20∘C.
  2. c=50×20900​=1000900​.
  3. Compute: 1000900​=0.9J/(g⋅∘C).
  4. The value 0.9 is much smaller than the specific heat of water, 4, so the metal needs less energy per degree.
Answer: c=0.9J/(g⋅∘C), and the metal warms faster than water.
0.25 mol of a fuel with ΔH=−120 kJ/mol burns, and 500 g of water absorbs all the heat released. By how much does the water's temperature rise? (Specific heat of water: 4J/(g⋅∘C))
Show the solution
  1. Heat released: 120×0.25=30 kJ (take the size).
  2. Convert to joules: 30×1000=30000 J.
  3. The water gains all of it: ΔT=500×430000​=200030000​.
  4. Compute: 200030000​=15∘C.
Answer: The water's temperature rises by 15∘C.
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting the mass and computing heat from specific heat and rise only. Specific heat is for one gram, so multiply by the number of grams.
  • Using the final temperature instead of the change. The law needs ΔT: final minus initial.
  • Thinking a bigger value means faster heating. It is the opposite: a large specific heat means slow heating.
  • Mixing kJ and J. The specific heat is in joules, so if the heat is in kJ, multiply it by 1000 first.
Summary
  • Specific heat is the heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 g by one degree.
  • q=m×c×ΔT.
  • ΔT = final - initial, and the unit is J, not kJ.
  • A large specific heat means slow heating and cooling (like water).

Hess's Law and Enthalpy of Formation

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Understand that the enthalpy change does not depend on the path.
  • Add, reverse and multiply equations to find a required ΔH.
  • Calculate ΔH from enthalpies of formation: products minus reactants.

Hess's law

Enthalpy is a state function: it depends only on where the reaction starts and ends, not on the route between. So the total change of several steps equals the sum of their changes.

It is like climbing to a rooftop: the total height is the same whether you take the stairs or the lift.

Three rules for handling equations

Start from the target equation, see where each substance sits, then arrange the given equations to match it.

  • Add equations: add their ΔH values, and cancel substances that appear on both sides.
  • Reverse an equation: the sign of ΔH flips, its size stays.
  • Multiply an equation by a number: multiply ΔH by the same number.

Enthalpy of formation

The standard enthalpy of formation is the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states.

The enthalpy of formation of an element in its standard state (such as OX2​, HX2​, graphite) is zero, because nothing has to be formed.

From tables of formation enthalpies we find ΔH of any reaction: add the values of the products and subtract the sum for the reactants, multiplying each by its coefficient.

Laws and rules

Hess's lawΔHtotal​=ΔH1​+ΔH2​+…All values in kJ. The direct route and the stepwise route give the same value.
ΔH from formation enthalpiesΔH=∑nΔHf​(products)−∑nΔHf​(reactants)Multiply each formation enthalpy (kJ/mol) by its moles in the equation. ΔHf​ of a standard-state element is 0.

Worked examples

ΔH for the step A to B is −30 kJ, and for B to C is −20 kJ. What is ΔH for A to C directly?
Show the solution
  1. The route A to C passes through two steps in a row.
  2. Add the changes: −30+(−20)=−50 kJ.
  3. By Hess's law the direct route has the same value.
Answer: −50 kJ.
If NX2​+OX2​​2NO has ΔH=+180 kJ and 2NO+OX2​​2NOX2​ has ΔH=−114 kJ, what is ΔH for NX2​+2OX2​​2NOX2​?
Show the solution
  1. Add the two equations: NX2​+OX2​+2NO+OX2​​2NO+2NOX2​.
  2. Cancel 2NO from both sides, leaving NX2​+2OX2​​2NOX2​, the target.
  3. Add the changes: +180+(−114)=+66 kJ.
Answer: +66 kJ (endothermic).
Find ΔH for CHX4​+2OX2​​COX2​+2HX2​O (liquid water) given ΔHf​ in kJ/mol: CHX4​ −75, COX2​ −394, HX2​O −286.
Show the solution
  1. Products: −394+2×(−286)=−394−572=−966 kJ.
  2. Reactants: −75+2×0=−75 kJ, since OX2​ is a standard element with 0.
  3. ΔH=−966−(−75)=−966+75=−891 kJ.
  4. Check: negative, as expected for burning methane.
Answer: −891 kJ (exothermic).
Common mistakes
  • Reversing an equation and forgetting to flip the sign. Reversing always flips the sign.
  • Multiplying the equation by a number but not ΔH. Whatever you do to the equation, you do to its value.
  • Subtracting the wrong way round: reactants minus products. The correct order is products minus reactants.
  • Forgetting that a standard-state element has ΔHf​=0, or forgetting to multiply by the coefficient.
Summary
  • The total change does not depend on the path: add the step changes.
  • Reversing flips the sign; multiplying by a number multiplies the value.
  • The formation enthalpy of a standard-state element is zero.
  • ΔH = formation enthalpies of products - those of reactants (each × coefficient).

Bond Energies

What you will learn
  • Understand that breaking bonds absorbs energy and forming bonds releases it.
  • Estimate ΔH from bond energies.
  • Decide whether a reaction is exothermic or endothermic from the two totals.

Bonds store energy

Bond energy is the energy needed to break one mole of a particular bond in the gas state. The stronger the bond, the more energy it takes to break.

A chemical reaction breaks the bonds of the reactants and then forms the bonds of the products.

A triple bond is stronger than a double bond, and a double bond is stronger than a single bond between the same atoms, so its energy is larger. Table values are averages, so the calculation is only an estimate.

Breaking and forming

The difference decides the reaction type: if more energy is absorbed than released the reaction is endothermic; if more is released the reaction is exothermic.

  • Breaking bonds always absorbs energy (an endothermic step).
  • Forming bonds always releases energy (an exothermic step).

How to calculate

Count every bond broken in the reactants and add their energies, then count every bond formed in the products and add their energies. ΔH = absorbed - released.

Watch the coefficients: if the equation has 2 mol of a compound, multiply its number of bonds by 2.

Laws and rules

ΔH from bond energiesΔH=∑Ebonds broken​−∑Ebonds formed​All energies in kJ. Negative = exothermic, positive = endothermic.

Worked examples

Breaking the reactants' bonds absorbs 500 kJ and forming the products' bonds releases 600 kJ. Find ΔH and the reaction type.
Show the solution
  1. Use ΔH=absorbed−released.
  2. Substitute: ΔH=500−600=−100 kJ.
  3. The sign is negative because more was released than absorbed.
Answer: ΔH=−100 kJ, exothermic.
Find ΔH for HX2​+ClX2​​2HCl given bond energies (kJ/mol): H−H 436, Cl−Cl 243, H−Cl 431.
Show the solution
  1. Broken: one H−H and one Cl−Cl: 436+243=679 kJ.
  2. Formed: two H−Cl bonds: 2×431=862 kJ.
  3. ΔH=679−862=−183 kJ.
Answer: −183 kJ (exothermic).
Find ΔH for NX2​+3HX2​​2NHX3​ given bond energies (kJ/mol): N≡N 945, H−H 436, N−H 391.
Show the solution
  1. Broken: one N≡N and 3 H−H: 945+3×436=945+1308=2253 kJ.
  2. Formed: two NHX3​ molecules with 3 N−H bonds each, so 6 bonds: 6×391=2346 kJ.
  3. ΔH=2253−2346=−93 kJ.
Answer: −93 kJ (exothermic).
Common mistakes
  • Subtracting in the wrong order. Remember: breaking comes first and absorbs, so it is the first term: absorbed - released.
  • Thinking that forming bonds absorbs energy. Bonds release energy when they form.
  • Counting only one bond in a molecule that has several, or forgetting the coefficient of the equation.
Summary
  • Breaking bonds absorbs energy; forming bonds releases energy.
  • ΔH = energy of breaking - energy of forming.
  • More released: exothermic. More absorbed: endothermic.
  • Count bonds carefully together with the coefficients.

Reaction Rate and the Rate Law

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Calculate an average rate from the change in concentration over time.
  • Write a rate law and find the order from the exponents.
  • Predict the effect of doubling a concentration and find the rate constant from data.

What is reaction rate?

Reaction rate is how much the concentration of a substance changes per unit time. Reactant concentrations fall with time and product concentrations rise.

Its unit is mol/(L⋅s). We take the size of the change so the rate is a positive number. The slope of the concentration-time curve at a given moment is the rate at that moment.

The rate is highest at the start and then drops because reactants are used up, so fewer collisions happen.

Rate and coefficients

The rate is measured for each substance separately, and the coefficients of the balanced equation link these rates. In 2A​B, A is used up twice as fast as B forms, because each mole of B needs two moles of A.

The rate law

The rate law links the rate to the concentrations of the reactants: rate = rate constant × each concentration raised to its exponent.

The exponents are found by experiment, and are not necessarily the coefficients of the balanced equation. The order with respect to a substance is its exponent, and the overall order is the sum of the exponents.

If no exponent is shown it is 1. If the exponent is 0, the rate does not depend on that substance.

Effect of changing concentration

When a concentration doubles, the rate is multiplied by 2 raised to the exponent: exponent 1 gives twice the rate, exponent 2 gives 4 times, exponent 0 gives no change.

The rate constant changes only with temperature or with a catalyst, not with the concentrations.

Laws and rules

Average raterate=Δt∣Δ[conc]∣​Concentration in mol/L, time in s; rate in mol/(L⋅s).
Rate lawrate=k[A]m[B]nk is the rate constant, a value that belongs to the reaction and the temperature. m and n are found by experiment. Overall order = m+n.

Worked examples

The concentration of a reactant fell from 0.50 to 0.30 mol/L in 10 s. What is the average rate?
Show the solution
  1. Change in concentration: 0.50−0.30=0.20 mol/L (take the size).
  2. Divide by time: rate=100.20​.
  3. Compute: 100.20​=0.02mol/(L⋅s).
Answer: 0.02mol/(L⋅s).
A rate law is rate=k[A][B]2. What is the overall order? What happens to the rate if both concentrations are doubled?
Show the solution
  1. The exponent of A is 1 (none shown) and of B is 2, so the overall order is 1+2=3.
  2. Doubling A multiplies the rate by 21=2.
  3. Doubling B multiplies the rate by 22=4.
  4. Total effect: 2×4=8 times.
Answer: Overall order 3; the rate becomes 8 times larger.
At the same temperature: when [A]=0.10 mol/L the rate was 0.002mol/(L⋅s), and when [A]=0.20 the rate was 0.008. What is the order with respect to A? What is k?
Show the solution
  1. The concentration doubled (0.100.20​=2) and the rate went up 0.0020.008​=4 times.
  2. Find the exponent m with 2m=4, so m=2: second order.
  3. Find k from experiment 1: k=[A]2rate​=0.1020.002​=0.010.002​=0.2.
  4. Check with experiment 2: 0.2×0.202=0.2×0.04=0.008. ✓
Answer: Second order, and k=0.2L/(mol⋅s).
Common mistakes
  • Taking the exponents from the coefficients of the balanced equation. Exponents come from experiment and may differ from the coefficients.
  • Always multiplying the rate by 2 when a concentration doubles. The effect is 2 to the power of the exponent: 1, 2, 4 or 8 times.
  • Forgetting to take the size of the change in a reactant concentration and getting a negative rate. Rate is always positive.
  • Thinking the rate constant changes with concentration. It changes only with temperature or a catalyst.
Summary
  • Rate = size of concentration change ÷ time, in mol/(L⋅s).
  • Rate law: constant × concentrations raised to exponents found by experiment.
  • Overall order = sum of the exponents.
  • Doubling a concentration multiplies the rate by 2 to the power of the exponent.

Collision Theory, Activation Energy and Catalysts

What you will learn
  • Explain why not every collision gives a reaction.
  • Read the activation energy and ΔH from an energy diagram.
  • Explain how a catalyst speeds up a reaction without changing its products.

Collision theory

For a reaction to happen, particles must collide. But most collisions do not give a reaction; only effective collisions do.

A collision is effective when two conditions are met: the particles together have at least the activation energy, and they meet in a suitable orientation.

The more effective collisions per second, the faster the reaction.

Activation energy

Activation energy is the minimum energy colliding particles need to react. It is like a hill that must be climbed before going down the other side.

On the diagram it is the difference between the top of the curve (the activated complex) and the reactants level. ΔH is the difference between the products level and the reactants level.

Every reaction has an activation energy, even an exothermic one. A small activation energy means a fast reaction.

Catalysts

A catalyst speeds up a reaction by giving another path with a lower activation energy, and it comes out unchanged at the end, not used up.

More particles have enough energy, so effective collisions increase. It lowers the activation energy of the forward and the reverse reactions by the same amount. But it changes neither the reactants level nor the products level, so ΔH and the amount of product stay the same.

Laws and rules

Forward activation energyEa​=Epeak​−Ereactants​Energy in kJ. For the reverse reaction subtract the products energy instead.
Change in enthalpyΔH=Eproducts​−Ereactants​Not affected by a catalyst.

Worked examples

On an energy diagram the reactants are at 50 kJ, the peak at 120 kJ and the products at 20 kJ. Find the forward activation energy and ΔH.
Show the solution
  1. Activation energy: 120−50=70 kJ.
  2. ΔH: 20−50=−30 kJ.
  3. The sign is negative, so the reaction is exothermic.
Answer: Ea​=70 kJ and ΔH=−30 kJ (exothermic).
A catalyst is added to the previous reaction and the peak drops to 90 kJ. What are the new activation energy and the new ΔH?
Show the solution
  1. New activation energy: 90−50=40 kJ (less than 70).
  2. The reactants and products levels did not change: 50 and 20.
  3. So ΔH is still 20−50=−30 kJ.
Answer: Ea​=40 kJ, and ΔH stays −30 kJ.
An endothermic reaction has a forward activation energy of 70 kJ and ΔH=+30 kJ. What is the activation energy of the reverse reaction?
Show the solution
  1. Endothermic: the products are 30 kJ above the reactants.
  2. The peak is 70 kJ above the reactants, so it is 70−30=40 kJ above the products.
  3. The reverse activation energy is the gap from the products up to the peak.
Answer: 40 kJ.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking a catalyst changes ΔH or increases the amount of product. It only speeds up the arrival; it does not change the two levels.
  • Thinking an exothermic reaction has no activation energy. Every reaction has one.
  • Measuring activation energy from reactants to products. It is from the reactants to the peak.
  • Thinking every collision gives a reaction. Only effective ones do: enough energy and a suitable orientation.
Summary
  • A reaction needs an effective collision: energy ≥ Ea​ and a suitable orientation.
  • Ea​ = peak - reactants.
  • A catalyst lowers Ea​, speeds up the reaction and is not used up.
  • A catalyst does not change ΔH or the amount of product.

Factors Affecting Rate: Surface Area, Temperature and Concentration

What you will learn
  • Explain the effect of surface area on reaction rate.
  • Explain the effect of raising temperature using collision theory.
  • Solve problems on the rule of thumb: every 10∘C doubles the rate.

Surface area

When a solid takes part in a reaction, the reaction happens only at its exposed surface. Breaking the solid up exposes more surface to collisions.

That is why wood shavings burn faster than a log, and powdered sugar dissolves faster than cubes. More surface area means more collisions per second, so a faster rate.

Temperature

Raising the temperature makes particles move faster, so there are more collisions. More importantly, a larger fraction of them have energy equal to or above the activation energy, so effective collisions increase greatly.

Rule of thumb: every rise of about 10∘C doubles the rate. Lowering the temperature slows reactions, which is why we keep food in the fridge.

Concentration and catalysts

A higher concentration puts more particles in each litre, so collisions per second increase and so does the rate.

A catalyst lowers the activation energy, which raises the fraction of effective collisions without raising the temperature.

Laws and rules

Rule of thumb for temperaturenew rate=old rate×2ΔT/10ΔT is the rise in ∘C. A rough rule: every 10∘C doubles the rate. The time to finish is inversely proportional to the rate.

Worked examples

A cube of side 1 cm is cut into 8 identical cubes of side 0.5 cm. Compare the total surface area before and after.
Show the solution
  1. A cube has 6 faces. Before: 6×12=6cm2.
  2. Surface of each small cube: 6×0.52=6×0.25=1.5cm2.
  3. Total for 8 cubes: 8×1.5=12cm2.
  4. Ratio: 612​=2.
Answer: The surface area doubled (from 6 to 12cm2), so the reaction rate increases.
A reaction rate at 20∘C is 3mol/(L⋅s). About what is it at 50∘C, using the rule of doubling per 10∘C?
Show the solution
  1. The rise: 50−20=30∘C, which is 3 steps of 10∘C.
  2. Doubling three times: 23=8.
  3. New rate: 3×8=24mol/(L⋅s).
Answer: About 24mol/(L⋅s).
A reaction takes 80 s to finish at 20∘C. How long does it take at 40∘C if every 10∘C rise doubles the rate?
Show the solution
  1. The rise: 40−20=20∘C, which is 2 steps of 10∘C.
  2. The rate doubles twice: 22=4 times.
  3. Time is inversely proportional to rate: 480​=20 s.
Answer: 20 s.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking a higher temperature speeds up a reaction only because there are more collisions. More important is that a larger fraction of collisions has enough energy.
  • Thinking temperature changes the activation energy. It increases the particles that pass it, without changing its value.
  • In temperature problems, multiplying by 2 just once whatever the rise. Count how many tens of degrees there are and use 2 to that power.
  • Mixing up time and rate: the rate doubles, so the time is halved.
Summary
  • Breaking a solid up increases surface area and so the rate.
  • Heating increases effective collisions by putting more particles above Ea​.
  • Every 10∘C roughly doubles the rate.
  • Higher concentration increases collisions per second.
