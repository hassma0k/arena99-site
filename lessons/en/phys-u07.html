Reflection and the Plane Mirror

What you will learn
  • Apply the law of reflection, measuring both angles from the normal
  • Describe a plane-mirror image and find its distance
  • Solve problems where an object moves toward its mirror

What happens in reflection?

Reflection is light bouncing off a shiny surface. At the point of incidence we draw a line at right angles to the surface, called the normal.

The angle of incidence is between the incoming ray and the normal; the angle of reflection is between the reflected ray and the normal. The law says they are equal, and that both rays and the normal lie in one plane.

  • Both angles are always measured from the normal, never from the mirror surface.
  • A ray along the normal has an angle of incidence of 0∘ and bounces straight back.

The image in a plane mirror

When you look in a plane mirror, the reflected rays never really meet; they only seem to come from a point behind the mirror. So the image is virtual: it cannot be caught on a screen.

The image is upright and the same size as the object, and it lies as far behind the mirror as the object is in front. It is also reversed left to right: your right hand looks like a left hand.

Two cases that keep coming up

If the mirror turns through some angle while the incident ray stays fixed, the reflected ray turns through twice that angle in the same direction. To see their whole image, a person needs a mirror only half their own height, with its top edge halfway between the eyes and the top of the head.

Laws and rules

Law of reflectionangle of reflection=angle of incidenceBoth angles are in degrees and measured from the normal to the surface.
Image distancedi​=do​Both are measured from the mirror surface in metres; the object is in front, the image behind.

Worked examples

A ray hits a plane mirror at 35∘ to the normal. What is its angle of reflection, and what is the angle between the incident and reflected rays?
Show the solution
  1. The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence because both are from the normal: 35∘.
  2. The two rays lie on either side of the normal, each 35∘ from it.
  3. The angle between them is 35+35=70∘.
Answer: Angle of reflection 35∘; angle between the rays 70∘.
A light ray makes 40∘ with the surface of a plane mirror. What is its angle of reflection, and the angle between the two rays?
Show the solution
  1. The given angle is from the surface, but the law needs it from the normal. The normal makes 90∘ with the surface.
  2. Angle of incidence =90−40=50∘.
  3. The angle of reflection is the same: 50∘.
  4. The angle between the rays =50+50=100∘.
Answer: Angle of reflection 50∘; angle between the rays 100∘.
A person stands 3m from a plane mirror and walks toward it at 0.5m/s for 2s. How far is the person from the image afterwards, and how fast does the gap close?
Show the solution
  1. Distance walked =0.5×2=1m, so the distance to the mirror is 3−1=2m.
  2. The image is also 2m behind the mirror.
  3. Person-to-image distance =2+2=4m.
  4. The person approaches the mirror at 0.5m/s and the image approaches it at the same speed, so the gap closes at 0.5+0.5=1m/s.
Answer: 4m, and the gap closes at 1m/s.
Common mistakes
  • Measuring the angle from the mirror surface instead of the normal. If you are given the angle to the surface, subtract it from 90∘ first.
  • Forgetting that the object-to-image distance is double the distance to the mirror, not just that distance.
  • Thinking the image is upside down. It is upright but reversed left to right.
  • Thinking the image is real. It cannot be caught on a screen, because it is virtual.
Summary
  • Angle of reflection = angle of incidence, both from the normal.
  • A plane-mirror image is virtual, upright and the same size.
  • Image distance behind the mirror = object distance in front.
  • Object-to-image distance = twice the object distance from the mirror.
  • Turning a mirror through an angle turns the reflected ray through twice that angle.

Spherical Mirrors: Concave and Convex

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Know the centre of curvature, the focus, and f=R÷2
  • State the image properties for concave and convex mirrors
  • Solve mirror-equation and magnification problems with correct signs

The spherical mirror and its focus

A spherical mirror is a piece of the surface of a sphere. The sphere’s centre is the centre of curvature and its radius R is the radius of curvature. The line through the centre of curvature and the middle of the mirror is the principal axis.

Rays parallel to the axis reflect and meet at the focus, which lies halfway between the mirror and the centre of curvature, so the focal length is f=R÷2.

The concave mirror (converging)

Its reflecting surface is on the inside of the curve. It brings parallel rays to the focus, and it has three special rays: a ray parallel to the axis reflects through the focus, a ray through the focus reflects parallel to the axis, and a ray through the centre of curvature bounces straight back.

Its image changes with the object position.

  • Beyond the centre of curvature: real, inverted, smaller.
  • At the centre of curvature: real, inverted, same size.
  • Between the centre of curvature and the focus: real, inverted, magnified.
  • At the focus: no image, the rays leave parallel.
  • Between the focus and the mirror: virtual, upright, magnified, like a shaving mirror.

The convex mirror (diverging)

Its reflecting surface is on the outside of the curve. It spreads parallel rays so they seem to come from a focus behind the mirror, so its focus is virtual and its focal length is negative.

Its image is virtual, upright and smaller wherever the object is. Because it shows a wide view, it is used for car wing mirrors.

The equation and the signs

Let do​ be the object distance, di​ the image distance and f the focal length, all measured from the mirror. A real image is in front of the mirror and has di​ positive; a virtual image is behind it and has di​ negative.

f is positive for a concave mirror and negative for a convex one. A negative magnification means an inverted image; a positive one means upright.

Laws and rules

Focal lengthf=2R​f is the focal length and R the radius of curvature, both in cm or m.
Mirror equationf1​=do​1​+di​1​do​ is the object distance, di​ the image distance, f the focal length, all in the same length unit.
Magnificationm=−do​di​​=ho​hi​​No unit; hi​ and ho​ are image and object heights. A negative sign means inverted.

Worked examples

A concave mirror has a radius of curvature of 24cm. What is its focal length, and where does a ray parallel to the axis go after reflection?
Show the solution
  1. Use f=2R​.
  2. f=224​=12cm.
  3. A ray parallel to the axis reflects through the focus, a point 12cm from the mirror.
Answer: f=12cm, and the ray reflects through the focus.
A 6cm tall object is 36cm from the concave mirror above (f=12cm). Where is the image, how tall is it and what kind is it?
Show the solution
  1. Isolate di​: di​1​=f1​−do​1​=121​−361​.
  2. Common denominator: 363​−361​=362​=181​, so di​=18cm.
  3. Magnification =−3618​=−0.5.
  4. Image height =0.5×6=3cm, and the minus sign means inverted.
Answer: di​=18cm in front of the mirror; a real, inverted, smaller image 3cm tall.
An 8cm tall object is 20cm from a convex mirror of focal length −20cm. Where is the image, how tall is it and what kind is it?
Show the solution
  1. di​1​=f1​−do​1​=−201​−201​=−202​=−101​.
  2. So di​=−10cm; negative means a virtual image behind the mirror.
  3. Magnification =−20−10​=0.5 (positive: upright).
  4. Image height =0.5×8=4cm.
Answer: di​=−10cm; a virtual, upright, smaller image 4cm tall, behind the mirror.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up the radius and the focal length: f is half of R, not equal to it.
  • Forgetting the negative sign of f for a convex mirror, which wrongly gives a real image.
  • Thinking a concave mirror always magnifies. It magnifies only when the object is inside the focus, or between the focus and the centre of curvature.
  • Thinking a negative di​ is a mistake. It means a virtual image behind the mirror.
Summary
  • Focal length f=R÷2.
  • A concave mirror converges; its image depends on object position, and is virtual and magnified when the object is inside the focus.
  • A convex mirror diverges; its image is always virtual, upright and smaller.
  • Positive di​ = real, in front; negative di​ = virtual, behind.

Refraction, Refractive Index and Snell’s Law

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Explain refraction as a change in light speed between two media
  • Find the refractive index, speed and wavelength in a medium
  • Apply Snell’s law and decide which way a ray bends

Why does light bend?

Refraction is the bending of a ray as it passes between two different transparent media, like air and water. The cause is that light travels at different speeds in different media, so the wave changes direction at the boundary if it arrives at a slant.

A ray that hits the boundary along the normal does not bend, but its speed still changes.

Refractive index

The refractive index n of a medium tells how much it slows light: it is the speed of light in vacuum divided by the speed in the medium. It is always 1 or more; air is about 1, water 1.33, glass about 1.5 and diamond 2.4.

Entering a medium of larger index lowers both the speed and the wavelength of light, but the frequency stays the same because the source sets it.

Direction of bending and Snell’s law

If a ray enters a medium of larger index (for example from air into glass) it bends toward the normal, so the angle of refraction is smaller than the angle of incidence. If it leaves into a medium of smaller index it bends away from the normal.

Snell’s law links the two angles with the indices of the media, and both angles are measured from the normal.

Laws and rules

Refractive indexn=speed of light in the mediumspeed of light in vacuum​n has no unit; the speed of light in vacuum is 3×108m/s.
Snell’s lawn1​sinθ1​=n2​sinθ2​n1​ and n2​ are the indices of the two media; θ1​ and θ2​ are the angles of incidence and refraction, measured from the normal.
Wavelength in a mediumλmedium​=nλair​​In nm or m; the frequency does not change.

Worked examples

The speed of light in a plastic is 2×108m/s. What is its refractive index, and what is the wavelength in the plastic of light that has a wavelength of 600nm in air?
Show the solution
  1. n=2×1083×108​=1.5.
  2. Wavelength in the medium =1.5600​.
  3. 600÷1.5=400nm.
Answer: Refractive index 1.5; wavelength 400nm (the frequency is unchanged).
Light goes from air (n=1) into a material of refractive index 2​ at an angle of incidence of 45∘. What is the angle of refraction?
Show the solution
  1. Snell: 1×sin45∘=2​×sinθ2​.
  2. sin45∘=22​​, so 22​​=2​×sinθ2​.
  3. Divide by 2​: sinθ2​=21​.
  4. The angle whose sine is 21​ is 30∘.
Answer: The angle of refraction is 30∘, smaller than 45∘, because the ray entered a medium of larger index and bent toward the normal.
A ray from air hits a liquid at 60∘ and refracts at 30∘. What is the liquid’s refractive index, and roughly what is the speed of light in it?
Show the solution
  1. Snell: 1×sin60∘=n×sin30∘.
  2. sin60∘=23​​ and sin30∘=21​.
  3. n=1/23​/2​=3​≈1.73.
  4. Speed =1.733×108​≈1.73×108m/s.
Answer: n≈1.73, and light travels at about 1.73×108m/s in the liquid.
Common mistakes
  • Measuring the two angles from the boundary surface instead of the normal.
  • Thinking frequency changes in the new medium. Speed and wavelength change, but frequency stays fixed.
  • Thinking light is faster in the medium with the larger index. It is the opposite: larger n means lower speed.
  • Mixing up the direction of bending: entering a larger-index medium bends the ray toward the normal; leaving it bends it away.
Summary
  • Refractive index = speed of light in vacuum ÷ speed in the medium, and it is 1 or more.
  • On crossing, the frequency stays fixed while the speed and wavelength scale by n.
  • Snell: n1​sinθ1​=n2​sinθ2​, with angles from the normal.
  • Entering a larger-n medium bends the ray toward the normal.

Total Internal Reflection and Optical Fibres

What you will learn
  • State the two conditions for total internal reflection
  • Calculate the critical angle between two media
  • Explain how an optical fibre works

When does light reflect completely?

When light goes from a medium of larger index to a smaller one (such as glass to air) it bends away from the normal. As the angle of incidence grows, the angle of refraction grows until it reaches 90∘, and the refracted ray skims along the boundary.

The angle of incidence that gives this is the critical angle. Beyond it there is no refracted ray at all, and all the light returns into the first medium: this is total internal reflection.

  • Condition 1: the light is in the medium of larger index (the optically denser one).
  • Condition 2: the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle.

The critical angle

We get it from Snell’s law by setting the angle of refraction to 90∘, whose sine is 1. If the other medium is air (n=1) then sinθc​=n1​.

The larger the refractive index, the smaller the critical angle; this is why diamond sparkles, since its index is large and light is trapped and reflected inside.

Optical fibres

An optical fibre is a thin glass thread (the core) surrounded by a cladding of lower refractive index. The light signal enters the core and hits its wall at more than the critical angle, so it reflects totally again and again and reaches the far end even when the fibre bends.

Fibres carry internet and telephone signals and are used in medical endoscopes, and very little light is lost.

Laws and rules

Critical angle between two mediasinθc​=n1​n2​​n1​ is the index of the denser medium (where the light travels), the larger one; n2​ is the index of the other; θc​ is the critical angle.
Critical angle with airsinθc​=n1​n is the refractive index of the material; air has index 1.

Worked examples

A transparent material has a refractive index of 2​. What is its critical angle with air?
Show the solution
  1. sinθc​=n1​=2​1​.
  2. 2​1​=22​​.
  3. The angle whose sine is 22​​ is 45∘.
Answer: The critical angle is 45∘.
Light inside glass of index 1.5 reaches the boundary with air. Is it totally reflected if the angle of incidence is 30∘? If it is 60∘? (Use sin30∘=0.5 and sin60∘≈0.87.)
Show the solution
  1. Sine of the critical angle: sinθc​=1.51​≈0.67.
  2. At 30∘: its sine is 0.5, less than 0.67, so the angle is below critical and the ray refracts out into the air (with a weak partial reflection).
  3. At 60∘: its sine is 0.87, more than 0.67, so the angle is above critical (about 42∘) and total internal reflection happens.
Answer: At 30∘ there is no total reflection; at 60∘ there is.
A fibre has a core of index 1.5 and a cladding of index 1.2. What is the sine of the critical angle? Is a ray that reaches the wall at 70∘ (sine about 0.94) trapped? And one at 40∘ (sine about 0.64)?
Show the solution
  1. The core is denser: sinθc​=n1​n2​​=1.51.2​=0.8 (the critical angle is about 53∘).
  2. At 70∘: sine 0.94>0.8, so the angle is above critical and the ray is totally reflected and stays in the fibre.
  3. At 40∘: sine 0.64<0.8, so the angle is below critical and some light leaks into the cladding.
Answer: The sine of the critical angle is 0.8; the 70∘ ray stays trapped and the 40∘ ray leaks out.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking total internal reflection can happen going from a less dense medium to a denser one. It happens only from the denser to the less dense.
  • Forgetting the second condition: the angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle, not just any angle.
  • Flipping the fraction for the critical angle: it is sinθc​=n1​n2​​, smaller over larger, and a sine can never exceed 1.
  • Thinking a fibre carries light by interference or diffraction. It uses total internal reflection.
Summary
  • Total internal reflection: from a denser to a less dense medium, at an angle above the critical angle.
  • The critical angle is the incidence angle that gives a 90∘ refraction.
  • sinθc​=n1​n2​​, and with air n1​.
  • Optical fibres carry signals by repeated total reflections inside the core.

Lenses: Convex and Concave

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell a converging lens from a diverging one and know each one’s image
  • Apply the lens equation and magnification with correct signs
  • Find the power of a lens in dioptres

Two kinds of lens

A lens is a transparent piece in which rays refract at both surfaces. A convex lens is thicker in the middle, so it brings parallel rays to a focus; it is called converging, and its focal length f is positive.

A concave lens is thinner in the middle, so it spreads rays so they seem to come from a virtual focus; it is called diverging, and its focal length is negative. Its image is always virtual, upright and smaller, so the equation gives a negative di​ and a magnification between 0 and 1.

The image of a convex lens

Two rays are enough to draw the image: the ray parallel to the axis refracts through the focus, and the ray through the centre of the lens goes straight on. The image tip is where they meet.

The image depends on where the object is:

  • Beyond 2f: real, inverted, smaller, between f and 2f.
  • At 2f: real, inverted, same size, at 2f.
  • Between f and 2f: real, inverted, magnified, beyond 2f.
  • At f: the rays leave parallel, so no image.
  • Inside f: virtual, upright, magnified, as in a hand magnifier.

The equation, magnification and power

The lens equation looks like the mirror equation. do​ is the object distance and di​ the image distance (positive for a real image beyond the lens, negative for a virtual image on the object’s side), and f is the focal length.

The power of a lens measures how strongly it converges or diverges: it is the reciprocal of the focal length in metres, measured in dioptres. Power is positive for convex and negative for concave. When two lenses touch, their powers add.

Laws and rules

Lens equationf1​=do​1​+di​1​f is the focal length, do​ the object distance and di​ the image distance, in one length unit (cm or m).
Magnificationm=−do​di​​=ho​hi​​No unit; negative means inverted, positive means upright.
Lens powerP=f1​P is in dioptres and f in metres. For example f=0.5m gives 2 dioptres.
Two lenses in contactP=P1​+P2​In dioptres, keeping the sign of each lens.

Worked examples

A convex lens has a focal length of 25,cm. What is its power? A concave lens of focal length −50,cm is then stuck to it; what are the power and focal length of the pair?
Show the solution
  1. Convert to metres: f=0.25,m, so power =0.251​=4 dioptres.
  2. Power of the concave lens =−0.51​=−2 dioptres.
  3. Total power =4+(−2)=2 dioptres.
  4. Focal length of the pair =21​=0.5,m=50,cm, and it is converging because the power is positive.
Answer: The convex lens has a power of 4 dioptres; the pair has a power of 2 dioptres and a focal length of 50,cm.
A 3cm tall object is 30cm from a convex lens of focal length 20cm. Where is the image, how tall is it and what kind is it?
Show the solution
  1. di​1​=f1​−do​1​=201​−301​.
  2. Common denominator: 603​−602​=601​, so di​=60cm.
  3. Magnification =−3060​=−2.
  4. Image height =2×3=6cm, inverted because the magnification is negative.
Answer: di​=60cm behind the lens; a real, inverted, magnified image 6cm tall.
A convex lens of focal length 10cm is used as a magnifier. A 3cm tall object is 5cm from it. Where is the image and how tall is it?
Show the solution
  1. The object is inside the focus (5<10), so expect a virtual image.
  2. di​1​=101​−51​=101​−102​=−101​, so di​=−10cm.
  3. Magnification =−5−10​=2 (positive: upright).
  4. Image height =2×3=6cm.
Answer: di​=−10cm on the object’s side; a virtual, upright, magnified image 6cm tall.
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting to convert the focal length from cm to m when finding power in dioptres.
  • Thinking a concave lens can give a real image. Its image is always virtual, smaller and upright.
  • Reading a negative di​ as a mistake. It is a virtual image on the object’s side.
  • Mixing up 2f and f when describing the image. An object at 2f gives a same-size image, also at 2f.
Summary
  • A convex lens converges and has positive power; a concave lens diverges and has negative power.
  • A virtual, magnified image appears when the object is between a convex lens and its focus.
  • f1​=do​1​+di​1​ and magnification =−do​di​​.
  • Power =f1​ with f in metres, and powers of touching lenses add.

The Wave Nature and Properties of Light

What you will learn
  • Decide constructive or destructive interference from the path difference
  • Find the fringe spacing in the double-slit experiment
  • Know diffraction, polarization, illuminance and adding colours of light

Interference and the double slit

When two coherent waves (from the same source) meet, they interfere. If their path difference is a whole number of wavelengths, crest meets crest: constructive interference and a bright fringe. If it is a half-odd number, crest meets trough: destructive interference and a dark fringe.

In the double-slit experiment, single-colour light passes through two close slits and the screen shows a series of bright and dark fringes, roughly evenly spaced. Seeing them proves that light is a wave.

  • Path difference =0,λ,2λ,…: bright fringe.
  • Path difference =0.5λ,1.5λ,2.5λ,…: dark fringe.

Diffraction and polarization

Diffraction is the bending and spreading of a wave around an obstacle or through an opening. It is clear when the opening is about as wide as the wavelength, and weak when the opening is much larger.

Polarizing light means making it vibrate in one direction only, by passing it through a polarizing filter. Only transverse waves can be polarized, so it shows that light is a transverse wave, unlike sound, which is longitudinal. Ordinary light vibrates in many directions, so a filter passes just one and the intensity drops to about half; two filters with perpendicular axes let no light through.

Illuminance and adding colours

Illuminance is the amount of light falling on a unit area of a surface, measured in lux. It is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from the source: doubling the distance cuts it to a quarter.

The primary colours of light are red, green and blue, and lights add: red with green gives yellow, red with blue gives magenta, green with blue gives cyan, and all three give white. This differs from mixing paints.

Laws and rules

Constructive interferencepath difference=m×λm=0,1,2,… and λ is the wavelength, in the same length unit.
Destructive interferencepath difference=(m+0.5)×λm=0,1,2,… and λ is the wavelength.
Fringe spacingΔy=dλL​λ is the wavelength, L the distance from slits to screen, d the slit separation; all in metres.
IlluminanceE=r2I​E is illuminance in lux, I the source intensity, r the distance from the source in metres.

Worked examples

Two light waves with wavelength 450nm meet. What kind of interference occurs if the path difference is 900nm? If it is 675nm?
Show the solution
  1. Divide the path difference by the wavelength.
  2. 900÷450=2: a whole number, so constructive (bright).
  3. 675÷450=1.5: a half-odd number, so destructive (dark).
Answer: At 900nm it is constructive; at 675nm it is destructive.
In a double-slit experiment the wavelength is 5×10−7m, the slit separation is 5×10−4m and the screen is 2m away. What is the spacing between neighbouring bright fringes?
Show the solution
  1. Write the relation: Δy=dλL​.
  2. Substitute: 5×10−45×10−7×2​.
  3. Numerator =10×10−7=10−6, so the result is 5×10−410−6​=0.2×10−2=2×10−3m.
Answer: The fringe spacing is 2×10−3m, that is 2mm.
A lamp gives an illuminance of 90 lux on a surface 2m away. What is the illuminance if the surface is moved to 6m?
Show the solution
  1. The distance becomes 3 times as large: 6÷2=3.
  2. Illuminance is inversely proportional to distance squared, so it falls to 321​=91​.
  3. 90÷9=10 lux.
Answer: The illuminance is 10 lux.
Common mistakes
  • Confusing path difference with wavelength. Divide the path difference by the wavelength first, then see whether the result is a whole or half-odd number.
  • Dividing illuminance by the distance only. The distance ratio must be squared: three times as far means dividing by 9.
  • Treating adding lights like mixing paints. Red light with green light gives yellow, not brown.
  • Thinking diffraction happens with very large openings. It is clear when the opening is about the same as the wavelength.
Summary
  • A path difference of a whole number of λ gives constructive interference; a half-odd number gives destructive.
  • The double slit gives alternating fringes, spaced dλL​ apart.
  • Diffraction is clear when the opening is about the wavelength; polarization shows light is a transverse wave.
  • Illuminance falls with the square of distance, and red plus green light gives yellow.

The Eye, Nearsightedness and the Compound Microscope

What you will learn
  • Explain nearsightedness and where the image forms
  • Choose the correcting lens and find its power
  • Find the total magnification of a compound microscope

How the eye sees

The cornea and lens of the eye act together as a convex lens that makes a real, inverted image on the retina at the back of the eye, and the brain interprets it. Vision is sharp when the image falls exactly on the retina.

Nearsightedness and its correction

A nearsighted person sees near objects clearly and distant ones blurred. The eyeball is too long, or its lens is too strong, so the image of a distant object forms in front of the retina.

It is corrected by a concave (diverging) lens placed in front of the eye, which spreads the rays slightly before they enter so that their focus moves back onto the retina. Farsightedness is corrected by a convex lens.

The lens is chosen so that very distant objects form a virtual image at the farthest point the person sees clearly (the far point). So its focal length is negative and equal in size to the distance of that point.

The compound microscope

A compound microscope has two convex lenses: the objective, with a short focal length, forms a real, inverted, magnified image of the specimen; the eyepiece works as a magnifier on that image and gives a final virtual, magnified image.

The total magnification is the product of the two magnifications, because the objective’s image is the eyepiece’s object.

Laws and rules

Power of the correcting lensP=f1​P is in dioptres and f in metres; a concave lens has negative f, so negative power.
Total magnification of the microscopemtotal​=mobjective​×meyepiece​Each magnification has no unit.

Worked examples

The farthest point a nearsighted person sees clearly is 50cm from the eye. What is the power of the lens that corrects the vision?
Show the solution
  1. The lens forms a virtual image of distant objects at the far point, so the focal length is negative: f=−50cm=−0.5m.
  2. Power =−0.51​=−2 dioptres.
  3. The negative sign confirms a concave lens.
Answer: A concave lens of power −2 dioptres.
The objective of a compound microscope magnifies 10 times and the eyepiece 15 times. What is the total magnification? What does it become if the objective is swapped for one that magnifies 40 times?
Show the solution
  1. Total magnification =10×15=150.
  2. After the swap: 40×15=600.
  3. We multiply, we do not add.
Answer: The total magnification is 150, and 600 after the swap.
A doctor prescribes glasses of power −4 dioptres for a nearsighted person. What is the farthest point the person sees clearly without glasses? What type of lens is it?
Show the solution
  1. Focal length f=P1​=−41​=−0.25m.
  2. The size 0.25m=25cm is the distance of the far point.
  3. The power is negative, so the lens is concave (diverging).
Answer: The far point is 25cm, and the lens is concave.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the image of a nearsighted eye forms behind the retina. It forms in front; behind is farsightedness.
  • Correcting nearsightedness with a convex lens. A convex lens adds more converging and makes it worse; a concave one is needed.
  • Adding the objective and eyepiece magnifications instead of multiplying.
  • Thinking a nearsighted person cannot see near objects. Near objects are clear; distant ones are the problem.
Summary
  • Nearsightedness: the image forms in front of the retina; correct it with a concave, diverging lens.
  • Farsightedness: the image forms behind the retina; correct it with a convex lens.
  • Power of the correcting lens =f1​, negative for nearsightedness.
  • Total magnification of a compound microscope = objective × eyepiece.
