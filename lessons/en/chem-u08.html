Theories of acids and bases

What you will learn
  • Define acids and bases by Arrhenius, Brønsted–Lowry and Lewis
  • Say who gives and who receives in each theory
  • See why each theory covers more substances than the last

Arrhenius: ions in water

According to Arrhenius, an acid is a substance that raises the concentration of hydrogen ions HX+ in water, and a base raises the concentration of hydroxide ions OHX−.

Example: HCl is an acid because it ionizes to give HX+; NaOH is a base because it dissociates to give OHX−. NaCl and CHX4​ give neither, so they are neither acid nor base.

  • Acid: HCl​HX++ClX−
  • Base: NaOH​NaX++OHX−
  • Limit: only for water solutions, and it cannot explain ammonia, which has no hydroxide ion in its formula.

Brønsted–Lowry: proton transfer

Here the proton is the ion HX+. An acid is a proton donor and a base is a proton acceptor.

Ammonia in water: NHX3​+HX2​O​NHX4​X++OHX−. NHX3​ accepts a proton from water, so it is a base, while water donates one, so it is an acid. Water can be an acid or a base depending on its partner.

In water HX+ does not stay free: it attaches to a water molecule to form the hydronium ion HX3​OX+, so we write HX+ or HX3​OX+ for the same idea. The Brønsted–Lowry theory is not limited to water solutions.

Lewis: the electron pair

A Lewis acid accepts an electron pair; a Lewis base donates an electron pair to form a coordinate covalent bond.

BFX3​ is a Lewis acid: boron lacks a pair to complete its octet. NHX3​ is a Lewis base because nitrogen has a lone pair to donate.

How to remember

Each theory is wider than the previous one: every Arrhenius acid or base is also one for Brønsted–Lowry, and every Brønsted–Lowry acid or base is one for Lewis, but not the other way round.

Laws and rules

ArrheniusHA​HX++AX−An acid gives HX+ in water; a base gives OHX−.
Brønsted–LowryHA+B​AX−+BHX+The acid HA donates the proton; the base B accepts it.

Worked examples

Which is an Arrhenius base: KOH, HNOX3​ or CHX4​?
Show the solution
  1. An Arrhenius base gives OHX− in water.
  2. KOH​KX++OHX− gives hydroxide ions.
  3. HNOX3​ gives HX+ so it is an acid, and CHX4​ does not ionize.
Answer: KOH
In HCl+HX2​O​HX3​OX++ClX− identify the acid and the base by Brønsted–Lowry.
Show the solution
  1. HCl lost HX+ and became ClX−, so it donates a proton.
  2. HX2​O took the proton and became HX3​OX+, so it accepts a proton.
Answer: Acid: HCl; base: HX2​O.
Why is BFX3​ a Lewis acid although it has no hydrogen?
Show the solution
  1. A Lewis acid is defined by accepting an electron pair, not by containing HX+.
  2. Boron in BFX3​ has only three bonding pairs, six electrons, so there is room for a fourth pair.
  3. NHX3​ donates its lone pair and forms a coordinate bond.
Answer: Because it accepts an electron pair.
Common mistakes
  • Expecting the acid to give, as in the two earlier theories. In fact a Lewis acid accepts the pair and the base donates it.
  • Saying water is always an acid. Water donates a proton to ammonia (acid) but accepts one from HCl (base).
  • Demanding OHX− in every base. Ammonia is a Brønsted–Lowry base without containing OHX−.
Summary
  • Arrhenius: acid gives HX+, base gives OHX− in water.
  • Brønsted–Lowry: acid donates a proton, base accepts it.
  • Lewis: acid accepts an electron pair, base donates it.
  • Each theory is wider than the one before.

Conjugate acid–base pairs

What you will learn
  • Write the conjugate acid and base of any species
  • Find the conjugate pairs in a reaction
  • Understand that a species can be both acid and base

A pair that differs by one proton

When an acid gives away a proton, what is left can take a proton back; it is the conjugate base. When a base accepts a proton it becomes its conjugate acid. A conjugate pair is two species that differ by exactly one HX+.

HCl and ClX− are a pair; NHX4​X+ and NHX3​ are a pair.

How to find the partner

Conjugate base: remove one HX+ from the acid, so one hydrogen is lost and the charge goes down by one. Conjugate acid: add one HX+ to the base, so one hydrogen is gained and the charge goes up by one.

  • HF is an acid; its conjugate base is FX−.
  • HX2​O is an acid with conjugate base OHX−, and a base with conjugate acid HX3​OX+.
  • HX2​POX4​X− has conjugate base HPOX4​X2− and conjugate acid HX3​POX4​.

One species, acid and base

Some species can both donate and accept a proton; they are called amphoteric (amphiprotic). Example: HCOX3​X− donates a proton to become COX3​X2−, and accepts one to become HX2​COX3​. Water does the same. Its role depends on the partner it meets.

Two pairs in a reaction

Every acid–base reaction contains two conjugate pairs: acid1 with its conjugate base, and base2 with its conjugate acid. In HCl+HX2​O​HX3​OX++ClX− the pairs are HCl/ClX− and HX3​OX+/HX2​O.

Note the opposite trend: the stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base. The conjugate base ClX− is very weak because HCl is a strong acid.

Laws and rules

Conjugate pairHA​HX++AX−HA is the acid and AX− its conjugate base; they differ by one HX+.

Worked examples

What is the conjugate base of HCl in its reaction with water?
Show the solution
  1. Remove one HX+ from HCl.
  2. ClX− is left.
Answer: ClX−
What is the conjugate acid of ammonia NHX3​?
Show the solution
  1. Ammonia accepts a proton, so add HX+.
  2. NHX3​+HX+​NHX4​X+.
Answer: NHX4​X+
Identify the conjugate pairs in HF+HX2​O​FX−+HX3​OX+.
Show the solution
  1. HF lost HX+ and became FX−: the first pair is HF/FX−.
  2. HX2​O gained HX+ and became HX3​OX+: the second pair is HX2​O/HX3​OX+.
Answer: HF/FX− and HX2​O/HX3​OX+.
Common mistakes
  • Pairing species that differ by more than one proton, such as HX2​SOX4​ and SOX4​X2−. They differ by two protons; the conjugate pair is HX2​SOX4​/HSOX4​X−.
  • Treating HX3​OX+ and OHX− as a conjugate pair. They differ by two protons; the partner of HX3​OX+ is HX2​O.
  • Thinking NaOH and NaX+ are a pair. A conjugate pair exchanges a proton, not sodium.
Summary
  • A conjugate pair is two species that differ by one proton.
  • An acid that gave away its proton becomes a conjugate base.
  • Every acid–base reaction has two conjugate pairs.
  • The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.

Acid strength and the ionization constant

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell strong from weak acids by degree of ionization
  • Memorise the common strong acids
  • Compare acid strengths using the ionization constant
  • Estimate the pH of a weak monoprotic acid

Strength is ionization, not concentration

A strong acid ionizes completely in water, so every molecule gives up its H+. A weak acid ionizes only partly: most of it stays as molecules in equilibrium with a few ions.

Strength is not concentration: a weak acid can be concentrated and a strong acid dilute. Strength is the fraction that ionizes; concentration is how much acid is dissolved.

Strong acids to memorise

The common strong acids are few: HCl, HBr, HI, HNOX3​, HX2​SOX4​ and HClOX4​. CHX3​COOH, HF and HX2​COX3​ are weak. For HX2​SOX4​ only the first ionization is complete.

  • Strong: HCl​HX++ClX− (one arrow).
  • Weak: CHX3​COOH​HX++CHX3​COOX− (equilibrium arrows).

The ionization constant

A weak acid HA has the equilibrium HA​HX++AX− with an acid ionization constant. The larger the constant, the more ionization and the stronger the acid. Example: an acid with constant 10−3 is stronger than one with 10−5 because 10−3 is the larger number.

For equal concentrations, the stronger acid has more HX+ and therefore a lower pH.

pH of a weak acid

Assume so little ionizes that the acid concentration stays about the same. Since HX+ and AX− form in equal amounts: [HX+]2= constant × concentration.

Take the square root and then find pH. The shortcut works for a weak, fairly concentrated acid.

A weak base works the same way: [OHX−]2 = base ionization constant × concentration; then find pOH and pH.

Laws and rules

Ionization constantKa​=[HA][HX+][AX−]​Concentrations in mol/L at equilibrium; a larger Ka​ means a stronger acid.
Weak acid shortcut[HX+]=Ka​×C​C is the acid concentration in mol/L; valid for a weak monoprotic acid.

Worked examples

Two solutions have the same concentration: HCl and CHX3​COOH. Which has the lower pH?
Show the solution
  1. HCl is strong and ionizes completely, giving the largest [HX+].
  2. CHX3​COOH is weak and ionizes partly, so its [HX+] is smaller.
  3. The more H+, the lower the pH.
Answer: The HCl solution.
Which constant belongs to the strongest acid: 10−3, 10−5, 10−8 or 10−10?
Show the solution
  1. Acid strength rises with the constant.
  2. Compare exponents: −3 is larger than −5, −8 and −10.
Answer: 10−3
A weak monoprotic acid has concentration 0.1mol/L and Ka​=10−5. Find the pH.
Show the solution
  1. [HX+]2=10−5×0.1=10−5×10−1=10−6.
  2. [HX+]=10−3mol/L.
  3. pH=−log10−3=3.
  4. Check: the ionized 10−3 is tiny compared with 0.1, so the shortcut is fine.
Answer: pH=3
Common mistakes
  • Mixing strength with concentration: concentrated acetic acid is still weak, and dilute hydrochloric acid is still strong.
  • Thinking a strong acid has a strong conjugate base. The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.
  • Thinking the smaller constant is stronger when comparing 10−3 with 10−10. The larger is stronger, and 10−3 is larger.
  • Treating a weak acid like a strong one and setting [HX+] equal to its concentration, or taking the square root without first multiplying the constant by the concentration.
Summary
  • A strong acid ionizes completely, a weak one partly.
  • Common strong acids: HCl, HNOX3​, HX2​SOX4​, HBr, HI, HClOX4​.
  • A larger ionization constant means a stronger acid.
  • For a weak acid: [HX+]=Ka​×C​.

The pH scale and calculating pH

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Read the pH scale and call a solution acidic, neutral or basic
  • Calculate pH and pOH from concentration
  • Convert between pH and ion concentration
  • Predict how dilution changes pH

What is pH?

pH expresses acidity as a small number instead of a tiny concentration. At 25∘C: pH below 7 is acidic, pH equal to 7 is neutral, and pH above 7 is basic.

The scale is logarithmic: one unit lower in pH means [HX+] is 10 times larger. If pH falls from 5 to 3, [HX+] rises 10×10=100 times.

Calculating pH and pOH

pH comes from [HX+] and pOH from [OHX−]. When the concentration is 10−x the value is simply x. A concentration of 10−4mol/L gives pH 4.

Pure water at 25∘C has [HX+]=[OHX−]=10−7 and their product is 10−14 at this temperature (the ionization constant of water). Hence pH + pOH = 14.

The calculation also runs backwards: [HX+]=10−pH. A solution of pH 3 has [HX+]=10−3mol/L, its pOH is 11, so [OHX−]=10−11mol/L.

Strong acids and bases

For a strong monoprotic acid [HX+] equals its concentration. Example: 0.01mol/L HCl has pH 2.

For a strong base find [OHX−] first, then pOH, then pH. Count the hydroxide ions: Ba(OH)X2​ gives two per formula unit, so [OHX−] is twice its concentration.

Dilution

Dilution changes concentration, not moles: moles before equal moles after. So C1​V1​=C2​V2​.

Diluting a strong acid 100 times cuts [HX+] to one hundredth, so pH rises by 2. Dilution moves pH toward 7 but never beyond it: diluting a strong base lowers pH toward 7 from above.

Laws and rules

pHpH=−log[HX+]Concentration in mol/L.
pOHpOH=−log[OHX−]Concentration in mol/L.
Relation (25 °C)pH+pOH=14Also [HX+][OHX−]=10−14.
Dilution lawC1​V1​=C2​V2​C is concentration in mol/L, V is volume in mL or L (same unit on both sides).

Worked examples

A solution has [OHX−]=10−2mol/L. Find the pH.
Show the solution
  1. pOH=−log10−2=2.
  2. pH=14−2=12.
Answer: pH=12 (basic).
A 0.005mol/L solution of Ba(OH)X2​. Find the pH.
Show the solution
  1. Each unit gives two ions: [OHX−]=2×0.005=0.01=10−2mol/L.
  2. pOH=2.
  3. pH=14−2=12.
  4. Forgetting the factor of two would use 0.005 and give a wrong value near 11.7.
Answer: pH=12
10mL of 0.1mol/L HCl is diluted to 100mL. Find the new pH.
Show the solution
  1. 0.1×10=C2​×100.
  2. C2​=1001​=0.01=10−2mol/L.
  3. HCl is strong, so [HX+]=10−2.
  4. pH=2. Check: pH was 1 before; volume grew 10 times, so pH rose by one unit.
Answer: pH=2
Common mistakes
  • Saying pH equals [HX+]. It equals the negative logarithm, so 10−4 gives 4, not −4.
  • Forgetting to double the concentration of Ba(OH)X2​. The number of hydroxide ions sets [OHX−].
  • Using [OHX−] directly as if it gave pH. Find pOH first and subtract it from 14.
  • Thinking two pH units means twice the concentration. It means 100 times.
Summary
  • pH=−log[HX+]; a concentration 10−x gives pH x.
  • pH + pOH = 14 at 25 °C.
  • One pH unit is a factor of 10 in concentration.
  • Dilution follows C1​V1​=C2​V2​.

Indicators, neutralization and titration

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Know common indicator colours in acid and base
  • Write a neutralization reaction
  • Calculate an unknown concentration or volume from a titration
  • Know the pH at the equivalence point

Indicators

An indicator is a substance that changes colour with the pH of the solution. Each one changes within its own pH range.

  • Litmus: red in acid, blue in base, so blue litmus paper turns red in an acidic solution.
  • Phenolphthalein: colourless in acid and neutral, pink in base (above pH about 8), so a solution of pH 12 is pink.
  • Methyl orange: red below pH about 3, yellow above pH about 4.5 (so yellow in neutral and basic solutions), orange in between.
  • Bromothymol blue: yellow in acid, green when neutral, blue in base.

Neutralization

In neutralization an acid reacts with a base to give a salt and water. The reason is that HX+ from the acid combines with OHX− from the base: HX++OHX−​HX2​O.

Example: HCl+NaOH​NaCl+HX2​O. The balanced equation gives the mole ratio: HX2​SOX4​+2NaOH​NaX2​SOX4​+2HX2​O means one mole of acid needs two moles of base.

Titration

A titration finds the concentration of an unknown acid (or base) by adding a solution of known concentration gradually until neutralization is complete; an indicator shows the end. A known volume of the unknown goes in a flask, and the solution of known concentration is added drop by drop from a burette. This point is the equivalence point.

At equivalence the moles of OHX− added equal the moles of HX+. For monoprotic acid and base, C1​V1​=C2​V2​. If the ratio differs, find the moles and apply the ratio of the balanced equation.

For a strong acid titrated with a strong base the pH at equivalence is 7 because the product is a neutral salt. A weak acid with a strong base gives pH above 7 at equivalence because the salt is basic (phenolphthalein suits it); a strong acid with a weak base gives pH below 7 because the salt is acidic.

Laws and rules

NeutralizationHX++OHX−​HX2​OThe products are a salt and water.
Titration (monoprotic acid and base)Ca​Va​=Cb​Vb​C is concentration in mol/L, V is volume in mL; a for acid, b for base.

Worked examples

How many mL of 0.1mol/L NaOH neutralize 10mL of 0.2mol/L HCl?
Show the solution
  1. Moles of acid: 0.2×10=2 mmol.
  2. The ratio is 1:1, so the base also needs 2 mmol.
  3. Volume = 0.12​=20mL.
Answer: 20mL
25mL of HCl of unknown concentration needs 50mL of 0.1mol/L NaOH. Find the acid concentration.
Show the solution
  1. Moles of base: 0.1×50=5 mmol.
  2. The ratio is 1:1, so the acid has 5 mmol.
  3. Concentration = 255​=0.2mol/L.
Answer: 0.2mol/L
How many moles of NaOH neutralize 0.1 mol of HX2​SOX4​?
Show the solution
  1. Equation: HX2​SOX4​+2NaOH​NaX2​SOX4​+2HX2​O.
  2. The ratio is 1:2.
  3. Moles = 2×0.1=0.2 mol.
Answer: 0.2 mol
Common mistakes
  • Using C1​V1​=C2​V2​ for HX2​SOX4​ or Ba(OH)X2​ without the ratio. Use the mole ratio from the balanced equation.
  • Believing the pH at equivalence is always 7. It is 7 only for a strong acid with a strong base.
  • Mixing up phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, pink in base, not the other way round.
  • Mixing units: mL times mol/L gives mmol; divide by 1000 to get moles.
Summary
  • An indicator changes colour with pH: litmus is red in acid, phenolphthalein is pink in base.
  • Neutralization: acid + base gives a salt and water.
  • At equivalence: moles of HX+ = moles of OHX−.
  • The equivalence pH of a strong acid with a strong base is 7.

Buffer solutions and the pH of salts

What you will learn
  • Know what a buffer contains and how it resists a pH change
  • Predict whether a salt solution is acidic, basic or neutral

The buffer solution

A buffer resists a change in pH when a little acid or base is added. It contains a weak acid together with its salt (its conjugate base), such as CHX3​COOH with CHX3​COONa, or a weak base with its salt.

HNOX3​ with NaNOX3​ is not a buffer because the acid is strong, and neither is CHX3​COOH with HCl because there is no conjugate base.

How the buffering works

When acid is added, the extra HX+ reacts with the conjugate base: CHX3​COOX−+HX+​CHX3​COOH. When base is added, OHX− reacts with the weak acid: CHX3​COOH+OHX−​CHX3​COOX−+HX2​O.

In both cases the added substance is used up and turned into a component with little effect, so pH hardly changes. A buffer has a limited capacity: once the added amount exceeds one of its components, the buffering ends. Blood has such a buffer, made of HX2​COX3​ and HCOX3​X−.

pH of salt solutions

A salt comes from an acid and a base, and its solution follows the stronger parent. The ion from the weak parent reacts with water and shifts pH; the ion from the strong parent does nothing.

  • Strong acid and strong base, like NaCl: neutral.
  • Strong acid and weak base, like NHX4​Cl: acidic, because NHX4​X+ donates a proton to water.
  • Weak acid and strong base, like CHX3​COONa: basic, because CHX3​COOX− takes a proton from water.
  • Weak acid and weak base: it depends on which is stronger (the larger ionization constant).

Laws and rules

Ammonium ionNHX4​X++HX2​O​NHX3​+HX3​OX+It gives a proton to water, so the solution is acidic.
Acetate ionCHX3​COOX−+HX2​O​CHX3​COOH+OHX−It takes a proton from water, so the solution is basic.

Worked examples

What is the nature of an NHX4​Cl solution?
Show the solution
  1. It comes from HCl (strong acid) and NHX3​ (weak base).
  2. ClX− comes from the strong parent and does not react.
  3. NHX4​X+ is the conjugate acid of a weak base, so it gives a proton to water and makes HX3​OX+.
Answer: Acidic
Which pair makes a buffer: HNOX3​ with NaNOX3​, or CHX3​COOH with CHX3​COONa?
Show the solution
  1. We need a weak acid with its conjugate base.
  2. HNOX3​ is a strong acid, so the first pair fails.
  3. CHX3​COOH is weak and CHX3​COONa supplies acetate ions, so the second works.
Answer: CHX3​COOH with CHX3​COONa
What happens in the CHX3​COOH/CHX3​COOX− buffer when a little HCl is added?
Show the solution
  1. HCl adds HX+.
  2. Acetate reacts with it: CHX3​COOX−+HX+​CHX3​COOH.
  3. A strong acid is turned into a weak one, so pH changes only slightly.
Answer: CHX3​COOX− uses up the added HX+.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking every salt is neutral. A salt from a weak acid or weak base can be acidic or basic.
  • Calling any acid–base mixture a buffer. It needs a weak species and its conjugate partner in similar amounts.
  • Judging the salt by its strong part. The ion from the weak parent is the one that reacts with water and sets the nature.
Summary
  • A buffer is a weak acid with its salt (or a weak base with its salt).
  • It resists pH change because the added acid or base is used up.
  • Strong + strong is neutral; strong acid + weak base is acidic; weak acid + strong base is basic.
