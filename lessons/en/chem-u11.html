Matter, States and Changes of State

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Define matter and tell it apart from energy
  • Describe the three states by how their particles behave
  • Name the changes of state and say whether each absorbs or releases energy
  • Explain why temperature stays constant during melting and boiling

What is matter?

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space. Stone, water and air are all matter, even though we cannot see or feel air.

Light, heat and sound are forms of energy: they cannot be weighed and do not fill a container, so they are not matter. Energy is neither created nor destroyed; it moves between objects and changes form, and it is what drives changes in matter.

  • Matter: air, water, iron, smoke.
  • Not matter: light, heat, sound.

The three states of matter

All matter is made of tiny particles in constant motion. Shape and volume depend on how close the particles are and how freely they move. (A fourth state, plasma, is a very hot gas of charged particles, as in the Sun and lightning.)

  • Solid: particles packed tightly, vibrating in place; fixed shape and fixed volume.
  • Liquid: particles close but sliding past each other; fixed volume, takes the shape of its container.
  • Gas: particles far apart and fast; no fixed shape or volume, fills its container and can be compressed.

Changes of state

When matter gains or loses energy its state may change while its identity stays the same: water is still water as ice or as steam.

  • Melting: solid to liquid (absorbs energy).
  • Freezing: liquid to solid (releases energy).
  • Evaporation and boiling: liquid to gas (absorbs energy).
  • Condensation: gas to liquid (releases energy).
  • Sublimation: solid straight to gas (absorbs energy), as dry ice does.
  • Deposition: gas straight to solid (releases energy).

Why does temperature stay constant during a change?

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles (how fast they move). During melting or boiling, all the incoming energy is used to pull particles apart, not to speed them up, so the temperature stays constant until the change is finished.

Then it rises again. On a heating curve this appears as two flat parts: at 0∘C (ice melting) and at 100∘C (water boiling).

Worked examples

Classify each as matter or not matter: air, light, water, sound.
Show the solution
  1. For each one ask: does it have mass? Does it take up space?
  2. Air is a gas: an inflated balloon is heavier than an empty one and takes up room, so it is matter.
  3. Water has mass and volume: matter.
  4. Light and sound are energy that cannot be weighed and do not fill a container: not matter.
Answer: Matter: air and water. Not matter: light and sound.
Name the change and say whether energy is absorbed or released: (a) a piece of dry ice turns into gas without becoming liquid. (b) Water vapor turns into droplets on a cold glass.
Show the solution
  1. Find the state before and after the change in each case.
  2. (a) Solid to gas directly: sublimation. The solid needs energy to change, so it absorbs energy.
  3. (b) Gas to liquid: condensation. The vapor particles lose energy as they move closer, so energy is released.
  4. Quick rule: a change toward a state where particles are farther apart (melting, evaporation, sublimation) absorbs energy; the reverse releases it.
Answer: (a) Sublimation, absorbs energy. (b) Condensation, releases energy.
Ice at −10∘C is heated steadily, as in the lesson's curve. Describe the temperature at each stage and explain why it does not rise during melting.
Show the solution
  1. From −10 to 0∘C: the ice absorbs energy and its temperature rises because the particles vibrate faster.
  2. At 0∘C melting starts and the temperature stays at 0∘C (flat part) because the energy loosens the ice particles.
  3. Once all the ice has melted, the liquid water warms from 0 to 100∘C.
  4. At 100∘C boiling occurs (second flat part): the energy pulls the particles apart into vapor.
  5. A flat line does not mean the heating stopped; the energy is changing the state instead of raising the temperature.
Answer: It rises, stays at 0∘C, rises again, then stays at 100∘C; during a change of state the energy changes the state.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking air is not matter because it cannot be seen. Correct: matter is defined by mass and space, not by sight or touch.
  • Counting light or heat as matter because they "exist". Correct: they are energy with no mass.
  • Believing temperature always rises when you heat something. Correct: it stays constant during melting and boiling.
  • Mixing up evaporation and boiling: evaporation happens at the surface at any temperature; boiling happens throughout the liquid at the boiling point.
Summary
  • Matter = mass + space; light, heat and sound are energy.
  • Solids, liquids and gases differ in how close and how free their particles are.
  • Melting, evaporation and sublimation absorb energy; freezing, condensation and deposition release it.
  • Temperature stays constant during a change of state because the energy loosens particles instead of speeding them up.

Chemistry and the Scientific Method

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell the five branches of chemistry apart
  • Order the steps of the scientific method and define a hypothesis
  • Identify independent, dependent and controlled variables
  • Spot the flaw in an experiment design

Branches of chemistry

Chemistry studies matter, its composition, properties and changes. It is divided into five branches, and in a question you look for the key word (carbon, living thing, components and amount, energy):

  • Organic: carbon compounds such as plastics, fuels and medicines.
  • Inorganic: substances not based on carbon, such as metals and salts.
  • Biochemistry: chemical reactions inside living organisms, such as digestion.
  • Analytical: finding what a sample contains and how much of each part.
  • Physical: energy changes and reaction rates.

The scientific method

The scientific method is an organized way to solve problems: observe, ask a question, form a hypothesis, test it with an experiment, analyze the data and conclude. Observations are of two kinds: qualitative (descriptive), such as “the solution is blue”, and quantitative (with numbers), such as “its mass is 5g”.

A hypothesis is a proposed explanation that can be tested; it may be supported or rejected. Rejecting it is not failure, since science advances by revising. A scientific law describes what happens in nature (often as a mathematical relationship), while a theory, supported by many tests, explains why it happens; one does not turn into the other.

Variables

A variable is any factor that can change in an experiment.

  • Independent variable: what we deliberately change.
  • Dependent variable: what we measure as the result.
  • Controlled variables: factors kept constant so the comparison is fair.
  • Control group: a sample where the tested factor is not changed, used for comparison.

Designing a good experiment

A fair test changes only one independent variable and keeps everything else constant; otherwise we cannot tell which factor caused the result.

We also repeat the trials and take the average to reduce random error.

Worked examples

Name the branch of chemistry for each job: (a) measuring the vitamin C content of a juice. (b) Studying the reactions that digest food in the body. (c) Making plastic from carbon chains.
Show the solution
  1. Look for the key word in each job.
  2. (a) Finding the amount of a component in a sample: analytical chemistry.
  3. (b) Reactions inside a living organism: biochemistry.
  4. (c) Carbon compounds: organic chemistry.
Answer: (a) Analytical, (b) biochemistry, (c) organic.
A student dissolves equal masses of sugar in 100mL of water at 20, 40 and 60∘C and times how long each takes to dissolve. Identify the independent, dependent and controlled variables.
Show the solution
  1. Independent variable: what the student deliberately changed, the water temperature (20, 40, 60∘C).
  2. Dependent variable: what was measured, the dissolving time.
  3. Controlled variables: mass of sugar, volume of water and stirring; they stay the same every trial.
  4. If the sugar mass changed too, the effect of temperature alone could not be known.
Answer: Independent: temperature. Dependent: dissolving time. Controlled: sugar mass, water volume, stirring.
A student tests the hypothesis "more light makes a plant grow taller". Plant A is put in sunlight with fertilizer; plant B is put in shade without fertilizer; both are measured after two weeks. What is wrong with the design and how can it be fixed?
Show the solution
  1. The intended independent variable is light only.
  2. But the student changed two factors at once: light and fertilizer.
  3. If A is taller, we cannot tell whether light or fertilizer caused it.
  4. Fix: two identical plants with the same soil, water and fertilizer, differing only in light, and repeat on several plants.
Answer: Two variables were changed together; change only the light and control everything else.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up independent and dependent variables. Independent: what I change; dependent: what I measure.
  • Treating a hypothesis as a proven fact. Correct: it is a proposed explanation that is tested and may be rejected.
  • Thinking a rejected hypothesis means the experiment failed. Correct: it is a useful result that leads to a better hypothesis.
  • Confusing biochemistry (living things) with organic chemistry (carbon compounds in general).
Summary
  • Analytical = what is in it and how much? Organic = carbon, biochemistry = living things, physical = energy.
  • A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation that comes before the experiment.
  • Change the independent variable, measure the dependent one, keep the controlled ones constant.
  • A fair test changes one factor at a time.

Measurement, Scientific Notation and Unit Conversion

What you will learn
  • Tell accuracy and precision apart
  • Convert between units using a conversion factor
  • Write numbers in scientific notation and multiply or divide them

Precision and accuracy

Measurement involves two different ideas. Precision: how close repeated measurements are to each other. Accuracy: how close a measurement is to the true value.

Measurements can be precise but not accurate, for example when a balance is not calibrated: the same reading repeats, but it is far from the truth.

Units and converting them

We measure length in meters, mass in kilograms, time in seconds, and volume in liters or cubic meters. To convert, multiply by a conversion factor: from a large unit to a small one multiply (the number grows); from a small unit to a large one divide (the number shrinks). Prefixes change the size of a unit:

  • kilo: 103 (thousand).
  • centi: 10−2 (one hundredth).
  • milli: 10−3 (one thousandth).
  • micro: 10−6 (one millionth).

Scientific notation

We write very large or very small numbers as a×10n where 1≤a<10 and n is an integer.

To multiply, multiply the numbers and add the exponents; to divide, divide the numbers and subtract the exponents.

  • Move the decimal point until it sits after the first non-zero digit.
  • The number of places you moved is the size of n.
  • A number that is 10 or more has a positive exponent; a number smaller than 1 has a negative exponent.

Laws and rules

Scientific notationnumber=a×10n1≤a<10; n is an integer, positive for large numbers and negative for small ones.
Unit conversionnew value=value×conversion factorThe factor is a ratio equal to 1, such as 1kg1000g​, so the quantity stays the same and only the unit changes.

Worked examples

Convert: (a) 3.2kg to g. (b) 450mL to L.
Show the solution
  1. (a) 1kg=1000g; going to a smaller unit gives a bigger number, so multiply.
  2. 3.2×1000=3200g.
  3. (b) 1L=1000mL; going to a larger unit gives a smaller number, so divide.
  4. 1000450​=0.45L. Check: 0.45×1000=450mL.
Answer: (a) 3200g. (b) 0.45L.
Write in scientific notation: (a) 56000. (b) 0.0072. Then compute (3×104)×(2×103).
Show the solution
  1. (a) Move the decimal point four places left so it sits after the 5; the number is large, so the exponent is positive: 5.6×104.
  2. (b) Move the decimal point three places right to reach 7.2; the number is below 1, so the exponent is negative: 7.2×10−3. Check: 7.2×0.001=0.0072.
  3. Multiplication: 3×2=6 and add the exponents 4+3=7.
Answer: (a) 5.6×104. (b) 7.2×10−3. Product: 6×107.
The true mass of a sample is 10.0g. Three students record: first 9.9, 10.0, 10.1; second 12.0, 12.1, 11.9; third 8.0, 12.0, 10.0. Describe each set.
Show the solution
  1. First: the readings are close together (precise) and the average 10.0 matches the true value (accurate).
  2. Second: the readings are close together (precise) but the average 12.0 is 2g from the truth, so not accurate; this suggests a systematic error such as an uncalibrated balance.
  3. Third: the readings are far apart (not precise); the average 38+12+10​=10 is close to the truth, but the spread makes any single reading unreliable.
Answer: First: precise and accurate. Second: precise but not accurate. Third: accurate on average but not precise.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up precision and accuracy. Precision: readings close to each other; accuracy: close to the true value.
  • Multiplying instead of dividing when converting. Ask: should the number grow (smaller unit) or shrink (larger unit)?
  • Miscounting decimal places: 0.00045=4.5×10−4, not 10−3. Check by moving the point back.
  • Calculating before unifying units, such as adding mL to L.
Summary
  • Precision = closeness of repeated measurements; accuracy = closeness to the true value.
  • Smaller unit = bigger number (multiply); larger unit = smaller number (divide).
  • Scientific notation: a×10n with 1≤a<10.
  • Multiply: add exponents; divide: subtract them.

Properties and Changes of Matter, Conservation of Mass

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell physical properties from chemical properties
  • Classify a change as physical or chemical
  • Use the law of conservation of mass and explain an apparent gain or loss

Physical and chemical properties

A physical property can be observed or measured without changing what the substance is: color, state, density, melting and boiling points, hardness, electrical conductivity, solubility.

A chemical property describes the ability of a substance to turn into a new substance, and shows only when a reaction happens: flammability, reaction with acids, rusting in moist air.

Physical properties are of two kinds: those that depend on the amount of matter, such as mass and volume, and characteristic ones that do not, such as density and melting point, which is why they are used to identify a substance.

Physical change

A physical change alters the shape, state or size of matter, but the substance stays the same: cutting paper, crushing salt, ice melting, water evaporating, sugar dissolving in water. It can often be reversed by physical means.

Chemical change

In a chemical change new substances with different properties form, because atoms are rearranged: wood burning, iron rusting, milk souring, an egg cooking. These signs suggest a change but do not prove it (boiling bubbles are a physical change; the decisive proof is a new substance forming). The signs:

  • A gas is released (bubbles not caused by boiling).
  • An unexpected color change.
  • A solid precipitate forms in a solution.
  • Heat or light is released, or heat is absorbed.
  • A new smell appears.

Conservation of mass

In any chemical change inside a closed system, the mass of the reactants equals the mass of the products; atoms are neither destroyed nor created, only rearranged. (Mass is also conserved in physical changes.)

If the mass seems to rise or fall, look for a gas that was not weighed: oxygen that entered the reaction (mass rises) or gas that escaped an open container (mass falls).

Laws and rules

Law of conservation of massmass of reactants=mass of productsIn a closed system; mass measured in grams (g).

Worked examples

Classify each as a physical or chemical change: (a) sugar dissolving in water. (b) An iron nail rusting. (c) Milk turning sour. (d) Cutting a sheet of paper.
Show the solution
  1. Ask: did a new substance with different properties form?
  2. (a) The sugar is still sugar and can be recovered by evaporating the water: physical.
  3. (b) Rust is a new brown, flaky substance, not iron: chemical.
  4. (c) An acid forms with a new taste and smell: chemical.
  5. (d) Only the shape changes: physical.
Answer: Physical: (a) and (d). Chemical: (b) and (c).
6g of magnesium burns completely with 4g of oxygen in a closed container, forming only magnesium oxide. What is the mass of the product?
Show the solution
  1. Use conservation of mass: mass of reactants = mass of products.
  2. Mass of reactants: 6+4=10g.
  3. So the magnesium oxide has a mass of 10g.
Answer: 10g.
In an open beaker, 50g of vinegar and 30g of baking soda give a total of 80g. After the fizzing stops the balance reads 78g. Where did the missing mass go?
Show the solution
  1. Mass before the reaction: 50+30=80g.
  2. The loss: 80−78=2g.
  3. The reaction produced carbon dioxide gas COX2​ that left the open beaker.
  4. In a closed container the reading would stay 80g; the law is not broken, the gas just was not weighed.
Answer: 2g left as COX2​ gas.
Common mistakes
  • Calling every change of shape or color chemical. A color change can be physical; always ask: did a new substance form?
  • Calling dissolving or boiling chemical reactions because "something changes". They are physical.
  • Believing mass "disappears" when something burns. Correct: it moves into gases, or is added from the air.
  • Mixing up a property with a change: flammability is a chemical property, burning is a chemical change.
Summary
  • Physical properties are measured without changing identity; chemical properties show when a new substance forms.
  • A physical change makes no new substance; a chemical change does.
  • Signs of a reaction (gas, color, precipitate, heat) suggest but do not prove.
  • Mass of reactants = mass of products in a closed system.

Density and Floating

What you will learn
  • Define density and compute it from mass and volume
  • Find mass or volume from density
  • Predict whether an object floats or sinks

What is density?

Density is the mass per unit volume of a substance, that is, how much mass is packed into a given volume. We find it by dividing mass by volume.

Density is a characteristic physical property that does not depend on the sample size. A large and a small piece of aluminum have the same density, 2.7g/cm3, while mass and volume change when we divide the sample, because both halve together and the ratio stays the same. Density changes slightly with temperature, usually decreasing on heating.

Measuring volume and units

Mass is measured with a balance. Liquid volume is measured with a graduated cylinder. For an irregular solid we find the volume by displacement: volume = water level after submerging − level before.

We use g/mL for liquids and g/cm3 for solids; they are equal because 1mL=1cm3. The density of water is about 1g/mL.

Floating and sinking

An object less dense than a liquid floats in it; a denser one sinks. So oil (0.8g/mL) floats on water, iron (7.9g/cm3) sinks in it, and ice (0.92g/cm3) floats on water.

When several liquids that do not mix are poured together they form layers, the densest at the bottom. A large volume does not mean sinking; only density matters. If the object and the liquid have equal density, the object stays suspended where it is.

Laws and rules

DensityDensity=volumemass​Mass in grams (g), volume in mL or cm3, so density is in g/mL or g/cm3.
MassMass=density×volumeMultiply both sides of the density formula by the volume.
VolumeVolume=densitymass​Divide the mass by the density.

Worked examples

A sample has a mass of 90g and a volume of 30mL. What is its density?
Show the solution
  1. Formula: density = mass ÷ volume.
  2. Substitute: 3090​.
  3. 3090​=3g/mL. Check: 3×30=90g.
Answer: 3g/mL.
The density of aluminum is 2.7g/cm3. What is the volume of a piece with a mass of 135g?
Show the solution
  1. Rearrange: volume = mass ÷ density.
  2. Substitute: 2.7135​.
  3. 2.7135​=50cm3. Check: 2.7×50=135g.
Answer: 50cm3.
A metal piece has a mass of 36g. It is submerged in a cylinder with 30mL of water and the level rises to 45mL. Find its density, then decide whether it floats in water and whether it floats in a liquid of density 2.8g/mL.
Show the solution
  1. Volume from displacement: 45−30=15mL.
  2. Density: 1536​=2.4g/mL.
  3. In water: 2.4>1, so the piece is denser than water and sinks.
  4. In the liquid: 2.4<2.8, so the piece is less dense than the liquid and floats.
Answer: Density 2.4g/mL; it sinks in water and floats in the liquid of density 2.8g/mL.
Common mistakes
  • Dividing volume by mass instead of the reverse. Check: density × volume must give the mass.
  • Thinking the heavier object always sinks. Compare the object's density with the liquid's density, not the masses.
  • Thinking that cutting a sample changes its density. Density is a characteristic property, constant for the same substance and temperature.
  • Forgetting to subtract the starting cylinder reading in displacement, or mixing up mL and L.
Summary
  • Density = mass ÷ volume, measured in g/mL or g/cm3.
  • Density does not change when you divide a sample, while mass and volume do.
  • Volume of an irregular object = difference of the two water readings.
  • Less dense than the liquid: floats; denser: sinks.

Elements, Compounds and Mixtures

What you will learn
  • Classify matter as element, compound or mixture
  • Tell homogeneous from heterogeneous mixtures
  • Apply the law of definite proportions

Pure substances and mixtures

A pure substance has a fixed composition and comes in two kinds. An element is one kind of atom that cannot be broken down by chemical means, such as oxygen OX2​ and iron Fe. A compound is two or more elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio, such as water HX2​O.

A compound's properties differ from those of its elements: sodium is a reactive metal and chlorine a poisonous gas, but the table salt made from them is safe. A compound can only be broken down by a chemical change.

Mixtures

A mixture is two or more substances physically combined with no chemical bonding. Its proportions can vary, each part keeps its own properties, and the parts can be separated by physical means.

  • Homogeneous mixture (solution): looks like one substance with the parts evenly spread, such as salt water, air and alloys.
  • Heterogeneous mixture: the parts can be seen or differ from place to place, such as sand in water, a salad and granite.
  • Two kinds of heterogeneous mixture: a suspension, whose large particles settle when left alone (muddy water), and a colloid, whose small particles stay suspended and scatter light (milk, fog).

Law of definite proportions

In a given compound the ratio of the masses of its elements is always the same, whatever the source or the sample size. In water, every 1g of hydrogen combines with 8g of oxygen, always.

This is what separates a compound from a mixture: the proportions of a mixture can be changed freely (one spoon or two of salt in water).

Laws and rules

Law of definite proportionsmass of second elementmass of first element​=constantFor any sample of the same compound, each mass in grams (g).

Worked examples

Classify as an element, a compound, a homogeneous mixture or a heterogeneous mixture: oxygen OX2​, water HX2​O, salt water, sand with water.
Show the solution
  1. Oxygen is one kind of atom: element.
  2. Water has hydrogen and oxygen atoms chemically bonded in a fixed ratio: compound.
  3. Salt water looks uniform and its parts can be separated by evaporation: homogeneous mixture.
  4. In sand with water the sand grains are visible and can be filtered: heterogeneous mixture.
Answer: Oxygen: element; water: compound; salt water: homogeneous mixture; sand with water: heterogeneous mixture.
A water sample contains 2g of hydrogen and 16g of oxygen. How many grams of hydrogen combine with 40g of oxygen? What is the mass of water formed?
Show the solution
  1. Ratio from the sample: 216​=8g of oxygen per 1g of hydrogen.
  2. Hydrogen needed: 840​=5g.
  3. Mass of water: 5+40=45g.
  4. Check: 540​=8, the same ratio.
Answer: 5g of hydrogen; the water has a mass of 45g.
A 8g sample of a compound contains 6.4g of copper and 1.6g of oxygen. (a) How much copper is in 20g of the same compound? (b) Another sample has 3g of copper and 1g of oxygen: is it the same compound?
Show the solution
  1. Fraction of copper: 86.4​=0.8, so 0.8 of the mass is copper.
  2. (a) In 20g: 0.8×20=16g of copper, leaving 4g of oxygen. Check: 16+4=20.
  3. (b) Copper to oxygen in the first sample: 1.66.4​=4.
  4. In the other sample: 13​=3. The ratios differ, so it is not the same compound (it may be another compound or a mixture).
Answer: (a) 16g of copper. (b) No, because the ratio 3 differs from 4.
Common mistakes
  • Treating a mixture as a compound. A compound has a fixed ratio and needs a chemical reaction to separate; a mixture has variable proportions and is separated physically.
  • Thinking every homogeneous mixture is a compound. Air and salt water are mixtures (solutions) because their proportions can vary.
  • Thinking a compound keeps the properties of its elements. Salt resembles neither sodium nor chlorine.
  • Applying the law of definite proportions to mixtures. It applies to compounds only.
Summary
  • An element is one kind of atom; a compound is elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio.
  • A mixture is physically combined, its parts keep their properties and are separated physically.
  • Homogeneous looks like one substance; in heterogeneous the parts can be seen.
  • Definite proportions: the mass ratio in a compound is constant.

Methods of Separating Mixtures

What you will learn
  • Choose the right separation method for a mixture
  • Explain distillation and why it works
  • Read a chromatography strip
  • Order the steps for separating a three-part mixture

The idea of separation

The parts of a mixture keep their own properties, so we use a difference in one physical property to separate them. The question is always: how do the two substances differ?

  • Filtration: separates an undissolved solid from a liquid (particle size).
  • Evaporation and crystallization: separate a dissolved solid from its liquid (volatility).
  • Magnet: separates a magnetic substance such as iron filings.
  • Decanting and a separating funnel: separate two liquids that do not mix, like oil and water (density).
  • Distillation: separates liquids with different boiling points.
  • Chromatography: separates dissolved substances (such as ink dyes) that differ in how strongly they are attracted to the solvent and the paper.

Distillation

We heat the solution; the substance with the lower boiling point evaporates first, the condenser cools it back into a liquid, and it is collected in a container. This gives pure water from sea water, because the salt does not evaporate and stays in the flask.

When ethanol (boils at 78∘C) and water (boils at 100∘C) are distilled, ethanol comes over first and the water stays behind. Density has nothing to do with the order of evaporation. The same idea separates crude oil into gasoline, kerosene and diesel in fractional distillation.

Chromatography

Put a spot of ink above the start line on a paper strip and dip its end in the solvent. The solvent climbs the paper carrying the dyes; a dye that dissolves well in the solvent and clings little to the paper travels farther. The dyes split into spots, and two spots at the same height with the same look are the same dye.

Separating a three-part mixture

Break the problem into steps, isolating one substance at a time using a property that sets it apart. Example: sand, salt and iron filings. Start with a magnet (iron), then add water, stir and filter (sand stays on the paper), then evaporate the filtrate to leave the salt.

Worked examples

Choose the right separation method: (a) sand from water. (b) Salt from salt water. (c) Pure water from sea water. (d) The dyes in green ink.
Show the solution
  1. Find the difference between the two parts in each case.
  2. (a) Sand is an undissolved solid: filtration.
  3. (b) We want the dissolved salt and do not need the water: evaporate until the salt crystallizes.
  4. (c) We want the water itself: distillation, cooling and collecting the vapor.
  5. (d) Dissolved dyes that differ in attraction to the solvent: chromatography.
Answer: (a) Filtration. (b) Evaporation. (c) Distillation. (d) Chromatography.
A mixture of iron filings, table salt and sand. Write the steps to separate the three parts in order.
Show the solution
  1. Pass a magnet over the mixture; the iron filings stick to it and are removed.
  2. Add water to the rest (salt and sand) and stir: the salt dissolves, the sand does not.
  3. Filter: the sand stays on the filter paper and the salt water (filtrate) passes through.
  4. Heat the filtrate until the water evaporates, leaving solid salt.
Answer: Magnet (iron), then dissolve and filter (sand), then evaporate the filtrate (salt).
On a chromatography strip (shown alongside) a spot of unknown ink and a spot of reference dye were placed. At the end the ink showed three spots, and the reference showed one spot at the same height as one of the ink spots. What do we conclude?
Show the solution
  1. Three spots from the ink mean it is a mixture of at least three dyes.
  2. The reference spot is at the same height as one ink spot: the reference dye is present in the ink.
  3. The other two spots are different dyes because they travelled different distances, so they are attracted differently to the solvent.
Answer: The ink is a mixture of three dyes, one of which is the reference dye.
Common mistakes
  • Using filtration to separate dissolved salt from water. Dissolved salt passes through the paper with the water; use evaporation or distillation.
  • Choosing evaporation to recover the water. Evaporating to dryness loses the water; to keep the water use distillation.
  • Linking the order of evaporation in distillation to density. The criterion is boiling point: the lower one evaporates first.
  • Filtering the dry mixture before adding water. Filtration separates a solid from a liquid, so the salt must be dissolved first.
Summary
  • Choose the method by the property that differs between the substances.
  • Filtration: undissolved solid; evaporation: dissolved solid; distillation: liquids with different boiling points; chromatography: dissolved dyes.
  • In distillation the lower-boiling substance evaporates first, then condenses and is collected.
  • A three-part mixture is separated step by step: magnet, then dissolve and filter, then evaporate.
