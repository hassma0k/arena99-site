The idea of a limit and estimating it

What you will learn
  • Understand that a limit is the value a function approaches
  • Estimate a limit from a table or a graph
  • Find the limit of a piecewise function from both sides

What does a limit mean?

We say limx→a​f(x)=L if the values f(x) get close to L as x gets close to a, without necessarily reaching it.

What matters is the values near a, not the value at a itself; the function may even be undefined at a and still have a limit.

Estimating from a table

Pick values of x approaching a from the left (smaller than a) and from the right (larger than a), and see where f(x) is heading.

If both sides head to the same number, that number is the limit.

One-sided limits

We write limx→a−​ for approaching from the left, and limx→a+​ for approaching from the right.

A function has a limit at a only when both one-sided limits are equal.

A limit may also fail to exist when the values grow without bound near a, as with x1​ at x=0.

Piecewise functions

A piecewise function uses a different rule on each side of its turning point. At the turning point we find the limit of the left rule and of the right rule, then compare them.

Laws and rules

Definition of a limitlimx→a​f(x)=LL is the number f(x) approaches as x approaches a.
Condition for a limit to existlimx→a−​f(x)=limx→a+​f(x)The left and right limits must be equal; if they differ, the limit does not exist.

Worked examples

f(x)=x−1x2−1​. Estimate limx→1​f(x) using x=0.9, 0.99, 1.01, 1.1.
Show the solution
  1. Simplify: x−1x2−1​=x+1 for every x=1.
  2. At 0.9 and 0.99 the values are 1.9 and 1.99 (approaching 2 from the left).
  3. At 1.01 and 1.1 the values are 2.01 and 2.1 (approaching 2 from the right).
  4. Both sides head to the same number.
Answer: The limit is 2, even though the function is undefined at x=1.
If f(x)=2x+1 for x<2 and f(x)=x+3 for x≥2, find limx→2​f(x).
Show the solution
  1. From the left use 2x+1: 2(2)+1=5.
  2. From the right use x+3: 2+3=5.
  3. The two one-sided limits are equal.
Answer: The limit is 5.
If f(x)=3x+2 for x<1 and f(x)=x2+1 for x≥1, find limx→1​f(x).
Show the solution
  1. From the left: 3(1)+2=5.
  2. From the right: 12+1=2.
  3. The one-sided limits differ: 5=2.
Answer: The limit does not exist, because the two sides give different values.
Common mistakes
  • Taking the value of the function at the point as the limit. The limit is what the nearby values approach.
  • Computing only one side of a piecewise function. You need both left and right.
  • Averaging or adding the two numbers when the sides differ; in that case the limit does not exist.
Summary
  • A limit is the value the function approaches, not its value at the point.
  • We estimate a limit with values close to the point on both sides.
  • The limit exists only if the left and right limits are equal.
  • For a piecewise function, use each side's own rule.

Evaluating limits algebraically

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Find a limit by direct substitution
  • Recognise the form 00​
  • Factor and cancel a common factor to find the limit

Step one: substitute

For polynomials, and for a quotient of polynomials whose denominator is not zero, we get the limit by substituting x=a directly.

For example limx→2​(3x+4)=3(2)+4=10.

When you get 00​

If substitution gives 0 on top and 0 on the bottom, that does not mean the limit is zero or that it does not exist. It means the numerator and denominator share a factor (x−a).

Factor both, cancel the common factor, then substitute again.

Factoring tools

Difference of squares: x2−b2=(x−b)(x+b). Difference of cubes: x3−b3=(x−b)(x2+bx+b2).

Also trinomial factoring and taking out a common factor such as x2+3x=x(x+3).

Why may we cancel?

A limit looks at x near a, not at a itself. Since x=a, the factor (x−a) is not zero, so it can be cancelled.

When a root appears: multiply by the conjugate

If substitution gives 00​ and the fraction contains a root, multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the expression with the root, then simplify. For example xx+4​−2​=x(x+4​+2)(x+4)−4​=x+4​+21​, so the limit as x→0 equals 41​.

Laws and rules

Limit lawslim(f+g)=limf+limgThe limit of a sum, difference or product equals the sum, difference or product of the limits.
Limit of a quotientlimgf​=limglimf​Provided limg=0.
Difference of squaresx2−b2=(x−b)(x+b)x and b are numbers.
Difference of cubesx3−b3=(x−b)(x2+bx+b2)x and b are numbers.

Worked examples

Find limx→3​(2x2−x).
Show the solution
  1. It is a polynomial, so substitute x=3 directly.
  2. 2(3)2−3=2(9)−3=18−3.
Answer: 15
Find limx→2​x−2x2−4​.
Show the solution
  1. Substitution gives 00​, so factor.
  2. x2−4=(x−2)(x+2).
  3. Cancel (x−2): x+2 remains.
  4. Substitute x=2: 2+2=4.
Answer: 4
Find limx→3​x2−5x+6x2−9​.
Show the solution
  1. Substitution gives 00​ because 9−9=0 and 9−15+6=0.
  2. Numerator: (x−3)(x+3). Denominator: (x−2)(x−3).
  3. Cancel (x−3): x−2x+3​ remains.
  4. Substitute x=3: 16​=6.
Answer: 6
Common mistakes
  • Stopping at 00​ and saying "does not exist" or "zero". It is a signal that you must factor.
  • Cancelling a term instead of a factor, like cancelling x in xx2+3​. Only multiplied factors can be cancelled.
  • Factoring the numerator but not the denominator, or forgetting to substitute after cancelling.
  • Treating 05​, for example, like 00​. If the denominator is zero and the numerator is not, there is no common factor, and the limit does not exist (or is infinite).
Summary
  • Try direct substitution first.
  • If you get 00​, factor the numerator and denominator.
  • Cancel the common factor (x−a), then substitute.
  • Memorise the difference of squares and of cubes.

Limits at infinity

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Understand what x→∞ means
  • Find the limit of a rational function by comparing degrees
  • Handle roots in the numerator or denominator

What happens when x gets huge?

The symbol x→∞ means x grows without bound. Then x1​ and x21​ get closer and closer to zero.

This is the one fact we need: limx→∞​xn1​=0 for every n>0.

Method: divide by the highest power in the denominator

Divide every term in the numerator and denominator by the highest power of x in the denominator. The fractional terms become x1​ and the like, and go to zero.

Shortcut by comparing degrees

  • Degree of numerator less than degree of denominator: the limit is 0.
  • Equal degrees: the limit is the ratio of the leading coefficients.
  • Degree of numerator greater: the limit is ∞ or −∞ (no finite limit).

When there is a root

As x→∞, x is positive, so x2​=x. So we divide by x and put it under the root as x2.

For example 4x2+1​ behaves like 2x when x is large.

But when x→−∞, x is negative and x2​=−x, so watch the sign.

Laws and rules

Limit of a reciprocal powerlimx→∞​xn1​=0n is a positive number.
Equal degreeslimx→∞​bxn+…axn+…​=ba​a and b are the leading coefficients of the numerator and denominator, and b=0.

Worked examples

Find limx→∞​2x−35x+1​.
Show the solution
  1. The highest power in the denominator is x, so divide top and bottom by x.
  2. We get 2−x3​5+x1​​.
  3. As x→∞, x1​ and x3​ go to 0.
  4. The result is 25​.
Answer: 25​
Find limx→∞​x2+1x+4​.
Show the solution
  1. The numerator has degree 1, less than the denominator's degree 2.
  2. Divide by x2: 1+x21​x1​+x24​​.
  3. The numerator goes to 0 and the denominator to 1.
Answer: 0
Find limx→∞​x−29x2+1​​.
Show the solution
  1. Divide the numerator and denominator by x.
  2. In the numerator: x9x2+1​​=9+x21​​ because x>0.
  3. In the denominator: xx−2​=1−x2​.
  4. As x→∞ the numerator goes to 9​=3 and the denominator to 1.
Answer: 3
Common mistakes
  • Thinking ∞∞​=1. It is an indeterminate form; you must divide by the highest power.
  • Dropping the root wrongly, writing 4x2+1​=2x+1. The correct idea is that it approaches 2x only.
  • Comparing the degrees the wrong way round. A numerator of higher degree means there is no finite limit.
Summary
  • xn1​ goes to 0 as x→∞.
  • Divide by the highest power of x in the denominator.
  • Degrees: smaller gives 0, equal gives the coefficient ratio, larger gives ∞.
  • With a root: x2​=x for positive x.

Continuity

What you will learn
  • Know the conditions for continuity at a point
  • Locate points of discontinuity
  • Find a constant that makes a function continuous

A continuous function

A function is continuous at x=a if you can draw its graph through that point without lifting your pen.

Mathematically, three conditions must hold together.

Conditions for continuity

  • f(a) is defined.
  • limx→a​f(x) exists.
  • The limit equals the function value: limx→a​f(x)=f(a).

Where does a function break?

Polynomials are continuous everywhere. Rational functions are continuous except at numbers that make the denominator zero.

For a piecewise function we only check the turning point: set the left limit, the right limit and the function value equal.

Types of discontinuity: a hole (the limit exists but differs from the value, or the value is undefined), a jump (the one-sided limits differ), and an infinite break (the denominator is zero and the values grow without bound).

Finding a constant for continuity

If the function has an unknown constant k, make the left rule at the turning point equal the right rule there, then solve an equation in k.

Laws and rules

Continuity at alimx→a​f(x)=f(a)It requires f(a) to be defined and the limit to exist.

Worked examples

Let f(x)=x−2x2−4​ for x=2, and f(2)=4. Is f continuous at x=2?
Show the solution
  1. f(2)=4 is defined.
  2. limx→2​x−2(x−2)(x+2)​=limx→2​(x+2)=4.
  3. The limit 4 equals f(2)=4.
Answer: Yes, f is continuous at x=2.
Find k that makes f continuous at x=3, where f(x)=2x+1 for x<3 and f(x)=kx−2 for x≥3.
Show the solution
  1. Left limit: 2(3)+1=7.
  2. Right value: 3k−2.
  3. Set them equal: 3k−2=7, so 3k=9.
Answer: k=3
Find k that makes f continuous at x=2, where f(x)=kx+1 for x<2 and f(x)=x2−k for x≥2.
Show the solution
  1. Left at 2: 2k+1.
  2. Right at 2: 4−k.
  3. Set them equal: 2k+1=4−k.
  4. 3k=3, so k=1.
  5. Check: left 3, right 3.
Answer: k=1
Common mistakes
  • Checking only that the limit exists and forgetting that it must equal the function value.
  • Equating the two rules at a wrong value; use the turning point itself in both rules.
  • Thinking a rational function is broken everywhere; it breaks only where the denominator is zero.
Summary
  • Continuous at a: defined, has a limit, and limit = value.
  • Polynomials are always continuous.
  • For a piecewise function, equate the two rules at the turning point.
  • An unknown constant comes from the equation left = right.

Tangents and instantaneous velocity

What you will learn
  • Understand the tangent slope as a limit
  • Define the derivative with a limit
  • Find an instantaneous velocity

From secant to tangent

Take two points on the curve: (a, f(a)) and (a+h, f(a+h)). The slope of the secant line between them is hf(a+h)−f(a)​.

As h gets smaller, the secant approaches the tangent, so the tangent slope is the limit of this fraction as h→0.

The derivative at a point

This limit is called the derivative of the function at x=a, written f′(a). So f′(a) is the slope of the tangent to the curve of f at x=a. It can also be written f′(a)=limx→a​x−af(x)−f(a)​.

Tangent line: y−f(a)=f′(a)(x−a).

Instantaneous velocity

If s(t) is an object's position at time t, the average velocity between two moments is the change in position divided by the time taken, that is a secant slope. The instantaneous velocity at t is the tangent slope, that is s′(t).

Its unit is m/s if s is in metres and t in seconds.

Laws and rules

Definition of the derivativef′(a)=limh→0​hf(a+h)−f(a)​f′(a) is the tangent slope at x=a, and h is a small amount going to zero.
Instantaneous velocityv(t)=s′(t)s is the position in metres, t the time in seconds, v the velocity in m/s.
Tangent liney−f(a)=f′(a)(x−a)A line through the point (a, f(a)) with slope f′(a).

Worked examples

Find the slope of the tangent to f(x)=x2 at x=3, then the tangent line.
Show the solution
  1. f(3)=9 and f(3+h)=9+6h+h2.
  2. The fraction: h6h+h2​=6+h.
  3. As h→0 the slope becomes 6.
  4. The line: y−9=6(x−3), that is y=6x−9.
Answer: Slope 6, tangent y=6x−9.
An object moves with s=t2+4t metres. Find its instantaneous velocity at t=3 s.
Show the solution
  1. s(3)=9+12=21.
  2. s(3+h)=9+6h+h2+12+4h=21+10h+h2.
  3. The fraction: h10h+h2​=10+h.
  4. As h→0 it becomes 10.
Answer: 10m/s
At which point of f(x)=x2 is the tangent slope 8?
Show the solution
  1. Slope at x=a: h(a+h)2−a2​=h2ah+h2​=2a+h.
  2. As h→0 the slope is 2a.
  3. 2a=8 gives a=4, and f(4)=16.
Answer: At the point (4, 16).
Common mistakes
  • Computing the secant slope between two far-apart points and calling it the tangent slope; you must let h→0.
  • Mixing average velocity with instantaneous velocity; the first is over an interval, the second at a moment.
  • Treating f′(a) as the function value f(a); the first is a slope, the second a height.
Summary
  • The tangent slope at a equals f′(a).
  • f′(a) is the limit of secant slopes as h→0.
  • Instantaneous velocity is the derivative of position with respect to time.
  • Tangent line: y−f(a)=f′(a)(x−a).

Differentiation rules

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Differentiate constants, linear functions, powers and sums
  • Apply the product rule and the chain rule
  • Find the value of a derivative at a point

We do not use the limit every time

From the limit definition we get quick rules that make differentiation mechanical. The most important is the power rule: to differentiate xn, bring the exponent down as a coefficient and lower the exponent by one.

Power and sum rules

The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, and a constant multiplier stays. For example (2x3+x)′=6x2+1.

The derivative of a constant is 0, and the derivative of a linear function ax+b is its slope a.

The product and quotient rules

The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives. Instead, differentiate the first and multiply by the second as it is, then add the first as it is times the derivative of the second.

The quotient rule: differentiate the numerator and multiply by the denominator, subtract the numerator times the derivative of the denominator, and divide by the square of the denominator.

The chain rule

If the function is a power of an expression (g(x))n, differentiate the power first, then multiply by the derivative of what is inside.

We call this "derivative of the outside times derivative of the inside".

The derivative at a point

To find f′(a), first differentiate to get f′(x), then substitute x=a. Never substitute before differentiating.

Laws and rules

Constant and linear function(k)′=0,(ax+b)′=ak, a and b are constants.
Power rule(xn)′=nxn−1n is a constant; for example (x4)′=4x3.
Sum and constant multiple(af+bg)′=af′+bg′a and b are constants, f and g are functions.
Product rule(f⋅g)′=f′⋅g+f⋅g′f and g are functions of x.
Chain rule((g(x))n)′=n(g(x))n−1⋅g′(x)g(x) is the inside expression and n the outside exponent.
Quotient rule(gf​)′=g2f′⋅g−f⋅g′​f and g are functions of x, and g=0.

Worked examples

Find f′(x) and then f′(1) for f(x)=5x4−3x2+8.
Show the solution
  1. Differentiate each term: (5x4)′=20x3.
  2. (3x2)′=6x and (8)′=0.
  3. So f′(x)=20x3−6x.
  4. Substitute x=1: 20−6=14.
Answer: f′(x)=20x3−6x and f′(1)=14.
Differentiate f(x)=x2(3x−1) and find f′(2).
Show the solution
  1. First: x2 with derivative 2x; second: 3x−1 with derivative 3.
  2. f′(x)=2x(3x−1)+x2⋅3.
  3. =6x2−2x+3x2=9x2−2x.
  4. Check by expanding: f=3x3−x2, whose derivative is 9x2−2x.
  5. At x=2: 9(4)−2(2)=36−4=32.
Answer: f′(x)=9x2−2x and f′(2)=32.
Find f′(1) if f(x)=(x2+1)3.
Show the solution
  1. Outside: exponent 3, derivative 3(x2+1)2.
  2. Inside x2+1 has derivative 2x.
  3. f′(x)=3(x2+1)2⋅2x=6x(x2+1)2.
  4. At x=1: 6(1)(2)2=6×4=24.
Answer: 24
Common mistakes
  • Writing the derivative of a constant like 8 as 8 or 1; it is 0.
  • Making the derivative of a product the product of the derivatives: (f⋅g)′=f′⋅g′.
  • Forgetting the inner derivative in the chain rule, writing ((2x+1)3)′=3(2x+1)2 instead of 6(2x+1)2.
  • Substituting the value into the function and then differentiating the resulting number; differentiate first, then substitute.
Summary
  • (xn)′=nxn−1 and a constant has derivative 0.
  • The derivative of ax+b is a.
  • Product: first' times second plus first times second'.
  • Chain: derivative of outside times derivative of inside.
  • For f′(a): differentiate, then substitute.

Increasing, decreasing and extreme values

What you will learn
  • Find increasing and decreasing intervals from the sign of the derivative
  • Find critical points
  • Compute the minimum or maximum value

The derivative tells us the direction

The derivative is the slope, and it tells us the monotonicity of the function: where it increases and where it decreases. If the slope is positive the curve is rising; if it is negative the curve is falling.

So a function is increasing where f′(x)>0 and decreasing where f′(x)<0.

Critical points

When the direction changes from falling to rising, the slope is momentarily zero, that is f′(x)=0. Solving this equation gives the critical point.

Deciding the type

  • If the sign of f′ changes from negative to positive, the point is a minimum.
  • If it changes from positive to negative, the point is a maximum.

Read the question carefully

The minimum value is the value of the function f(x) at the critical point, not the value of x itself. So substitute the critical x into f.

Laws and rules

Sign of the derivativef′(x)>0⇒ increasingIf f′(x)<0 the function is decreasing on that interval.
Critical pointf′(x)=0Solving this equation gives the values of x that may give a minimum or maximum.

Worked examples

On which interval is f(x)=x2+6x increasing?
Show the solution
  1. f′(x)=2x+6.
  2. We need f′(x)>0: 2x+6>0.
  3. 2x>−6, so x>−3.
Answer: It is increasing when x>−3.
Find the minimum value of f(x)=x2−4x.
Show the solution
  1. f′(x)=2x−4.
  2. Set it to zero: 2x−4=0, so x=2.
  3. The sign of f′ is negative before 2 and positive after, so it is a minimum.
  4. Substitute into the function: f(2)=4−8=−4.
Answer: The minimum value is −4, at x=2.
Find the increasing and decreasing intervals of f(x)=x3−12x, and its maximum and minimum values.
Show the solution
  1. f′(x)=3x2−12=3(x−2)(x+2).
  2. The roots are x=−2 and x=2.
  3. For x>2 or x<−2 the sign is positive: increasing. For −2<x<2 it is negative (at 0 it equals −12): decreasing.
  4. At x=−2 it goes from increasing to decreasing: f(−2)=−8+24=16 is a maximum.
  5. At x=2 it goes from decreasing to increasing: f(2)=8−24=−16 is a minimum.
Answer: Increasing on x<−2 and on x>2, decreasing on −2<x<2; maximum 16, minimum −16.
Common mistakes
  • Giving the critical x as the minimum value; the answer is f at that point. (For x2−6x+11 the critical point is 3 and the minimum value is 2.)
  • Forgetting to flip the inequality when dividing by a negative number.
  • Claiming every point where f′=0 is a minimum or maximum without checking the sign change.
Summary
  • f′>0 means increasing, f′<0 means decreasing.
  • Critical points come from f′(x)=0.
  • A sign change from negative to positive means a minimum.
  • The minimum value is found by substituting the critical point into f.
