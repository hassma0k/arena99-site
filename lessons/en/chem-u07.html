Dynamic Equilibrium and Catalysts

What you will learn
  • Understand why concentrations stay constant at equilibrium even though reactions go on
  • Compare forward and reverse rates before and at equilibrium
  • Know what a catalyst does to equilibrium and what it does not do

Reversible reactions

Some reactions run in both directions: reactants form products, and products turn back into reactants. We write this with two arrows: A​B.

If we start with reactants only, the forward rate is high and the reverse rate is zero, because no products exist yet.

How equilibrium is reached

As time passes, reactant concentrations fall so the forward reaction slows, and product concentrations rise so the reverse reaction speeds up.

At some moment the two rates become equal. From then on each substance forms at the same rate at which it is used up, so its concentration stops changing. This is dynamic equilibrium.

Why "dynamic"?

Because both reactions keep going; molecules change in both directions all the time. Only the concentrations are steady, not the motion.

Picture a person walking down an escalator that moves up at the same speed: they stay in place while their legs keep moving.

  • Concentrations at equilibrium are constant, not necessarily equal.
  • Equilibrium needs a closed system: nothing enters or leaves.
  • We reach the same equilibrium whether we start from reactants or from products.

Catalysts

A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a reaction by giving it an easier path (lower activation energy). It speeds up the forward and reverse reactions by the same amount.

So the system reaches equilibrium sooner, but the equilibrium concentrations and the value of K do not change, and the equilibrium is not shifted either way.

Laws and rules

Condition for equilibriumforward rate=reverse rateEach rate is measured in mol/L per second. When they are equal, concentrations stay constant.

Worked examples

A reversible reaction starts with reactants only. The forward rate at the start is 8 units. At equilibrium the forward rate is 5 units. What is the reverse rate at the start and at equilibrium?
Show the solution
  1. At the start there are no products, so the reverse rate is zero.
  2. The forward rate slowed from 8 to 5 because reactants were used up.
  3. At equilibrium the rates are equal, so the reverse rate equals the forward rate: 5 units.
  4. Forward fell from 8 to 5 and reverse rose from 0 to 5, so they met at equilibrium.
Answer: Reverse rate: zero at the start and 5 units at equilibrium.
A catalyst is added to a reversible reaction at equilibrium whose K is 4. What happens to K and to the product concentration? Would the time to reach equilibrium change if we repeated the experiment?
Show the solution
  1. A catalyst speeds both directions equally, so the rates stay equal and nothing pushes the system either way.
  2. So the product concentration stays the same.
  3. K changes only with temperature, so it stays 4.
  4. If we repeated the reaction with the catalyst present, equilibrium would be reached in a shorter time.
Answer: K stays 4, product concentration is unchanged, and equilibrium is reached sooner.
In a closed container at equilibrium a student says: "The reaction has stopped because the concentrations no longer change." Is the student right? What happens if the container is open and a gaseous product escapes?
Show the solution
  1. Constant concentrations do not mean the reaction stopped; they mean forward rate equals reverse rate.
  2. Each substance is used up at the same rate at which it forms, so the student is wrong.
  3. In an open container the gaseous product keeps escaping, so its concentration keeps falling and the reverse rate stays below the forward rate.
  4. So equilibrium is never reached in an open system.
Answer: Not right: both reactions continue at equal rates, and equilibrium needs a closed system.
Common mistakes
  • Many students think equilibrium means the reaction has stopped. In fact both reactions continue at equal rates.
  • They think concentrations are equal at equilibrium. They are constant; reactants may be far more or far less than products.
  • They think a catalyst increases the amount of product. It only makes equilibrium arrive sooner and does not move its position.
Summary
  • At equilibrium the forward and reverse rates are equal and concentrations are constant.
  • Equilibrium is dynamic: both reactions go on.
  • It is reached in a closed system, from either side.
  • A catalyst speeds up reaching equilibrium but does not change concentrations or K.

The Equilibrium Expression, Pure Substances and Solubility Product

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Write the equilibrium expression from a balanced equation
  • Know which substances appear in it and which do not
  • Write the solubility product for a sparingly soluble salt

The rule for writing it

At equilibrium a certain ratio of concentrations stays constant at a fixed temperature. Product concentrations go on top and reactant concentrations go below.

Each concentration is raised to a power equal to its coefficient in the balanced equation. Square brackets mean concentration, so [NHX3​] is the concentration of ammonia in mol/L.

  • For NX2​(g)+3HX2​(g)​2NHX3​(g) the expression is [NX2​][HX2​]3[NHX3​]2​.
  • Always balance the equation first.

What goes in and what stays out

Gases and dissolved substances change concentration during the reaction, so they appear in the expression.

A pure solid or pure liquid has a fixed concentration (set by its density), so it does not change however much there is. We leave it out; its value is absorbed into K.

  • CaCOX3​(s)​CaO(s)+COX2​(g): only [COX2​] appears.
  • A gas always appears, and a dissolved ion appears.
  • Liquid water is left out too: for NHX3​(aq)+HX2​O(l)​NHX4​X+(aq)+OHX−(aq) the expression is [NHX3​][NHX4​X+][OHX−]​.

The solubility product

A sparingly soluble salt such as PbIX2​ sets up an equilibrium in water between the solid and its dissolved ions: PbIX2​(s)​PbX2+(aq)+2IX−(aq).

The solid is left out, so the expression is just the product of the ion concentrations, each raised to its coefficient. We call it the solubility product.

The smaller its value, the less of the salt dissolves.

Laws and rules

Equilibrium constantK=[reactants]coefficients[products]coefficients​Square brackets mean concentration in mol/L; exponents are the coefficients of the balanced equation. Pure solids and liquids are not written.
Solubility productKsp​=[cation]its coefficient×[anion]its coefficientWritten for a sparingly soluble salt; the solid salt does not appear.

Worked examples

Write the equilibrium expression for 2SOX2​(g)+OX2​(g)​2SOX3​(g).
Show the solution
  1. Products on top: [SOX3​] with coefficient 2, so exponent 2.
  2. Reactants below: [SOX2​] with exponent 2 and [OX2​] with exponent 1 (not written).
  3. Combine: [SOX2​]2[OX2​][SOX3​]2​.
Answer: [SOX2​]2[OX2​][SOX3​]2​
Write the equilibrium expression for each: (a) CaCOX3​(s)​CaO(s)+COX2​(g) (b) C(s)+COX2​(g)​2CO(g).
Show the solution
  1. (a) CaCOX3​ and CaO are solids, so they stay out. Only [COX2​] is left.
  2. (b) C is a solid, so it stays out. Both gases appear: CO on top with exponent 2, COX2​ below.
  3. Check: every gas appears and every solid is missing.
Answer: (a) [COX2​] (b) [COX2​][CO]2​
In a saturated solution of PbIX2​, [PbX2+]=1×10−3 and [IX−]=2×10−3 mol/L. Calculate the solubility product.
Show the solution
  1. Equation: PbIX2​(s)​PbX2+(aq)+2IX−(aq); the solid is left out.
  2. Expression: [PbX2+]×[IX−]2, since the coefficient of iodide is 2.
  3. Substitute: (1×10−3)×(2×10−3)2.
  4. (2×10−3)2=4×10−6, then 1×10−3×4×10−6=4×10−9.
Answer: 4×10−9
Common mistakes
  • Flipping the fraction by putting reactants on top. Products always go on top.
  • Multiplying by the coefficient instead of using it as an exponent, such as 2[NHX3​] instead of [NHX3​]2.
  • Including pure solids or pure liquids in the expression. They never appear.
  • Putting the solid salt in the denominator of the solubility product. A solid never appears.
Summary
  • K = products over reactants, each concentration raised to its coefficient.
  • Gases and dissolved ions appear; pure solids and liquids do not.
  • Write the expression from the balanced equation.
  • The solubility product is the product of the ion concentrations raised to their coefficients.

Calculating and Manipulating K

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Calculate K from equilibrium concentrations
  • Use an initial, change, equilibrium table
  • Adjust K when you reverse an equation, multiply its coefficients or add two equations

Direct calculation

If you are given equilibrium concentrations, write the expression and substitute. Use equilibrium concentrations, never the starting ones.

In exam problems we treat K as a number without units.

If K and all concentrations but one are given, substitute and solve for the unknown. For example, for 2A​B with K=5 and [A]=0.4: [B]=5×0.42=0.8 mol/L.

The initial, change, equilibrium table

If you are given starting concentrations and one concentration at equilibrium, use a three-row table: initial, change, equilibrium.

The change in each substance follows its coefficient: if a substance with coefficient 1 changes by x, one with coefficient 2 changes by 2x. Reactants decrease and products increase.

  • Equilibrium = initial + change.
  • Reactants decrease (negative change); products increase (positive change).
  • After completing the table, substitute the equilibrium row into the expression.

Manipulating K

When we change the form of the equation, K changes by simple rules, at the same temperature:

  • Reverse the equation: new K=K1​.
  • Multiply the coefficients by n: new K = K raised to the power n.
  • Add two equations: new K = the product of their K values.

Laws and rules

Reversing the equationKnew​=K1​The new constant belongs to the reversed equation at the same temperature.
Multiplying coefficients by nKnew​=Knn may be a fraction, such as one half when coefficients are divided by two.
Adding two equationsKtotal​=K1​×K2​Add the equations and cancel substances that appear on both sides.

Worked examples

At equilibrium for NX2​(g)+3HX2​(g)​2NHX3​(g) the concentrations in mol/L are [NX2​]=0.5, [HX2​]=2 and [NHX3​]=4. Calculate K.
Show the solution
  1. Expression: [NX2​][HX2​]3[NHX3​]2​.
  2. Top: 42=16.
  3. Bottom: 0.5×23=0.5×8=4.
  4. K=416​=4.
Answer: K=4
1.0 mol/L of HX2​ and 1.0 mol/L of IX2​ are placed in a vessel and form HI by HX2​+IX2​​2HI. At equilibrium [HI]=1.6 mol/L. Calculate K.
Show the solution
  1. Initial: [HX2​]=1.0, [IX2​]=1.0, [HI]=0.
  2. HI rose from 0 to 1.6 and its coefficient is 2, so 2x=1.6 and x=0.8.
  3. Reactants have coefficient 1 so each falls by 0.8: [HX2​]=1.0−0.8=0.2 and [IX2​]=0.2.
  4. K=[HX2​][IX2​][HI]2​=0.2×0.21.62​.
  5. 0.042.56​=64.
Answer: K=64
K=4 for NX2​+3HX2​​2NHX3​ at a given temperature. Find K at the same temperature for (a) 2NHX3​​NX2​+3HX2​ and (b) 21​NX2​+23​HX2​​NHX3​.
Show the solution
  1. (a) The equation is exactly reversed, so new K=41​=0.25.
  2. (b) All coefficients are divided by 2, i.e. multiplied by 21​, so new K=421​=4​=2.
  3. Check (b): doubling this equation gives the original, and 22=4 as given.
Answer: (a) 0.25 (b) 2
Common mistakes
  • Substituting starting concentrations instead of equilibrium concentrations.
  • Forgetting that change follows the coefficient: using x for HI instead of 2x.
  • Multiplying K by the number when the equation is multiplied, instead of raising K to that power.
  • Keeping K the same when the equation is reversed, instead of taking its reciprocal.
Summary
  • Substitute equilibrium concentrations only.
  • In the table, the change follows the coefficients.
  • Reversing the equation takes the reciprocal of K.
  • Multiplying coefficients by n raises K to the power n; adding equations multiplies the K values.

Meaning of K and the Reaction Quotient

What you will learn
  • Interpret large and small values of K
  • Calculate the reaction quotient from current concentrations
  • Compare it with K to find the direction the reaction will go

What does the value of K tell us?

Products are on top and reactants below, so K tells us where equilibrium settles: toward products or toward reactants.

  • Very large K (much greater than 1): products dominate and the reaction almost goes to completion.
  • Very small K (much less than 1): reactants dominate and hardly any reaction occurs.
  • K near 1: sizeable amounts of both sides.

K does not tell us the speed

K tells us the position of equilibrium, not how fast it is reached; K can be huge and the reaction still very slow.

The reaction quotient

We write the same expression but with the concentrations present in the mixture right now, whatever they are. The result is the reaction quotient, Q.

At equilibrium Q equals K. If the mixture is not at equilibrium, comparing Q with K tells us which way it will go.

  • Q less than K: too few products, so the reaction goes forward.
  • Q equals K: the mixture is at equilibrium.
  • Q greater than K: too many products, so the reaction goes in reverse.

Sparingly soluble salts

Same idea: if the product of the ion concentrations (current values) is greater than the solubility product, the salt precipitates; if it is smaller, no precipitate forms.

Laws and rules

Reaction quotientQ=[reactants]coefficients[products]coefficients​Calculated from the present concentrations in mol/L, not from equilibrium concentrations.

Worked examples

Reaction A has K=105 and reaction B has K=10−5. Which one almost goes to completion? Can we tell which is faster?
Show the solution
  1. K of A is much greater than 1, so products dominate at equilibrium.
  2. K of B is much less than 1, so reactants dominate and little product forms.
  3. K describes only the position of equilibrium, so it says nothing about speed.
Answer: Reaction A almost goes to completion; K cannot tell us which is faster.
K=64 for HX2​+IX2​​2HI. A mixture has [HX2​]=0.5, [IX2​]=0.5 and [HI]=2 mol/L. Which way does the reaction go?
Show the solution
  1. Q=[HX2​][IX2​][HI]2​=0.5×0.522​.
  2. 0.254​=16.
  3. Compare: 16 is less than 64, so there are too few products.
  4. The reaction goes forward and forms more product.
Answer: Forward (toward products), because Q=16 is less than K=64.
K=4 for NX2​OX4​​2NOX2​. A mixture has [NOX2​]=4 and [NX2​OX4​]=2 mol/L. Which way does the reaction go, and what happens to [NOX2​]?
Show the solution
  1. Q=[NX2​OX4​][NOX2​]2​=242​.
  2. 216​=8.
  3. Compare: 8 is greater than 4, so there are too many products.
  4. The reaction goes in reverse until Q falls from 8 to 4, so [NOX2​] decreases.
Answer: In reverse (toward reactants), and [NOX2​] decreases.
Common mistakes
  • Calculating Q with equilibrium concentrations. Use the concentrations at that moment.
  • Reversing the comparison. A smaller Q means too few products, so the reaction goes forward.
  • Thinking a large K means a fast reaction. K gives position, not speed.
  • Thinking K changes when concentrations change. K changes only with temperature.
Summary
  • Large K: products dominate. Small K: reactants dominate.
  • K gives the position of equilibrium, not its speed.
  • Q is the same expression with current concentrations.
  • Q < K: forward. Q = K: equilibrium. Q > K: reverse.

Le Chatelier's Principle and Concentration

What you will learn
  • State Le Chatelier's principle and apply it
  • Predict the shift when a substance is added or removed
  • Know that K does not change when concentration changes

The idea

If we disturb a system at equilibrium with a change (in concentration, pressure or temperature), the system moves in the direction that reduces the effect of that change, until it reaches a new equilibrium.

A shift to the right means more products form; a shift to the left means more reactants form.

Changing concentration

If we add a substance, the system uses some of it up. If we remove a substance, the system makes some more.

  • Add a reactant: shifts right.
  • Remove a product: shifts right.
  • Add a product: shifts left.
  • Remove a reactant: shifts left.

Why K does not change

After the addition, Q differs from K, so the system moves until Q returns to the same value as K. Concentrations change, but the ratio between them returns to what it was.

K changes only with temperature.

  • Adding a pure solid or pure liquid has no effect, since its concentration is fixed and it is not in the expression.

Worked examples

In the equilibrium NX2​(g)+3HX2​(g)​2NHX3​(g), HX2​ is added. What happens to the concentrations of NX2​ and NHX3​ and to K?
Show the solution
  1. Adding HX2​ (a reactant) makes the system use some up, so equilibrium shifts right.
  2. HX2​ reacts with NX2​, so [NX2​] falls.
  3. And [NHX3​] rises.
  4. K does not change because the temperature did not change.
Answer: [NX2​] falls, [NHX3​] rises, K is unchanged.
In CO(g)+2HX2​(g)​CHX3​OH(g) the methanol is continually removed from the vessel. How does this affect the amount of methanol produced?
Show the solution
  1. Removing the product lowers its concentration.
  2. The system makes up the loss, so equilibrium shifts right.
  3. New methanol keeps forming.
Answer: More methanol is produced.
K=4 for NX2​+3HX2​​2NHX3​. At equilibrium [NX2​]=0.5, [HX2​]=2 and [NHX3​]=4 mol/L. HX2​ is added so that its concentration suddenly rises to 4. Use Q to show which way the system moves.
Show the solution
  1. Q after the addition =0.5×4342​.
  2. 43=64, so the bottom is 0.5×64=32 and the top is 16.
  3. Q=3216​=0.5.
  4. 0.5 is less than K=4, so the reaction goes forward, to the right, which agrees with Le Chatelier's principle.
Answer: Q=0.5 is less than 4, so the system shifts right.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking that adding a substance pushes the reaction toward its own side. The system uses it up, so the shift is away from it.
  • Thinking K changes with concentration. K changes only with temperature.
  • Thinking the added substance returns to its old concentration. It stays higher than before the addition but lower than right after adding.
Summary
  • The system opposes the change: it uses what we add and replaces what we remove.
  • Add reactant or remove product: right. Add product or remove reactant: left.
  • Changing concentration does not change K.
  • Changing the amount of a pure solid or liquid has no effect.

Effect of Pressure, Volume and Temperature

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Predict the shift for a volume or pressure change by counting gas moles
  • Predict the effect of heating or cooling from whether the reaction is endothermic or exothermic
  • Know that only temperature changes the value of K

Pressure and volume

Pressure affects equilibrium only if gases are involved and the number of gas moles differs between the two sides. Count gas moles only, not solids or liquids.

Reducing the volume raises the pressure, so equilibrium shifts toward the side with fewer gas moles. Increasing the volume lowers the pressure, so it shifts toward the side with more gas moles.

  • Equal gas moles on both sides: no shift.
  • Adding an inert gas at constant volume: no shift, since the concentrations of the reacting substances have not changed.

Effect of temperature

Treat heat as if it were a substance: in an exothermic reaction it is a product, and in an endothermic reaction it is a reactant.

Heating shifts equilibrium toward the direction that absorbs heat (endothermic). Cooling shifts it toward the direction that releases heat (exothermic).

  • Exothermic reaction: heating shifts toward reactants and makes K smaller; cooling shifts toward products and makes K larger.
  • Endothermic reaction: heating shifts toward products and makes K larger; cooling does the opposite.

Temperature changes K

Temperature is the only factor that changes the value of K itself. So if you are given K at two temperatures, compare them: if K grows when heated, the reaction is endothermic; if it shrinks, the reaction is exothermic.

Worked examples

Which way does equilibrium shift when the container volume is reduced in each of: (a) NX2​OX4​(g)​2NOX2​(g) (b) HX2​(g)+IX2​(g)​2HI(g) (c) CaCOX3​(s)​CaO(s)+COX2​(g)?
Show the solution
  1. Reducing the volume raises the pressure, so equilibrium shifts toward the side with fewer gas moles.
  2. (a) Left has 1 mol of gas and right has 2, so the shift is to the left.
  3. (b) Left 2 mol and right 2 mol, so no shift.
  4. (c) Count gas only: left 0 and right 1, so the shift is to the left.
Answer: (a) left. (b) no shift. (c) left.
The reaction NX2​(g)+3HX2​(g)​2NHX3​(g) is exothermic. What happens to the amount of ammonia and to K when the temperature is raised? And when it is lowered?
Show the solution
  1. The reaction is exothermic, so heat is a product: NX2​+3HX2​​2NHX3​ + heat.
  2. Heating shifts equilibrium toward the endothermic direction, which is the reverse (to the left).
  3. So the amount of ammonia falls and K gets smaller, because the product in the numerator has decreased.
  4. Cooling does the opposite: a shift right, more ammonia and a larger K.
Answer: Heating: less ammonia and smaller K. Cooling: more ammonia and larger K.
For NX2​OX4​​2NOX2​, K=0.1 at one temperature and K=2 at a higher temperature. Is the forward reaction endothermic or exothermic? What happens to [NOX2​] when the temperature is lowered?
Show the solution
  1. K rose from 0.1 to 2 on heating, so heating shifted equilibrium toward the products.
  2. Heating shifts toward the endothermic direction, so the forward reaction is endothermic.
  3. Cooling shifts toward the exothermic direction, which is the reverse.
  4. So [NOX2​] falls and K gets smaller.
Answer: The forward reaction is endothermic, and [NOX2​] falls when cooled.
Common mistakes
  • Counting all substances, including solids and liquids, when comparing moles. Count gas only.
  • Thinking higher pressure shifts toward the side with more moles. It shifts toward fewer.
  • Thinking pressure or concentration changes K. Only temperature does.
  • Mixing up the effect of heat: heating shifts toward the direction that absorbs heat (endothermic), not the one that releases it.
  • Thinking that adding an inert gas at constant volume shifts the equilibrium. The concentrations of the reacting substances do not change, so there is no shift.
Summary
  • Higher pressure (or smaller volume) shifts toward fewer gas moles.
  • If gas moles are equal, or there are no gases, pressure has no effect.
  • Heating shifts toward the endothermic direction; cooling toward the exothermic direction.
  • Only temperature changes the value of K.
