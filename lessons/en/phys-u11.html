Angular speed and angular acceleration

What you will learn
  • Tell linear speed from angular speed.
  • Connect angular speed to frequency.
  • Compute angular acceleration and the angle turned.

How do we describe rotation?

When a rigid body turns about a fixed axis, every point sweeps out an angle in the same time. We measure angles in radians, and the angle turned each second is the angular speed ω in rad/s.

One full turn is 2π radians, about 6.28 rad.

Linear versus angular

Every point of a disc has the same angular speed because they finish a turn together. But a point far from the axis draws a bigger circle in the same time, so it moves faster along its path: its linear speed v is larger.

That is why v=ωr: linear speed grows with distance from the axis while the angular speed stays the same.

Frequency and angular acceleration

If frequency is the number of turns per second, each turn gives 2π radians, so ω=2πf.

If the angular speed changes there is an angular acceleration α in rad/s2. It is like ordinary acceleration, but for rotation.

Laws and rules

Linear speedv=ωrv: linear speed (m/s), ω: angular speed (rad/s), r: distance from the axis (m).
Angular speedω=tθ​θ: angle turned (rad), t in s. Since one full turn takes the period T, ω=T2π​.
Angular speed and frequencyω=2πff in hertz (turns/s), ω in rad/s.
Angular accelerationα=tΔω​α: angular acceleration (rad/s2), Δω: change in angular speed (rad/s), t: time (s).
Angle from restθ=21​αt2θ: angle turned (rad) under constant angular acceleration starting from rest.

Worked examples

A wheel turns 3 times every second. What is its angular speed?
Show the solution
  1. Frequency f=3 Hz.
  2. ω=2π×3
  3. ω=6π≈18.8 rad/s.
Answer: 6π≈18.8rad/s
A disc has angular speed 6 rad/s and radius 0.5 m. Find the linear speed of a point on its rim and of a point 0.25 m from the centre.
Show the solution
  1. Rim: v=6×0.5=3 m/s.
  2. Nearer point: v=6×0.25=1.5 m/s.
  3. Check: half the distance, half the speed.
Answer: 3m/s and 1.5m/s
A flywheel starts from rest and reaches 20 rad/s after 5 s with constant angular acceleration. Its radius is 0.5 m. Find the angular acceleration, the angle turned, and the rim's linear speed at 5 s.
Show the solution
  1. α=520−0​=4 rad/s2.
  2. θ=21​×4×52=50 rad.
  3. Check: average angular speed 10, and 10×5=50.
  4. v=20×0.5=10 m/s.
Answer: 4rad/s2, 50rad, 10m/s
Common mistakes
  • Thinking linear speed is the same for all points of a disc. What is shared is ω; v grows with distance from the axis.
  • Forgetting that one turn is 2π radians, and multiplying frequency by π only.
  • Mixing angular acceleration (rad/s2) with linear acceleration (m/s2); the unit gives the mistake away.
Summary
  • Angular speed is the angle turned per second and is the same for every point of a rigid body.
  • Linear speed equals angular speed times distance from the axis.
  • Angular speed equals 2π times frequency.
  • Angular acceleration is the change in angular speed per second.

Torque and the lever arm

What you will learn
  • Define torque and lever arm.
  • Compute torque for perpendicular and slanted forces.
  • Find the net torque of several torques.

A force pushes, a torque turns

A force alone does not guarantee rotation; where it acts matters. Torque measures how strongly a force turns an object about an axis.

A bigger force, or one farther from the axis, gives more torque. That is why we push a door at the edge far from the hinge; pushing near the hinge needs a much bigger force.

The lever arm

The lever arm is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of action of the force. If the force is perpendicular to the arm, it is simply the distance from the axis to where the force acts.

If the force makes an angle θ with the arm, only its perpendicular component turns the object: Fsinθ. A force along the arm (θ=0) gives zero torque.

Net torque

A torque that turns anticlockwise is counted positive; a clockwise one is negative. The net torque is their sum, and it tells us which way the object turns.

Laws and rules

Torqueτ=F×dτ: torque (N⋅m), F: force (N), d: lever arm (m), force perpendicular to the arm.
Torque at an angleτ=Frsinθr: distance from the axis (m), θ: angle between the force and the arm.
Net torqueτnet​=∑τAnticlockwise positive, clockwise negative.

Worked examples

A force of 20 N acts perpendicular to a wrench at 0.3 m from the bolt's axis. What is the torque?
Show the solution
  1. The force is perpendicular, so the lever arm is 0.3 m.
  2. τ=20×0.3=6 N⋅m.
Answer: 6N⋅m
A student pushes a door with 15 N at 0.8 m from the hinge, the force making 30∘ with the plane of the door. What is the torque?
Show the solution
  1. τ=15×0.8×sin30∘
  2. sin30∘=0.5, so τ=12×0.5
  3. τ=6 N⋅m.
  4. A perpendicular push would give 12 N⋅m, twice as much.
Answer: 6N⋅m
On a wheel of radius 0.5 m, a tangential 8 N force acts anticlockwise at the rim, and a tangential 12 N force acts clockwise at 0.25 m from the axis. Find the net torque and its direction.
Show the solution
  1. First torque =8×0.5=4 N⋅m (positive).
  2. Second torque =12×0.25=3 N⋅m (negative).
  3. Net =4−3=1 N⋅m.
  4. Positive, so the wheel turns anticlockwise.
Answer: 1N⋅m anticlockwise
Common mistakes
  • Using the slanted distance instead of the perpendicular arm, or forgetting sinθ when the force is not perpendicular.
  • Believing a bigger force always means a bigger torque; a force near the axis is weaker in effect than a smaller force far from it.
  • Adding opposite torques as if all were positive; the clockwise one must be subtracted.
Summary
  • Torque is force times lever arm.
  • The lever arm is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of action of the force.
  • A slanted force counts only through its perpendicular part: distance times sine of the angle.
  • Net torque is the signed sum, and its sign gives the direction of turning.

Equilibrium and centre of mass

What you will learn
  • State and apply the two conditions for equilibrium.
  • Solve lever and uniform-plank problems.
  • Find a centre of mass and explain stability.

When is an object in equilibrium?

An object in equilibrium neither moves nor rotates. It needs two conditions: the net force is zero so it does not move, and the net torque about any point is zero so it does not turn.

For a lever or seesaw we take the pivot as the axis. The pivot force then has zero lever arm and drops out, leaving: the clockwise torques equal the anticlockwise torques.

Uniform plank and centre of mass

The centre of mass is the point where we can imagine all the mass gathered. In a uniform plank it lies at the middle, so we place the plank's whole weight there.

For several masses, the centre of mass is the sum of (mass × position) divided by the total mass, so it sits closer to the bigger mass.

Stability

An object stays stable as long as the vertical line through its centre of mass falls inside its base. A wide base and a low centre of mass make it harder to tip.

Once it leans so far that this line leaves the base, the weight turns the object over.

Laws and rules

Conditions for equilibrium∑F=0,∑τ=0The first stops motion, the second stops rotation.
Balanced leverF1​d1​=F2​d2​Distances d are measured from the pivot (m), forces in N.
Centre of massxcm​=∑m∑mx​Masses in kg, positions in m from one common origin.

Worked examples

A balanced seesaw has a child of weight 400 N at 1.5 m from the pivot. At what distance on the other side must a child of weight 300 N sit?
Show the solution
  1. First child's torque =400×1.5=600 N⋅m.
  2. Balance: 300×d=600.
  3. d=300600​=2 m.
  4. Check: the lighter child sits farther out, as expected.
Answer: 2m
A 2 kg mass is at position 0 and a 6 kg mass at 4 m. Where is the centre of mass?
Show the solution
  1. ∑mx=2×0+6×4=24.
  2. Total mass =2+6=8 kg.
  3. xcm​=824​=3 m.
  4. Check: closer to the bigger mass (at 4 m), as it should be.
Answer: 3m from the first mass
A uniform plank 4 m long weighing 80 N rests on a pivot 0.5 m from its left end. What weight at the left end balances it? What is the pivot force?
Show the solution
  1. The plank's centre is 2 m from the left end, so 2−0.5=1.5 m from the pivot.
  2. Torque of the plank's weight =80×1.5=120 N⋅m.
  3. The new weight's torque is W×0.5 and must equal 120, so W=240 N.
  4. Pivot force equals the total weight: 240+80=320 N (first condition).
Answer: 240N; pivot force 320N
Common mistakes
  • Measuring distances from the end of the lever instead of from the pivot.
  • Forgetting the plank's own weight, or putting it at an end instead of the middle.
  • Using only the torque condition and ignoring the force condition when finding the pivot force.
  • Thinking an object tips just because it leans; it tips only when the weight line leaves the base.
Summary
  • Equilibrium needs zero net force and zero net torque.
  • A balanced lever has equal torques on its two sides about the pivot.
  • The weight of a uniform plank acts at its middle.
  • An object is stable while the line down from its centre of mass lies inside its base.

Rotational inertia and angular momentum

What you will learn
  • Link torque, angular acceleration and moment of inertia.
  • Compute the moment of inertia of a point mass.
  • Apply conservation of angular momentum.

Newton's second law for rotation

In straight-line motion a force gives an acceleration set by the mass. In rotation a torque gives an angular acceleration set by the moment of inertia: torque = moment of inertia × angular acceleration.

Moment of inertia

It is the resistance of a body to a change in its rotation. It depends not only on the mass but on how the mass is spread: the farther the mass from the axis, the larger the moment of inertia.

For a point mass, I=mr2. Because the distance is squared, doubling the distance makes the resistance four times bigger.

Angular momentum and its conservation

Angular momentum is moment of inertia times angular speed. With no external torque it stays constant.

That is why a skater who pulls her arms in lowers her moment of inertia and spins faster, and slows again when she opens them.

Laws and rules

Torque and angular accelerationτ=Iατ in N⋅m, I (moment of inertia) in kg⋅m2, α in rad/s2.
Point massI=mr2m in kg, r = distance from the axis in m.
Angular momentumL=IωUnit: kg⋅m2/s.
ConservationI1​ω1​=I2​ω2​When there is no external torque.

Worked examples

A net torque of 12 N⋅m acts on a wheel with moment of inertia 3 kg⋅m2. What is its angular acceleration?
Show the solution
  1. α=Iτ​
  2. α=312​=4 rad/s2.
Answer: 4rad/s2
A 2 kg ball is fixed on a light rod 0.5 m from the axis. Find its moment of inertia and the torque needed for an angular acceleration of 6 rad/s2.
Show the solution
  1. I=2×0.52=2×0.25=0.5 kg⋅m2.
  2. τ=0.5×6=3 N⋅m.
Answer: 0.5kg⋅m2 and 3N⋅m
A skater spins at 3 rad/s with moment of inertia 4 kg⋅m2. She pulls her arms in and the moment of inertia becomes 2 kg⋅m2. What is her new angular speed?
Show the solution
  1. Initial angular momentum =4×3=12.
  2. Conservation: 2×ω2​=12.
  3. ω2​=6 rad/s.
  4. Check: halving I doubled ω.
Answer: 6rad/s
Common mistakes
  • Using r instead of r2 in the moment of inertia.
  • Thinking moment of inertia depends on mass only; how the mass is spread about the axis matters most.
  • Thinking that pulling the arms in changes the angular momentum; it is the momentum that stays constant, while the speed changes.
Summary
  • Torque equals moment of inertia times angular acceleration.
  • For a point mass, moment of inertia is mass times distance squared.
  • Angular momentum equals moment of inertia times angular speed.
  • With no external torque, angular momentum stays constant.

Density and pressure in liquids

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Compute density and pressure from their definitions.
  • Explain how pressure changes with area and depth.
  • Find liquid pressure and total pressure.

Density

Density is mass per unit volume: ρ=Vm​. Water has density 1000 kg/m3, iron about 7800, and wood less.

Density belongs to the material, not to the amount: a big piece and a small piece of iron have the same density.

Pressure

Pressure is the perpendicular force on each unit of area: P=AF​, measured in pascals (N/m2).

That is why a nail sinks in by its sharp tip (small area) but not by its wide head under the same force. Less contact area means more pressure; more area means less.

Remember: 1 m2 =10000 cm2, so convert the area to m2 before dividing to get pascals.

Pressure inside a liquid

A liquid presses in all directions, and the pressure grows with depth because a taller column of liquid lies above: P=ρgh.

Only the vertical depth matters, not the shape of the vessel, its width, or how much liquid it holds. Points at the same depth in the same liquid have the same pressure in a narrow or a wide vessel.

With an open surface the air also presses, so total pressure = atmospheric pressure +ρgh. Atmospheric pressure at sea level is about 100000 Pa (exactly 101325).

Laws and rules

Densityρ=Vm​ρ in kg/m3, m in kg, V in m3.
PressureP=AF​P in pascals, F perpendicular force in N, A in m2.
Liquid pressureP=ρghg=10 m/s2, h = depth below the surface (m).
Total pressurePtotal​=Patm​+ρghIn pascals.

Worked examples

A metal piece has mass 2 kg and volume 0.00025 m3. What is its density?
Show the solution
  1. ρ=Vm​=0.000252​
  2. 0.00025=2.5×10−4, so ρ=2.5×10−42​=0.8×104
  3. ρ=8000 kg/m3.
Answer: 8000kg/m3
A crate weighing 600 N rests on an area of 0.02 m2, then on an area of 0.06 m2. Compare the pressures.
Show the solution
  1. First: 0.02600​=30000 Pa.
  2. Second: 0.06600​=10000 Pa.
  3. Three times the area gives a third of the pressure.
Answer: 30000Pa then 10000Pa
A diver is 5 m deep in water (ρ=1000 kg/m3, g=10 m/s2). What is the water pressure on the diver, and the total pressure if atmospheric pressure is 100000 Pa?
Show the solution
  1. Water pressure =1000×10×5=50000 Pa.
  2. Total =100000+50000=150000 Pa.
  3. In a wider vessel at the same depth the pressure would be the same.
Answer: 50000Pa; total 150000Pa
Common mistakes
  • Thinking pressure depends on the vessel's shape or the amount of liquid; only the vertical depth counts.
  • Dividing force by a wrong area (cm2 instead of m2) without converting.
  • Forgetting atmospheric pressure when total pressure is asked, or adding it when only liquid pressure is asked.
  • Confusing density with mass; a bigger object is not necessarily denser.
Summary
  • Density is mass divided by volume.
  • Pressure is force divided by area; a smaller area gives more pressure.
  • Liquid pressure equals density times g times depth.
  • Only the vertical depth sets the pressure, not the vessel shape.
  • Total pressure is atmospheric pressure plus liquid pressure.

Pascal's principle and the hydraulic press

What you will learn
  • State Pascal's principle.
  • Compute forces in a hydraulic press.
  • Relate area to displacement and see that work is not increased.

Pascal's principle

If the pressure rises at any point of an enclosed liquid at rest, the rise is passed in full to every point of the liquid and to the walls of the container.

The reason is that a liquid hardly compresses, so it hands the push on in all directions.

The hydraulic press

Two cylinders are joined by liquid, one with a small area and one with a large area. A small force on the small piston makes a pressure that is passed in full to the large piston.

The pressure is equal at both pistons: A1​F1​​=A2​F2​​. So the big force appears on the big area: force is multiplied by the ratio of the areas. Only the ratio of the areas matters, so it is enough for both to be in the same unit (cm2 or m2), with no conversion.

But work is not increased

The liquid cannot be compressed, so the volume pushed down by the small piston equals the volume raised by the large one: A1​d1​=A2​d2​.

If the force is multiplied by twenty, the large piston moves twenty times less. Force × distance (work) is the same on both sides: we gain force, not energy.

Laws and rules

Pascal's principle in the pressA1​F1​​=A2​F2​​Forces in N, areas in m2; 1 is the small piston, 2 the large one.
DisplacementA1​d1​=A2​d2​d in m; the volume of liquid moved is constant.

Worked examples

A hydraulic press has a small piston of area 0.01 m2 and a large one of 0.2 m2. A force of 50 N acts on the small piston. What force acts on the large one?
Show the solution
  1. Area ratio =0.010.2​=20.
  2. F2​=50×20=1000 N.
Answer: 1000N
In the same press the small piston moves 0.4 m. How far does the large piston move? Compare the work on both sides.
Show the solution
  1. 0.01×0.4=0.2×d2​, so 0.004=0.2d2​.
  2. d2​=0.02 m.
  3. Work in =50×0.4=20 J; work out =1000×0.02=20 J.
Answer: 0.02m; the two works are equal
A hydraulic car lift has a small piston of area 0.03 m2 and a large one of 0.6 m2. What is the smallest force needed to lift a car of weight 12000 N? How far must the small piston go down to raise the car 0.1 m?
Show the solution
  1. Area ratio =0.030.6​=20.
  2. F1​=2012000​=600 N.
  3. The small piston moves twenty times farther: 0.1×20=2 m.
Answer: 600N; the small piston moves 2m
Common mistakes
  • Using the area ratio upside down; force grows where the area grows.
  • Thinking the press gives free energy; force grows and distance shrinks by the same factor.
  • Using the ratio of diameters instead of areas (area goes with the square of the diameter).
Summary
  • Pressure added to an enclosed liquid is passed in full in every direction.
  • In a hydraulic press the pressure is equal, so force is proportional to area.
  • Liquid volume is constant, so displacement is inversely proportional to area.
  • Force is multiplied but work is not.

Buoyancy and Archimedes' principle

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Explain the buoyant force with Archimedes' principle.
  • Decide when an object floats or sinks.
  • Compute apparent weight and, from it, density.

The buoyant force

A liquid pushes upward on an object immersed in it. That is the buoyant force. It arises because the pressure under the object is greater than on top, since the bottom is deeper.

Archimedes' principle

The buoyant force equals the weight of the liquid the object displaces: Fb​=ρliquid​gVsubmerged​.

Note that the density here is the liquid's, and the volume is only the submerged part, not the whole body unless it is fully under.

Float or sink?

If the object's density is less than the liquid's it floats; if more, it sinks; if equal, it hangs in place. A floating object has buoyancy balancing weight, so the submerged fraction of its volume =ρliquid​ρobject​​.

A ship floats because its average density (air inside included) is less than water's. Moving from a river to a denser sea, its buoyancy still equals its fixed weight, so the submerged volume shrinks and the ship rides a little higher.

Apparent weight

Hang the object from a spring balance in the liquid and the reading is less than its real weight, because buoyancy lifts it: apparent weight = real weight − buoyant force.

From the two readings we get the density: ρobject​=ρliquid​×buoyant forcereal weight​, valid when the object is fully under.

Laws and rules

Buoyant forceFb​=ρliquid​gVsub​Fb​ in N, density in kg/m3, g=10 m/s2, volume in m3.
Apparent weightWapp​=W−Fb​In newtons.
Floating objectVVsub​​=ρliquid​ρobject​​Fraction of the volume under the surface; buoyancy equals weight.

Worked examples

An object of volume 0.002 m3 is fully under water (1000 kg/m3). What is the buoyant force?
Show the solution
  1. Fb​=1000×10×0.002
  2. =20 N.
Answer: 20N
An object weighs 30 N in air and 20 N apparent weight in water. Find its density.
Show the solution
  1. Buoyant force =30−20=10 N.
  2. Volume: 10=1000×10×V, so V=0.001 m3.
  3. Mass =1030​=3 kg.
  4. Density =0.0013​=3000 kg/m3.
  5. Check with the ratio: 1000×1030​=3000.
Answer: 3000kg/m3
A wooden block of density 600 kg/m3 and volume 0.01 m3 floats on water. What part of its volume is under water?
Show the solution
  1. Weight =600×0.01×10=60 N.
  2. Floating: buoyancy = weight =60 N.
  3. 60=1000×10×Vsub​, so Vsub​=0.006 m3.
  4. Fraction =0.010.006​=0.6, which is 1000600​ as the rule says.
Answer: 0.006m3, i.e. 60% of the volume
Common mistakes
  • Using the object's density instead of the liquid's in the buoyancy formula.
  • Using the whole volume of a floating object instead of only the submerged part.
  • Thinking a heavy object always sinks; density decides, which is why a huge ship floats.
  • Forgetting that apparent weight is smaller than real weight and that the difference is the buoyant force.
  • Thinking a ship sinks deeper in denser water; buoyancy stays equal to the fixed weight, so it is the submerged volume that decreases.
Summary
  • Buoyant force equals the weight of the displaced liquid.
  • Buoyancy = liquid density ×g× submerged volume.
  • An object floats if its density is less than the liquid's.
  • Apparent weight = real weight − buoyant force.
  • Real and apparent weights give the object's volume and density.

Moving fluids and surface effects

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Compute flow rate and apply the continuity equation.
  • Apply Bernoulli's principle together with continuity.
  • Explain surface tension, adhesion and capillary action.

Flow rate and the continuity equation

Flow rate is the volume of liquid passing each second: Q=Av, which also equals tV​, in m3/s.

An incompressible liquid cannot pile up or vanish inside a pipe, so the flow rate is constant: A1​v1​=A2​v2​. In a narrow part the speed goes up.

Bernoulli's principle

When a fluid speeds up, its pressure drops. In a horizontal pipe, P+21​ρv2 is the same all along.

So in the narrow part the speed is greater and the pressure lower. This is how fast air over a wing lifts it, and how a spray bottle works.

Solve problems in two steps: continuity gives the new speed, then Bernoulli gives the pressure difference.

Surface tension

Liquid molecules attract each other, and a surface molecule is pulled only inward, so the surface acts like a stretched skin. That is why drops are round and a small insect can walk on water.

Adhesion and capillary action

Cohesion is attraction between molecules of the liquid; adhesion is attraction between the liquid and the container wall. If adhesion is stronger (water in glass) the liquid climbs the wall and its surface curves upward; if cohesion is stronger (mercury) it dips.

In a thin tube, water rises higher the narrower the tube. That is how water climbs in plant stems and paper towels.

Laws and rules

Flow rateQ=Av=tV​A in m2, v in m/s, Q in m3/s.
Continuity equationA1​v1​=A2​v2​For an incompressible fluid.
Bernoulli's principle (horizontal pipe)P1​+21​ρv12​=P2​+21​ρv22​P in Pa, ρ in kg/m3, v in m/s.

Worked examples

Water flows at 3 m/s in a pipe of cross-section 0.002 m2. What is the flow rate? How long to fill a 0.06 m3 tank?
Show the solution
  1. Q=0.002×3=0.006 m3/s.
  2. t=0.0060.06​=10 s.
Answer: 0.006m3/s and 10s
Water flows at 2 m/s in a pipe of area 0.04 m2, which narrows to 0.01 m2. What is the speed in the narrow part?
Show the solution
  1. 0.04×2=0.08 m3/s.
  2. 0.01×v2​=0.08.
  3. v2​=8 m/s.
  4. Check: the area became a quarter, so the speed is four times.
Answer: 8m/s
In the previous problem (horizontal pipe, water 1000 kg/m3), what is the pressure difference between the wide and narrow parts?
Show the solution
  1. Bernoulli: P1​−P2​=21​×1000×(82−22).
  2. 82−22=64−4=60.
  3. Difference =500×60=30000 Pa.
  4. The pressure in the wide (slow) part is higher.
Answer: 30000Pa, higher in the wide part
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the speed drops at a narrowing; it rises, because the flow rate is constant.
  • Thinking more speed means more pressure; Bernoulli says the opposite.
  • Forgetting to square the speed in Bernoulli, or forgetting the 21​.
  • Mixing up cohesion and adhesion: the first is liquid to liquid, the second is liquid to wall.
Summary
  • Flow rate equals area times speed and stays constant along a pipe.
  • A narrower pipe means a faster flow.
  • Faster flow means lower pressure (Bernoulli).
  • Surface tension makes a liquid surface behave like a stretched skin.
  • When adhesion beats cohesion, liquid rises in thin tubes.
