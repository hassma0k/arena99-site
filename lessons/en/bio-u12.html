Muscles: Types and How They Contract

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell skeletal, smooth and cardiac muscle apart
  • Decide which is voluntary and which is striated
  • Explain how sliding filaments shorten a muscle

Three types of muscle

The human body has three muscle types. They differ in how the fibre looks and in who controls it: you, or the body on its own.

Ask yourself two questions: do the fibres look striped under a microscope? Can you move them whenever you want?

  • Skeletal muscle: striated and voluntary. It attaches to bones and moves them; its fibres are long with many nuclei.
  • Smooth muscle: not striated and involuntary. It lines the stomach, intestines and blood vessels; its cells are spindle-shaped with one nucleus.
  • Cardiac muscle: striated and involuntary. It is found only in the heart; its fibres are branched and joined by intercalated discs that pass the signal quickly, so the heart beats as one unit.

Why "striated"?

The stripes are the regular arrangement of actin and myosin filaments inside the fibre, giving alternate light and dark bands. In smooth muscle the filaments are not arranged so regularly, so no stripes show.

The unit of contraction

A striated fibre is made of small repeating units called sarcomeres. Each sarcomere lies between two Z lines and holds thin actin filaments and thick myosin filaments.

When the nerve signal arrives, calcium ions are released from their stores inside the fibre and uncover binding sites on actin. Myosin heads then attach to actin and pull it toward the centre of the sarcomere in a rowing-like stroke.

Filaments slide, they do not shorten

During contraction actin slides over myosin and overlaps it more, so the Z lines move closer, the sarcomere shortens, and then the whole muscle shortens. Each filament keeps its length.

Myosin heads need ATP to let go of actin and grab again. When the signal stops, calcium returns to its stores and the muscle relaxes.

Laws and rules

Sarcomeres in a fibrefibre shortening=shortening of one sarcomere×number of sarcomeres in a rowSarcomeres are joined end to end, so their small shortenings add up to the shortening of the fibre.

Worked examples

Among cardiac, smooth and skeletal muscle, which is both striated and voluntary?
Show the solution
  1. We look for two features together: striated, and voluntary.
  2. Smooth is not striated, so cross it out.
  3. Cardiac is striated but involuntary, so cross it out.
  4. Skeletal is striated and we control it at will.
Answer: Skeletal muscle.
A relaxed sarcomere is 2.4μm long and shortens by one third when it contracts. How long is it afterwards?
Show the solution
  1. One third of the length =2.4÷3=0.8μm.
  2. Length after contraction =2.4−0.8=1.6μm.
  3. Check: 1.6+0.8=2.4.
Answer: 1.6μm.
A fibre has 1000 sarcomeres in a row and each shortens by 0.8μm. By how much does the fibre shorten? Do the actin filaments themselves shorten?
Show the solution
  1. Total shortening =1000×0.8=800μm.
  2. 1000μm=1mm, so the shortening is 0.8mm.
  3. The shortening comes from overlapping filaments; the filaments keep their length.
Answer: The fibre shortens 800μm (0.8mm); the filaments do not shorten.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the filaments shorten or break during contraction; they slide and overlap, and their length stays the same.
  • Mixing up "striated" and "voluntary"; cardiac muscle is striated but involuntary, so these are independent features.
  • Saying smooth muscle is voluntary; no smooth muscle in the body is voluntary.
  • Forgetting ATP; without it myosin heads cannot detach from actin.
Summary
  • Skeletal: striated, voluntary. Smooth: not striated, involuntary. Cardiac: striated, involuntary.
  • Contraction is actin sliding over myosin.
  • Filaments do not shorten; the sarcomere does.
  • Calcium starts contraction and ATP releases the myosin heads.

Joints and Their Types

What you will learn
  • Define a joint and its job
  • Separate fixed, slightly movable and freely movable joints
  • Link each movable joint to a body example

What is a joint?

A joint is where two or more bones meet. Some joints never move because their job is protection; others move freely because their job is movement.

Three groups by movement

Shape follows function: where protection is needed the bones are joined firmly, and where movement is needed, cartilage and fluid let the bones glide.

  • Fixed joints: no movement, such as the sutures between the skull bones that protect the brain.
  • Slightly movable joints: little movement, such as the joints between vertebrae, which are separated by cartilage.
  • Freely movable (synovial) joints: wide movement, with articular cartilage covering the bone ends, synovial fluid to reduce friction, and a joint capsule around them.

Types of freely movable joints

The type is decided by how many directions the joint allows. Know the movement and you know the joint.

  • Ball-and-socket: a ball in a cup, movement in all directions: shoulder and hip.
  • Hinge: movement in one plane, bending and straightening like a door: elbow and knee.
  • Pivot: rotation around an axis, as when you turn your head side to side: between the first two vertebrae of the neck.
  • Gliding: flat bones slide over each other: wrist bones.

Worked examples

The knee joint only bends and straightens the leg. What type is it?
Show the solution
  1. The movement is bending and straightening in one direction.
  2. A ball-and-socket moves in all directions, so it does not fit.
  3. A hinge moves like a door on its hinge.
Answer: A hinge joint.
Order these joints from least to most movement: shoulder, skull sutures, joints between vertebrae.
Show the solution
  1. Skull sutures are fixed: no movement.
  2. Vertebral joints are slightly movable: little movement.
  3. The shoulder is a ball-and-socket: the widest movement.
Answer: Skull sutures, then vertebral joints, then the shoulder.
Match each movement to its joint type: bending a finger, shaking the head to say "no", circling the hip.
Show the solution
  1. Bending a finger is bending and straightening in one plane: a hinge joint.
  2. Shaking the head is rotation around an axis between the first two vertebrae: a pivot joint.
  3. Circling the hip is movement in all directions: a ball-and-socket joint.
  4. Rotation alone does not decide the type: the shoulder also rotates, but it moves in all directions, not only around one axis.
Answer: Finger: hinge. Head: pivot. Hip: ball-and-socket.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up hinge and ball-and-socket; ask how many directions: one = hinge, all = ball-and-socket.
  • Thinking every joint moves; skull joints are fixed.
  • Confusing pivot (rotation around an axis) with ball-and-socket (all directions).
Summary
  • A joint is where bones meet.
  • Fixed, slightly movable, freely movable.
  • Hinge: elbow and knee. Ball-and-socket: shoulder and hip.
  • Pivot: neck (shaking the head). Gliding: wrist.

Ligaments, Tendons and Antagonistic Muscles

What you will learn
  • Tell a ligament from a tendon
  • Understand why muscles work in pairs
  • Name the contracting and relaxing muscle when the elbow bends or straightens

Ligament or tendon?

Both are tough fibrous connective tissue; the difference is what they join. A ligament joins bone to bone at a joint and holds it steady. A tendon joins muscle to bone and passes the muscle's force to the bone.

Memory trick: a tendon carries the muscle to the bone, a ligament ties bone to bone.

Do not confuse them with cartilage: cartilage covers the bone ends to reduce friction and does not join anything to anything.

A muscle pulls, it never pushes

A muscle can only contract, that is, pull the bone toward itself. It cannot push the bone back the other way.

So one muscle cannot move a joint both ways. Muscles work in antagonistic pairs: when one contracts, the other relaxes.

Example: bending and straightening the elbow

  • Bending: the biceps (front of the upper arm) contracts and the triceps relaxes, so the forearm moves toward the upper arm.
  • Straightening: the triceps (back) contracts and the biceps relaxes, so the arm straightens.

Worked examples

Which joins the biceps to the forearm bone: a ligament or a tendon?
Show the solution
  1. The two ends are a muscle and a bone.
  2. Tissue that joins muscle to bone is a tendon.
Answer: A tendon.
You lift a cup of water to your mouth and your elbow bends. What are the two muscles doing?
Show the solution
  1. Bending brings the forearm toward the upper arm.
  2. The biceps contracts to pull the forearm.
  3. The triceps relaxes to allow the movement.
Answer: Biceps contracted, triceps relaxed.
What is the effect of a cut tendon of the biceps at the forearm, and of a torn knee ligament? How do they differ?
Show the solution
  1. A tendon passes the muscle's force to the bone: once it is cut, the muscle contracts but does not pull the forearm, so the elbow cannot bend.
  2. A ligament steadies the two bones at a joint: once it is torn, the muscles can still pull, but the joint loses stability and the bones move unevenly.
  3. The difference: a tendon is about passing on force from a muscle, a ligament is about the stability of a joint.
Answer: A cut tendon stops force reaching the bone, so the elbow cannot bend; a torn ligament makes the joint unstable.
Common mistakes
  • Swapping the two names: a tendon goes with a muscle, a ligament joins two bones.
  • Thinking a muscle pushes bone; it only pulls, which is why an opposing pair is needed.
  • Thinking both muscles contract together for one movement; one contracts and the other relaxes.
  • Thinking cartilage is a ligament; cartilage covers the bone ends and reduces friction, it does not join the two bones.
Summary
  • Ligament: bone to bone.
  • Tendon: muscle to bone.
  • A muscle can only pull.
  • Antagonistic muscles: one contracts while the other relaxes, like biceps and triceps.

The Male Reproductive System

What you will learn
  • Name the parts of the male system and what each does
  • Follow a sperm from formation to exit
  • Describe the structure of a sperm cell

The testes: sperm factory

The testes are two oval organs inside the scrotum, outside the body cavity. The scrotum is about two to three degrees Celsius cooler than the body, which is needed for healthy sperm formation.

Inside each testis are coiled seminiferous tubules where sperm form. Between them, cells make testosterone, the hormone behind male characteristics.

The sperm's journey

  • Seminiferous tubules: sperm are made.
  • Epididymis: a tube coiled on the testis where sperm finish maturing and are stored.
  • Vas deferens: a muscular tube that carries sperm from the epididymis to the urethra.
  • Urethra: the shared passage for semen and urine.

Accessory glands and semen

The seminal vesicles add a sugar-rich fluid that feeds the sperm, the prostate adds an alkaline fluid that neutralises the acidity of the vagina, and Cowper's glands add a fluid that neutralises acidity in the urethra and eases the passage of semen.

Sperm mixed with these fluids is semen.

Structure of a sperm

A sperm is a small motile cell. The head holds the nucleus with 23 chromosomes and, at its tip, the acrosome, full of enzymes that digest the layers around the egg.

The midpiece is packed with mitochondria that supply energy, and the tail is a whip that drives the sperm along. Many mitochondria make sense: the tail beats constantly, which uses a lot of energy.

Worked examples

Why are the testes in the scrotum outside the body?
Show the solution
  1. Sperm formation needs a temperature below body temperature.
  2. The scrotum is about two to three degrees Celsius cooler.
Answer: It keeps the lower temperature that suits sperm formation.
Order the sperm's path: urethra, seminiferous tubules, vas deferens, epididymis.
Show the solution
  1. Made in the seminiferous tubules.
  2. Move to the epididymis to mature.
  3. Then the vas deferens.
  4. Then the urethra to leave.
Answer: Seminiferous tubules, epididymis, vas deferens, urethra.
A man's vas deferens is blocked but his testes are healthy. Are sperm still made? Does testosterone secretion continue? Do sperm reach the urethra?
Show the solution
  1. Sperm are made in the seminiferous tubules inside the testis, which is healthy, so production continues.
  2. Testosterone is released by cells of the testis into the blood, not through the vas deferens, so secretion continues.
  3. The vas deferens is the route from the epididymis to the urethra; when it is blocked, sperm cannot reach the urethra.
Answer: Sperm production and testosterone continue, but the sperm do not reach the urethra.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up where sperm are made (seminiferous tubules) with where they mature and are stored (epididymis).
  • Mixing up the acrosome (enzymes to enter the egg) with mitochondria (energy).
  • Forgetting that the urethra is shared by semen and urine.
  • Thinking testosterone leaves with the sperm through the vas deferens; the hormone goes into the blood.
Summary
  • The testes make sperm and testosterone.
  • Path: tubules, epididymis, vas deferens, urethra.
  • Accessory glands add the fluids that make semen.
  • Sperm: head with nucleus and acrosome, midpiece with mitochondria, tail.

Fertilization

What you will learn
  • Define fertilization and where it happens
  • Order its steps from meeting to zygote
  • Count chromosomes before and after fertilization

What is fertilization?

Fertilization is the fusion of a sperm nucleus with an egg nucleus to make one cell called a zygote. It usually happens in the upper third of the fallopian tube.

After ovulation the egg lives only about one day, so fertilization must happen in that short time.

Steps of fertilization

  • A sperm reaches the egg, which is surrounded by protective layers.
  • The acrosome releases enzymes that digest the layers and open a path.
  • The sperm membrane fuses with the egg membrane and its nucleus enters.
  • The egg surface changes and blocks a second sperm from entering.
  • The two nuclei fuse and the zygote forms.

Why chromosome number matters

Human body cells have 46 chromosomes; sex cells (gametes) have half, 23, because they are made by meiosis, which halves the number. If the sperm and egg each had 46 the zygote would have 92, and the number would double every generation.

The zygote carries one set from the father and one from the mother, then divides to become an embryo.

Laws and rules

Chromosomes in the zygotezygote chromosomes=23+23=46The sperm gives 23 chromosomes and the egg gives 23.

Worked examples

How many chromosomes does the zygote have if the sperm has 23 and the egg has 23?
Show the solution
  1. Add what each cell contributes.
  2. 23+23=46.
Answer: 46 chromosomes.
Order: nuclei fuse, protective layers digested, membranes fuse, sperm arrives.
Show the solution
  1. The sperm arrives first.
  2. Then the layers are digested by acrosome enzymes.
  3. Then the membranes fuse.
  4. Finally the nuclei fuse.
Answer: Arrive, digest, membranes fuse, nuclei fuse.
If two sperm entered the egg, how many chromosomes would the zygote carry? Why is that dangerous?
Show the solution
  1. The egg has 23 and two sperm give 23+23=46.
  2. Total 23+46=69, which is three sets of chromosomes instead of two.
  3. An abnormal number stops the embryo developing.
  4. That is why the egg surface changes to block a second sperm.
Answer: 69 chromosomes, an abnormal number.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking fertilization happens in the uterus; it usually happens in the upper third of the fallopian tube.
  • Thinking only one sperm makes the trip; millions start but only one fertilizes.
  • Mixing up fertilization (nuclei fusing) with implantation (the embryo attaching to the uterine wall later).
  • Mixing up ovulation (the egg leaving the ovary) with fertilization (the nuclei fusing).
Summary
  • Fertilization is sperm and egg fusing.
  • It happens in the upper third of the fallopian tube.
  • The acrosome digests the protective layers.
  • The zygote has 46 chromosomes: 23 from each parent.
