Neuron Structure and the Nervous System

What you will learn
  • Name the parts of a neuron and say what each does
  • Tell the central system from the peripheral one, and sympathetic from parasympathetic
  • Follow a reflex arc step by step
  • Match each brain region to its job

The neuron: a cell that carries a signal

A neuron is a nerve cell. Its job is to carry electrical signals quickly from one place in the body to another. A signal has a fixed route: it comes in at one end and leaves from the other.

  • Dendrites: short branched extensions that receive signals from other neurons.
  • Cell body: holds the nucleus and gathers incoming signals.
  • Axon: a long fibre that carries the signal away from the cell body.
  • Myelin sheath: a fatty insulating wrap around the axon in sections; between the sections are small gaps called nodes of Ranvier.
  • Axon terminals: release the neurotransmitter toward the next cell.

Why myelin?

The sheath is an insulator, so current cannot pass through it and the signal jumps from one node to the next. This jumping is much faster than continuous travel along a bare axon, and it saves energy.

When the sheath is damaged, as in multiple sclerosis, signals slow down or get scrambled.

Types of neurons and divisions of the nervous system

A sensory neuron carries a signal from a receptor to the central nervous system, an interneuron connects cells inside it, and a motor neuron carries the command from the central system to the effector (a muscle or gland).

The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord: it analyses information and decides. The peripheral system is the nerves outside them, carrying signals to and from the body.

The peripheral system has a voluntary part and an involuntary (autonomic) part; the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions are the two parts of the autonomic system, and they work in opposition: one speeds things up, the other slows them down.

  • Sympathetic division: prepares the body for danger («fight or flight»): speeds the heart, widens the pupils and airways, slows digestion.
  • Parasympathetic division: works at rest: slows the heart, narrows the pupils, boosts digestion.

Brain regions and the reflex

Each part of the brain has its own job.

A reflex is a fast, involuntary response. The signal does not go to the cerebrum first; it turns around in the spinal cord. That is why you pull away before you feel the pain.

  • Cerebrum: thinking, memory, sensation and voluntary movement.
  • Cerebellum: balance and coordination of movement.
  • Medulla oblongata: controls breathing and heartbeat.
  • Hypothalamus: body temperature, thirst and hunger; it is linked to the pituitary gland.

Laws and rules

The reflex arcreceptor→sensory neuron→spinal cord→motor neuron→effectorThe spinal cord is the relay centre (an interneuron); the effector is a muscle or a gland.

Worked examples

Sami touched a hot pot and pulled his hand away before he felt any pain. Order the signal path and explain the speed.
Show the solution
  1. A receptor in the skin detects the heat and fires a signal.
  2. A sensory neuron carries it to the spinal cord.
  3. In the spinal cord an interneuron passes it to a motor neuron without waiting for the brain.
  4. The motor neuron delivers the command to an arm muscle, which contracts.
  5. A copy of the signal also travels up to the cerebrum, but it takes longer, so we feel the pain after the withdrawal.
Answer: The route is short and runs through the spinal cord, so the withdrawal comes before the pain.
Before something frightening, Layla's heart pounded, her pupils widened and her mouth went dry. Which division is active?
Show the solution
  1. Faster heart and wider pupils prepare the body to face danger.
  2. That preparation is the work of the sympathetic division (fight or flight).
  3. The parasympathetic division does the opposite: it slows the heart, narrows the pupils and boosts digestion.
Answer: The sympathetic nervous system.
One man damages his cerebellum in an accident and another damages his medulla oblongata. What is disturbed in each?
Show the solution
  1. Memory and thinking are the work of the cerebrum, so they are not linked to the cerebellum.
  2. The cerebellum coordinates muscle movements and keeps the body balanced, so the first man has trouble with balance and walking.
  3. The medulla oblongata controls breathing and heartbeat, so damage to it is more dangerous because it threatens basic life functions.
Answer: First: disturbed balance and coordination. Second: disturbed breathing and heartbeat.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up the axon and the dendrites: dendrites receive; the axon sends the signal away from the cell body.
  • Thinking the sheath receives or creates signals: it only insulates and speeds up conduction.
  • Believing a reflex goes through the cerebrum first. The brain finds out after the response has happened.
  • Mixing sensory with motor: sensory goes to the centre; motor goes from the centre to the effector.
Summary
  • Dendrites receive, the axon sends, and myelin speeds the signal by letting it jump between nodes.
  • Central means brain and spinal cord. A motor neuron carries the command to a muscle or gland.
  • Sympathetic is for danger and action; parasympathetic is for rest and digestion.
  • A reflex runs through the spinal cord without waiting for the cerebrum.

The Nerve Impulse: Resting Potential, Action Potential and the Synapse

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Explain why the inside of a resting neuron is negative
  • Order the stages of an action potential and name the ion moving in each
  • Explain how a local anaesthetic stops a signal
  • Describe how a signal crosses a synapse

Resting potential

In a neuron that is not stimulated, the inside is negative compared with the outside by about −70mV. This is the resting potential, a stored charge ready to be used, like a charged battery.

Two things create it: the sodium-potassium pump pushes 3 NaX+ ions out and brings only 2 KX+ in, using ATP, and leak channels let more KX+ out than NaX+ in.

  • Resting ions: NaX+ is more concentrated outside the cell, and KX+ more concentrated inside.
  • The more positive charge leaves compared with what enters, the more negative the inside becomes.

The action potential, step by step

When a strong enough stimulus arrives, the potential rises to the threshold (about −55mV) and voltage-gated NaX+ channels open.

Depolarization: NaX+ rushes into the cell, so the inside becomes positive (about +30mV). It is called "de-polarization" because the negative difference disappears.

Repolarization: the NaX+ channels close and the KX+ channels open, so KX+ leaves and the inside turns negative again, dipping briefly below the resting potential before it settles. The pump then restores the ions to their places.

All-or-nothing rule: if the stimulus does not reach the threshold, nothing happens; if it does, a complete action potential of the same size occurs.

What stops a signal?

A local anaesthetic (such as the dentist's injection) blocks NaX+ channels. Without NaX+ entering there is no depolarization, so no action potential forms, and the pain signal never reaches the brain.

The synapse

A synapse is where the end of one neuron meets the next cell, with a tiny gap between them. The electrical signal does not jump across the gap; it is passed on chemically.

  • The action potential reaches the axon terminal and calcium ions enter.
  • Vesicles of neurotransmitter (such as acetylcholine) fuse with the membrane and empty into the gap.
  • The transmitter binds to receptors on the next cell, its channels open, and a new signal starts.
  • The transmitter is then broken down or taken back up, ending the signal. A synapse works in one direction only.

Laws and rules

Stages of the action potentialrest→depolarization→repolarization→restDepolarization is NaX+ entering; repolarization is KX+ leaving.
The pump in each cycle3 Na out,2 K inThe pump needs ATP energy because it moves ions against their concentration gradients.

Worked examples

A neuron has a resting potential of −70mV and a threshold of −55mV. A weak stimulus raises the potential to −60mV; then a stronger stimulus raises it to −50mV. What happens each time?
Show the solution
  1. −60 is below the threshold −55 (more negative), so the NaX+ channels do not open and the potential returns to rest.
  2. −50 is above the threshold, so the NaX+ channels open and sodium rushes in: depolarization begins.
  3. The potential reaches about +30mV and then returns: a complete action potential, which would not be bigger with a stronger stimulus (all-or-nothing).
Answer: The first time no action potential forms; the second time a complete action potential forms.
A dentist injects a local anaesthetic that blocks sodium channels in a tooth nerve. Why does the patient feel no pain?
Show the solution
  1. Pain is a signal travelling along the nerve as a chain of action potentials.
  2. An action potential begins with NaX+ entering through its channels, and the channels are now blocked.
  3. No depolarization happens, so no action potential forms and the signal stops before it reaches the brain.
Answer: Blocking the sodium channels prevents the action potential from forming.
A pump in a neuron completes 10 full cycles. How many NaX+ ions leave, how many KX+ ions enter, and what is the net positive charge moved out?
Show the solution
  1. Sodium: 10×3=30 ions leave.
  2. Potassium: 10×2=20 ions enter.
  3. Net positive charge out: 30−20=10 charges.
  4. More positive charge leaving than entering makes the inside more negative.
Answer: 30 sodium out, 20 potassium in, a net of 10 positive charges out, so the inside becomes more negative.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the resting potential is zero. The inside is about −70mV.
  • Thinking depolarization means an ion leaves. It is sodium entering that removes the negative difference.
  • Thinking the pump makes the rise of the action potential. The rise comes from sodium channels; the pump restores the balance afterwards.
  • Thinking the signal jumps electrically across the synapse. It crosses as a neurotransmitter.
Summary
  • At rest the inside is negative, NaX+ is mostly outside and KX+ inside, and the pump moves 3 out and 2 in.
  • Depolarization is NaX+ entering. Repolarization is KX+ leaving.
  • A local anaesthetic blocks sodium channels and so blocks the action potential.
  • At the synapse: calcium, then neurotransmitter, then receptor, in one direction.

Endocrine Glands and Hormones

What you will learn
  • Explain how hormones work and reach their target cells
  • Tell the hypothalamus from the pituitary
  • Link adrenaline, antidiuretic hormone and thyroxine to their jobs
  • Order the menstrual-cycle hormones

Hormones: messages in the blood

Endocrine glands release their hormones straight into the blood, so they reach the whole body, but they act only on target cells that carry the matching receptors. Hormones act more slowly than nerves, but for longer.

The hypothalamus and the pituitary gland

The hypothalamus is a part of the brain that monitors the blood and measures temperature and water; it links the nervous system to the glands. It releases hormones that regulate the anterior pituitary.

The pituitary gland is pea-sized and called the master gland because it controls other glands: it releases thyroid-stimulating hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone, luteinizing hormone and growth hormone. The posterior pituitary stores and releases two hormones made by the hypothalamus: antidiuretic hormone and oxytocin.

Adrenaline and antidiuretic hormone (ADH)

Adrenaline is released by the adrenal gland during fear or effort: it speeds the heart, widens the airways, sends blood to the muscles and makes the liver release glucose.

ADH increases water reabsorption in the kidney. When you are dehydrated more is released, so urine is less and more concentrated; after drinking a lot, less is released, so urine is plentiful and dilute.

Iodine and thyroxine

The thyroid gland needs iodine to make thyroxine, the hormone that raises the body's metabolic rate. Iodine is a raw material, not the hormone itself.

If iodine is lacking, thyroxine falls, so the pituitary releases more thyroid-stimulating hormone and the gland swells as it tries to compensate. This is a goitre.

Normally a high level of thyroxine holds back the release of thyroid-stimulating hormone: negative feedback. Low thyroxine removes that brake, so the stimulating hormone rises.

Menstrual-cycle hormones

The order of events over about 28 days:

  • Follicle-stimulating hormone grows a follicle in the ovary.
  • The follicle releases oestrogen, which builds up the uterine lining.
  • A sudden peak of oestrogen triggers a surge of luteinizing hormone, and ovulation occurs around day 14.
  • The corpus luteum releases progesterone, which keeps the lining ready.
  • If there is no pregnancy, progesterone falls and the lining breaks down (menstruation).

Laws and rules

The thyroxine chainiodine→thyroxine→higher metabolic rateLack of iodine lowers thyroxine, raises thyroid-stimulating hormone, and the gland swells.

Worked examples

A man sweats a lot and does not drink. What happens to ADH and to his urine?
Show the solution
  1. Water loss makes the blood more concentrated, and the hypothalamus detects this.
  2. The posterior pituitary releases more ADH.
  3. The kidney reabsorbs more water into the blood.
  4. Urine volume falls and urine becomes concentrated.
Answer: ADH rises, and urine becomes less and more concentrated.
A person lived on a diet without iodine and his thyroid gland swelled. Explain the steps.
Show the solution
  1. Without iodine the thyroid cannot make thyroxine.
  2. Low thyroxine in the blood raises the pituitary's output of thyroid-stimulating hormone.
  3. The gland grows and swells while trying to produce without its raw material.
Answer: Lack of iodine lowers thyroxine, raises stimulation, and the gland swells.
On day 14 luteinizing hormone peaks. What happens, and which hormone keeps the uterine lining afterwards?
Show the solution
  1. The luteinizing hormone peak makes the egg leave the follicle: ovulation.
  2. The empty follicle turns into a corpus luteum.
  3. The corpus luteum releases progesterone, which maintains the lining.
Answer: Ovulation occurs, and progesterone maintains the lining.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the pituitary is the hypothalamus: the hypothalamus is in the brain and makes the two hormones the posterior pituitary stores.
  • Thinking ADH increases urine. It reduces urine and saves water.
  • Mixing iodine with thyroxine: iodine is a raw material used to make the hormone.
  • Mixing oestrogen (builds the lining) with progesterone (maintains it).
Summary
  • The hypothalamus links the nervous system to glands; the pituitary regulates other glands.
  • Adrenaline prepares the body for effort; ADH saves water.
  • Iodine is needed to make thyroxine; lacking it swells the thyroid.
  • The cycle: follicle-stimulating hormone, oestrogen, luteinizing hormone (ovulation), progesterone.

Blood Glucose, Feedback and Diabetes

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Explain how insulin and glucagon keep blood glucose steady
  • Tell negative feedback from positive feedback with examples
  • Tell type 1 diabetes from type 2
  • Explain what insulin resistance means

Two hormones working together

The body needs a roughly constant blood glucose level. Two hormones released by the pancreas control it, and the liver carries out their orders.

  • Insulin: released by beta cells of the pancreas when glucose rises. It makes cells take up glucose and makes the liver turn it into stored glycogen, so glucose falls.
  • Glucagon: released by alpha cells when glucose falls. It makes the liver break down glycogen and release glucose, so glucose rises.

Feedback

Negative feedback opposes a change: a rise in glucose causes a response that lowers it, and a fall causes a response that raises it. "Negative" means it opposes the change; it does not mean "bad". It is the basis of homeostasis.

Positive feedback amplifies a change in the same direction until an event is complete. Examples: oxytocin during childbirth (more contraction releases more hormone), the surge of luteinizing hormone before ovulation, and blood clotting. It does not go on forever: it ends when the event is over.

Diabetes and insulin resistance

Diabetes is chronically high blood glucose. Typical signs are thirst and frequent urination, because the extra glucose leaves in the urine and carries water with it.

Type 1: the immune system destroys the insulin-making cells of the pancreas, so almost no insulin exists and the patient needs insulin injections.

Type 2: insulin is present, but cells respond to it poorly. This is insulin resistance. Glucose and insulin are both high at first; it is linked to obesity and inactivity, and diet and exercise help.

Laws and rules

Insulin controlglucose rises→insulin released→glucose fallsNegative feedback.
Glucagon controlglucose falls→glucagon released→glucose risesAlso negative feedback.

Worked examples

A student ate sweets and his blood glucose rose. Order what happens until it returns to normal.
Show the solution
  1. The pancreas detects the rise in blood glucose.
  2. Beta cells release insulin.
  3. Cells take up glucose, and the liver turns it into glycogen.
  4. Glucose falls to normal, so insulin release decreases.
Answer: Insulin lowers glucose by storage and uptake, and the response stops once glucose is normal.
Classify each: (a) insulin after a meal, (b) oxytocin during childbirth.
Show the solution
  1. (a) Insulin lowers raised glucose, so it opposes the change: negative.
  2. (b) Uterine contraction stimulates oxytocin, and oxytocin increases contraction: it amplifies the change.
  3. A response that amplifies the change is positive, and it ends with the birth.
Answer: (a) Negative feedback. (b) Positive feedback.
Two patients both have high blood glucose. The first has very low insulin; the second has high insulin. Which type of diabetes does each have?
Show the solution
  1. First: not enough insulin because the cells that make it were destroyed: type 1.
  2. Second: plenty of insulin but cells do not respond to it: insulin resistance, which is type 2.
Answer: The first has type 1; the second has type 2.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking insulin raises blood sugar. Insulin lowers it; glucagon raises it.
  • Reading "negative" as harmful. Negative opposes the change and keeps things steady.
  • Thinking type 2 is caused by no insulin. The main cause is poor response of the cells to it.
  • Forgetting the liver: it stores glucose as glycogen or releases it.
Summary
  • Insulin lowers blood glucose, glucagon raises it, and the liver carries it out.
  • Negative feedback opposes a change; positive feedback amplifies it (childbirth, for example).
  • Type 1: no insulin. Type 2: insulin resistance.
  • The goal: a steady glucose level that keeps balance.

Innate Immunity and the Lymphatic Organs

What you will learn
  • List the innate lines of defence and state that they are non-specific
  • State the role of bone marrow, thymus and lymph nodes
  • Explain why lymph nodes swell during infection

Innate immunity: defence ready from birth

Innate immunity is present from birth and attacks any microbe in the same way: it is non-specific, fast, and has no memory.

  • First line (barriers): skin, mucus and cilia in the airways, stomach acid, tears and saliva that contain the enzyme lysozyme.
  • Second line: phagocytosis by phagocytes, inflammation, fever, and natural killer cells.

Inflammation and fever: why?

When tissue is injured, damaged cells and mast cells release histamine. Blood vessels widen and become leakier, so the area turns red, warm and swollen, and phagocytes from the blood reach it to engulf the microbes.

Fever is a rise in body temperature set by the hypothalamus; it slows microbial growth and speeds up the immune cells.

The lymphatic organs

These are the organs where immune cells (lymphocytes) are made, mature or gather.

  • Bone marrow: makes all blood cells; B cells arise and mature here.
  • Thymus: lies behind the breastbone; T cells mature here.
  • Lymph nodes: filter lymph and trap microbes; B cells, T cells and phagocytes gather in them.
  • Spleen and tonsils: filter blood and guard the entrances.

Why do lymph nodes swell in infection?

When microbes enter the lymph, the nodes trap them and the lymphocytes inside multiply quickly to fight them, so the node enlarges. That is why neck nodes swell with a sore throat.

Worked examples

Classify each: (a) stomach acid, (b) phagocytosis, (c) specific antibodies.
Show the solution
  1. (a) A barrier that keeps microbes out: innate, first line.
  2. (b) Cells engulf any microbe: innate, second line.
  3. (c) Specific to one antigen and remembered: adaptive, not innate.
Answer: (a) and (b) are innate; (c) is adaptive.
Where do T cells mature, and where do B cells?
Show the solution
  1. Both arise in the bone marrow from stem cells.
  2. B cells mature in the bone marrow itself.
  3. T cells travel to the thymus and mature there.
Answer: T cells in the thymus; B cells in the bone marrow.
A child is born without a thymus. How is his immune system affected?
Show the solution
  1. The thymus is where T cells mature, so no mature T cells form.
  2. Without helper T cells the B cells are not activated, even though they matured in the bone marrow.
  3. Without killer T cells, virus-infected cells are cleared poorly.
  4. Innate immunity does not need the thymus.
Answer: Adaptive immunity is weakened in both branches, while innate immunity works as usual.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking innate immunity remembers a microbe. It has no memory.
  • Saying T cells mature in bone marrow. They mature in the thymus.
  • Thinking swollen nodes are a disease in themselves. They show immune cells at work.
Summary
  • Innate immunity is fast, non-specific and has no memory.
  • Bone marrow makes blood cells and B cells mature there.
  • T cells mature in the thymus.
  • Lymph nodes filter lymph and swell during infection.

Adaptive Immunity: Antigens, T Cells and B Cells

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Define an antigen and explain why the response to it is specific
  • State the roles of helper T cells, killer T cells and B cells
  • Tell humoral from cell-mediated immunity
  • Explain why the secondary response is faster and stronger

The antigen

An antigen is a molecule foreign to the body, often on the surface of a microbe, that immune cells recognise and respond to. Each lymphocyte recognises one particular antigen, like a lock that fits only one key.

The chain starts when a phagocyte engulfs a microbe and displays a piece of its antigen on its surface; a helper T cell recognises it and becomes active.

Who does what?

Antibodies are fork-shaped (Y-shaped) proteins with two arms; each type binds only one antigen. They bind the microbe and disable it, clump microbes together, and mark them for phagocytosis.

  • Helper T cells: the leaders of the response; they activate B cells and killer T cells with chemical signals. If they are destroyed, all immunity weakens, which is what the human immunodeficiency virus does.
  • B cells: become plasma cells that release antibodies, and memory cells that last for years.
  • Killer T cells: destroy virus-infected body cells and cancer cells by direct contact.

Two kinds of response

Humoral immunity: works through antibodies in body fluids, facing microbes outside cells such as bacteria and toxins. B cells lead it.

Cell-mediated immunity: carried out by killer T cells, which face microbes inside cells (such as viruses) and cancer cells.

Primary and secondary responses

At the first encounter with an antigen the response takes days to start, and the amount of antibody stays fairly low.

Memory cells remain afterwards. At the second encounter they recognise the antigen at once and multiply fast, so the secondary response is faster, stronger and longer.

Laws and rules

Humoral sequenceantigen→helper T cell→B cell→antibodiesThe B cell also produces memory cells.

Worked examples

Which branch of immunity faces (a) bacteria in the blood, (b) a virus inside an infected cell?
Show the solution
  1. (a) Bacteria are outside cells, in body fluids: antibodies face them, so humoral immunity.
  2. (b) A virus inside a cell is out of reach of antibodies: killer T cells destroy the infected cell.
Answer: (a) Humoral, through B cells. (b) Cell-mediated, through killer T cells.
Why does a person whose helper T cells are destroyed become weak against all microbes?
Show the solution
  1. Helper cells activate B cells.
  2. They also activate killer T cells.
  3. Without them neither humoral nor cell-mediated immunity starts.
Answer: Because they lead both branches.
Antibody production began after 7 days at the first encounter and after 2 days at the second, and peaked at 100 units then 1000 units. How many days faster was the second, and how many times higher?
Show the solution
  1. Difference in start time: 7−2=5 days.
  2. Ratio of peaks: 1000÷100=10.
Answer: 5 days faster and 10 times higher, because of memory cells.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking antibodies destroy virus-infected cells. That is the job of killer T cells.
  • Mixing antigen with antibody: the antigen is foreign and belongs to the microbe; the antibody is made by the body.
  • Thinking the secondary response starts from scratch. Memory cells make it faster and stronger.
  • Forgetting the helper cells: no strong humoral response without them.
Summary
  • An antigen is a foreign molecule that triggers a specific response.
  • Helpers lead, B cells make antibodies, killers destroy infected cells.
  • Humoral is for microbes outside cells; cell-mediated is for infected cells.
  • The secondary response is faster and stronger because of memory cells.

Vaccines, Passive Immunity, Allergy and Antibiotics

What you will learn
  • Explain how a vaccine gives immunity
  • Tell a vaccine from a serum, and active from passive immunity
  • Explain allergy
  • Know when antibiotics work and when they do not

Vaccines: training without disease

A vaccine contains a weakened or dead microbe, or a part of one. It prompts the body to produce a response and memory cells without making you ill. This is active immunity: the body itself makes the antibodies, so it starts slowly and lasts a long time.

Serum and passive immunity

A serum is ready-made antibodies taken from a person or animal. It works immediately but disappears after weeks, and the body makes no memory. This is passive immunity. An example is the mother's antibodies passing to the fetus through the placenta and to the infant in milk. Serum is used in emergencies such as a snake bite.

Active and passive immunity: four cases

  • Natural active: after catching the disease itself, such as chickenpox.
  • Artificial active: after receiving a vaccine.
  • Natural passive: the mother's antibodies passing to the fetus and infant.
  • Artificial passive: an injection of serum, such as snake-bite serum.

Allergy

An allergy is an exaggerated immune response to a harmless substance called an allergen, such as pollen. At the first encounter antibodies form and attach to mast cells. At the next encounter these cells release histamine and symptoms appear: sneezing, redness, swelling. Antihistamines reduce the symptoms.

Antibiotics

Antibiotics kill bacteria or stop their growth. They have no effect on viruses, so they do not treat colds or influenza. Misusing them selects resistant bacteria that are hard to treat.

Worked examples

A snake bites a man. Is he given a vaccine or a serum, and why?
Show the solution
  1. The venom acts within minutes; there is no time to wait for the body to make its own antibodies.
  2. Serum contains ready-made antibodies that act at once.
  3. A vaccine trains the body slowly and would not beat the venom.
Answer: Serum: immediate passive immunity.
Name the type of immunity in each case: (a) a child who caught chickenpox and never got it again, (b) a girl who received the measles vaccine, (c) a baby who does not catch its mother's illnesses in its first months, (d) a man given snake-bite serum.
Show the solution
  1. First ask: did the body make its own antibodies (active) or receive them ready-made (passive)?
  2. (a) The body made them after the infection and memory cells remained: natural active.
  3. (b) The body made them after the vaccine: artificial active.
  4. (c) The mother's antibodies came through the placenta and milk; the baby did not make them, and they fade after months: natural passive.
  5. (d) Ready-made antibodies were injected: artificial passive.
Answer: (a) Natural active. (b) Artificial active. (c) Natural passive. (d) Artificial passive.
A patient with influenza asks for an antibiotic. Will it help?
Show the solution
  1. Influenza is caused by a virus.
  2. Antibiotics target bacteria and do not affect viruses.
Answer: No, and it raises the risk of bacterial resistance.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing a vaccine (active, memory, slow) with a serum (passive, immediate, temporary).
  • Thinking an allergy is a response against a microbe. It is against a harmless substance.
  • Thinking an antibiotic treats viruses.
Summary
  • A vaccine makes memory and gives long active immunity.
  • Serum is ready-made antibodies: passive, immediate, short-lived.
  • Allergy is an exaggerated reaction through histamine.
  • Antibiotics work on bacteria only.
