Hydrocarbons: Types and Sources

What you will learn
  • Classify a hydrocarbon as saturated, unsaturated or aromatic.
  • Know the sources of hydrocarbons and how fractional distillation separates crude oil.
  • Understand a homologous series.
  • Describe the structure of benzene and why it differs from alkenes.

What is a hydrocarbon?

A hydrocarbon is a compound made of only two elements: carbon and hydrogen. Its main sources are oil and natural gas, which are fossil fuels formed from the remains of ancient living things.

A hydrocarbon is saturated if all its bonds are single, because it holds the most hydrogen possible. It is unsaturated if it has a double or triple bond, so it holds less hydrogen. Let n be the number of carbon atoms.

  • Alkanes (saturated): single bonds only, with 2n+2 hydrogens.
  • Alkenes (unsaturated): one double bond, with 2n hydrogens.
  • Alkynes (unsaturated): one triple bond, with 2n−2 hydrogens.
  • Aromatic compounds: contain a benzene ring, such as benzene CX6​HX6​.

Fractional distillation of oil

Crude oil is a mixture of hundreds of hydrocarbons. We heat it so it vaporises, and the vapour rises in the fractionating tower, getting cooler as it climbs.

Each component condenses at the level whose temperature is close to its boiling point. Small molecules (gases, gasoline) have weak attractions and low boiling points, so they reach the top. Large molecules (bitumen) have high boiling points and stay at the bottom. The separation depends on differences in boiling point.

Homologous series

A homologous series is a group of compounds with the same general formula and the same functional group, each member differing from the next by one CHX2​ unit.

Their chemical properties are alike, and their physical properties, such as boiling point, change steadily as the chain gets longer.

Benzene and aromatic compounds

Benzene CX6​HX6​ is a flat ring of 6 carbon atoms, with one hydrogen on each carbon. All the carbon-carbon bonds have the same length, between a single and a double bond, because the bonding electrons are spread over the whole ring.

So benzene does not behave like an alkene: it does not decolourise bromine water easily, and it prefers substitution reactions that keep the ring intact. Any compound with a benzene ring is called aromatic. Here benzene is a chemical, not car gasoline.

Laws and rules

Hydrogens in an alkanehydrogens=2n+2n is the number of carbon atoms.
Hydrogens in an alkenehydrogens=2nn is the number of carbon atoms; one double bond.
Hydrogens in an alkynehydrogens=2n−2n is the number of carbon atoms; one triple bond.

Worked examples

Assuming open chains, classify CX5​HX12​, CX5​HX10​ and CX5​HX8​ as alkane, alkene or alkyne.
Show the solution
  1. In all three, n=5.
  2. An alkane holds 2n+2=2(5)+2=12 hydrogens, which is CX5​HX12​.
  3. An alkene holds 2n=10 hydrogens, which is CX5​HX10​.
  4. An alkyne holds 2n−2=8 hydrogens, which is CX5​HX8​.
Answer: CX5​HX12​ is an alkane, CX5​HX10​ an alkene, CX5​HX8​ an alkyne.
The molar mass of ethane CX2​HX6​ is 30g/mol and of propane CX3​HX8​ is 44g/mol. What is the difference? What is the molar mass of butane CX4​HX10​?
Show the solution
  1. The difference is 44−30=14g/mol.
  2. A CHX2​ unit has mass 12+2=14g/mol, which is the gap between neighbours in a homologous series.
  3. Butane follows propane, so its mass is 44+14=58g/mol.
  4. Check: 4(12)+10(1)=58.
Answer: The difference is 14g/mol and butane has molar mass 58g/mol.
Order gasoline, kerosene and bitumen from the top of the tower to the bottom, and explain.
Show the solution
  1. The order follows boiling point: the lowest boiling point rises highest.
  2. Gasoline has the smallest molecules (about 5 to 10 carbons), so the lowest boiling point.
  3. Kerosene has bigger molecules and a higher boiling point, so it leaves below gasoline.
  4. Bitumen has by far the biggest molecules and the highest boiling point, so it stays at the bottom.
Answer: Top to bottom: gasoline, kerosene, bitumen, because boiling point rises with molecular size.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking "saturated" means the compound will not burn or react. It only means all carbon bonds are single.
  • Judging from a 2n formula alone that the compound is an alkene. It may also be a cycloalkane; we can be sure it is an alkene only when we know the chain is open.
  • Mixing up benzene the chemical with car gasoline. Benzene CX6​HX6​ is an aromatic compound with a very stable ring.
  • Saying benzene has three double and three single bonds of different lengths. All its bonds are the same length.
Summary
  • A hydrocarbon contains only carbon and hydrogen; oil and natural gas are the main sources.
  • An alkane has 2n+2 hydrogens, an alkene 2n, an alkyne 2n−2.
  • Fractional distillation separates oil by boiling point.
  • Members of a homologous series differ by CHX2​ (mass 14g/mol).
  • Benzene is a flat ring with equal bonds and prefers substitution to addition.

Alkanes: Naming and Isomers

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Write the general formula of alkanes and find their molar mass.
  • Name straight and branched alkanes by the systematic rules.
  • Recognise cycloalkanes.
  • Find the structural isomers of a molecular formula.

What are alkanes?

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons: every bond between carbons is single. If n is the number of carbons, the number of hydrogens is 2n+2. So methane is CHX4​, ethane CX2​HX6​ and propane CX3​HX8​.

n carbons weigh 12n and the hydrogens weigh 2n+2, so the molar mass of an alkane is 14n+2g/mol. Alkanes are not very reactive; their main reactions are combustion and substitution.

Names of straight chains

A name has a prefix for the carbon count followed by the ending -ane.

  • Prefixes: meth (1), eth (2), prop (3), but (4), pent (5), hex (6), hept (7), oct (8), non (9), dec (10).
  • Example: CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX2​−CHX2​−CHX3​ has 5 carbons, so it is pentane.

Naming branched alkanes

Follow four steps in order:

  • Choose the longest continuous chain of carbons, even if it is drawn bent. This is the parent.
  • Number the chain from the end nearest the first branch.
  • Name the branches: methyl (CHX3​) and ethyl (CX2​HX5​).
  • Write the branch number, a hyphen, the branch name, then the parent name. For repeated branches use di- and tri- with commas between numbers; for different branches list them alphabetically.

Cycloalkanes

When the chain closes into a ring, the compound is a cycloalkane and we add "cyclo" to the name: cyclohexane CX6​HX12​.

It has 2n hydrogens, two fewer than the open-chain alkane. Even so it is saturated, because all its bonds are single.

Structural isomers

Structural isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but a different arrangement of atoms, so their physical properties differ. The formula CX4​HX10​ has two isomers: butane and 2-methylpropane.

The more branched the isomer, the lower its boiling point, because the molecule is closer to a sphere and the attractions between molecules are weaker.

Laws and rules

General formula of alkaneshydrogens=2n+2n is the number of carbon atoms.
Molar mass of an alkaneM=14n+2n is the number of carbons; M in g/mol (C = 12, H = 1).

Worked examples

Name CHX3​−CH(CHX3​)−CHX2​−CHX3​.
Show the solution
  1. The longest chain has 4 carbons, so the parent is butane.
  2. The CHX3​ branch is a methyl group.
  3. Numbering from the left puts the branch on carbon 2; from the right, on carbon 3. Choose the smaller, 2.
Answer: 2-methylbutane, formula CX5​HX12​.
An alkane has 8 carbon atoms. What are its molecular formula and molar mass?
Show the solution
  1. Hydrogens: 2n+2=2(8)+2=18.
  2. The formula is CX8​HX18​, octane.
  3. Mass: 8(12)+18(1)=96+18=114g/mol.
  4. Check with the law: 14(8)+2=114.
Answer: CX8​HX18​ (octane), molar mass 114g/mol.
How many structural isomers has CX5​HX12​? Name them.
Show the solution
  1. CX5​HX12​ is an alkane; arrange its five carbons in different ways.
  2. Straight chain of 5: pentane CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX2​−CHX2​−CHX3​.
  3. Chain of 4 with one methyl on carbon 2: 2-methylbutane. (A methyl on carbon 3 is the same compound, numbered from the other end.)
  4. Chain of 3 with two methyls on carbon 2: 2,2-dimethylpropane C(CHX3​)X4​.
  5. No fourth arrangement exists. Check: each has 5 carbons and 12 hydrogens.
Answer: Three isomers: pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane.
Common mistakes
  • Picking the chain that is drawn straight instead of the longest chain. The longest chain may bend inside the drawing, so count carefully.
  • Numbering from the wrong end. The first branch must get the smallest number possible.
  • Calling a cycloalkane unsaturated or giving it 2n+2 hydrogens. It is saturated with 2n hydrogens.
  • Counting two drawings as isomers when they are the same molecule rotated. Isomers differ in how the atoms are connected.
Summary
  • An alkane is saturated with 2n+2 hydrogens and molar mass 14n+2.
  • Name = branch number and name, then the name of the longest chain.
  • The first branch gets the smallest possible number.
  • A cycloalkane is saturated with formula 2n hydrogens.
  • Structural isomers: one formula, different atom connections; more branching means lower boiling point.

Alkenes, Alkynes and Addition Reactions

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Write and name the formulas of alkenes and alkynes.
  • Explain why they react by addition.
  • Write the products of hydrogenation and hydration.
  • Know the bromine water test and the idea of polymerisation.

Alkenes and alkynes: structure and formula

An alkene is an unsaturated hydrocarbon with one double bond C=C and 2n hydrogens. The simplest are ethene CX2​HX4​ then propene CX3​HX6​.

An alkyne has a triple bond C≡C and 2n−2 hydrogens. The simplest are ethyne (acetylene) CX2​HX2​ then propyne CX3​HX4​. A double bond removes two hydrogens compared with the alkane, and a triple bond removes four.

Naming

Change alkane naming with these rules:

  • The longest chain must contain the double or triple bond.
  • Replace the ending -ane with -ene for an alkene and -yne for an alkyne.
  • Number from the end nearest the bond, and write the number of the first carbon of the bond: 1-butene, 2-butene, 2-butyne.
  • Ethene, propene, ethyne and propyne need no number because the bond has only one possible position.
  • Branches take their numbers from the same numbering that the bond decided: CHX2​=CH−CH(CHX3​)−CHX3​ is 3-methyl-1-butene.

Addition reactions

The second (or third) bond is weaker than the first, so it opens and adds new atoms, leaving a single product. That is why alkenes and alkynes are known for addition.

  • Hydrogenation (hydrogen HX2​ with a nickel or platinum catalyst): CHX2​=CHX2​+HX2​​CHX3​−CHX3​; the product is an alkane.
  • Adding bromine: CHX2​=CHX2​+BrX2​​CHX2​Br−CHX2​Br.
  • Hydration (adding water with an acid catalyst): CHX2​=CHX2​+HX2​O​CHX3​−CHX2​OH; the product is an alcohol.
  • Adding a hydrogen halide: CHX2​=CHX2​+HBr​CHX3​−CHX2​Br; the product is an alkyl halide.
  • Hydrogenation of an alkyne takes two steps: CH≡CH+HX2​​CHX2​=CHX2​ then CHX2​=CHX2​+HX2​​CHX3​−CHX3​, so 2 mol of hydrogen per mole of alkyne reaches the alkane.

Test for unsaturation

Add orange bromine water to the sample and shake. If the colour disappears the compound is unsaturated (alkene or alkyne), because bromine added across the bond. If the orange colour stays, it is an alkane.

Polymerisation

In addition polymerisation, thousands of small molecules (monomers) join into a long chain (polymer) by opening the double bond in each one. Ethene gives polyethylene: nCHX2​=CHX2​​[−CHX2​−CHX2​X−]Xn​.

Propene gives polypropylene, and vinyl chloride CHX2​=CHCl gives PVC. The monomer must contain a double bond so that it can open and join the molecules.

Laws and rules

Hydrogens in an alkenehydrogens=2nn is the number of carbon atoms.
Hydrogens in an alkynehydrogens=2n−2n is the number of carbon atoms.

Worked examples

Write the molecular formula of an alkene and an alkyne that each have 5 carbon atoms.
Show the solution
  1. n=5.
  2. Alkene: 2n=10 hydrogens, so CX5​HX10​.
  3. Alkyne: 2n−2=2(5)−2=8 hydrogens, so CX5​HX8​.
Answer: The alkene is CX5​HX10​ and the alkyne is CX5​HX8​.
Name CHX3​−CH=CH−CHX2​−CHX3​.
Show the solution
  1. The chain has 5 carbons and a double bond, so the parent is pentene.
  2. Numbering from the left: the bond is between carbons 2 and 3, so the number is 2.
  3. Numbering from the right: the bond is between carbons 3 and 4, so the number is 3.
  4. Choose the smaller, 2.
Answer: 2-pentene.
0.5 mol of propyne CX3​HX4​ is fully hydrogenated. How many moles of hydrogen are needed, and what is the mass of the alkane formed? (C = 12, H = 1)
Show the solution
  1. Equation: CX3​HX4​+2HX2​​CX3​HX8​.
  2. Full hydrogenation takes 2 mol of hydrogen per mole of propyne: 0.5×2=1 mol.
  3. One mole of propane forms per mole of propyne, so 0.5 mol.
  4. Molar mass of propane: 3(12)+8=44g/mol.
  5. Mass: 0.5×44=22 g.
Answer: 1 mol of hydrogen is needed and 22 g of propane forms.
Common mistakes
  • Numbering from the end nearest the first branch instead of the end nearest the bond. In alkenes the bond has priority.
  • Saying an alkane decolourises bromine water. An alkane does not react in the dark, so the colour stays.
  • Forgetting that an alkyne needs 2 mol of hydrogen to reach the alkane but only 1 mol to reach the alkene.
  • Mixing up addition and substitution: in addition one product forms and no atom leaves the molecule.
Summary
  • An alkene has a double bond and 2n hydrogens; an alkyne has a triple bond and 2n−2.
  • Number from the end nearest the bond and write its number before the name.
  • Hydrogenation gives an alkane, hydration gives an alcohol, bromine addition gives a dibromo compound, and adding HBr gives an alkyl halide.
  • Unsaturated compounds decolourise orange bromine water.
  • Addition polymerisation joins alkene molecules into a polymer.

Combustion of Hydrocarbons and Calculations

What you will learn
  • Tell complete from incomplete combustion.
  • Balance the combustion equation of any hydrocarbon.
  • Calculate moles, masses and volumes from the equation.

Complete combustion

When a hydrocarbon burns in plenty of oxygen it gives carbon dioxide COX2​ and water HX2​O and releases a lot of heat, with a clean blue flame. For propane: CX3​HX8​+5OX2​​3COX2​+4HX2​O.

Balancing a combustion equation

Always use the same order:

  • Balance carbon: each carbon atom gives one COX2​.
  • Balance hydrogen: each pair of hydrogens gives one HX2​O.
  • Balance oxygen last: count the oxygen atoms in the products and halve to get the coefficient of OX2​.
  • If a fractional coefficient appears, multiply the whole equation by 2.

Incomplete combustion

When oxygen is limited, the products are carbon monoxide CO, which is poisonous (it binds to hemoglobin in the blood and blocks oxygen transport), colourless and odourless, or carbon as black soot, plus water. The flame is yellow and sooty and less heat is released.

Examples: 2CHX4​+3OX2​​2CO+4HX2​O and CHX4​+OX2​​C+2HX2​O.

Calculations from the equation

The coefficients of a balanced equation give the ratio of moles. Convert the known quantity to moles, multiply by the ratio of coefficients, then convert the wanted quantity to mass. For gases at the same pressure and temperature, the volume ratio equals the mole ratio.

Useful molar masses (g/mol): OX2​=32, COX2​=44, HX2​O=18.

Laws and rules

Molesn=Mm​m is mass in g, M is molar mass in g/mol, n is moles.
Mole rationwanted​=nknown​×coefficient knowncoefficient wanted​The coefficients come from the balanced equation.
Oxygen for burning an alkanenO2​​=23n+1​×nalkane​n is the number of carbons in the alkane; valid for complete combustion of alkanes only. Example: propane has n=3, so it needs 210​=5 mol per mole.

Worked examples

Balance the combustion of ethane: CX2​HX6​+OX2​​COX2​+HX2​O.
Show the solution
  1. Carbon: ethane has 2 atoms, so write 2COX2​.
  2. Hydrogen: 6 atoms, so write 3HX2​O.
  3. Oxygen in the products: 2×2+3=7 atoms, which is 3.5 molecules of OX2​.
  4. The coefficient is fractional, so multiply everything by 2.
Answer: 2CX2​HX6​+7OX2​​4COX2​+6HX2​O. Oxygen check: 14=8+6.
11g of propane CX3​HX8​ burns completely. Find the mass of COX2​ formed. (C = 12, H = 1)
Show the solution
  1. Molar mass of propane: 3(12)+8=44g/mol.
  2. Moles: 4411​=0.25 mol.
  3. From CX3​HX8​+5OX2​​3COX2​+4HX2​O, each mole of propane gives 3 mol of COX2​: 0.25×3=0.75 mol.
  4. Molar mass of COX2​ is 12+32=44g/mol, so the mass is 0.75×44=33 g.
  5. Check mass conservation: oxygen used is 0.25×5=1.25 mol, which is 40 g, so the reactants weigh 11+40=51 g. Water formed is 0.25×4=1 mol, which is 18 g, so the products weigh 33+18=51 g.
Answer: 33 g of COX2​.
One mole of methane CHX4​ burns. How many moles of OX2​ are needed for complete combustion, for forming CO, and for forming carbon?
Show the solution
  1. Complete: CHX4​+2OX2​​COX2​+2HX2​O, so 2 mol.
  2. To CO: 2CHX4​+3OX2​​2CO+4HX2​O, so 23​=1.5 mol per mole of methane.
  3. To carbon: CHX4​+OX2​​C+2HX2​O, so 1 mol.
Answer: Complete: 2 mol; to carbon monoxide: 1.5 mol; to carbon: 1 mol. Incomplete combustion uses less oxygen.
Common mistakes
  • Balancing oxygen first. Start with carbon, then hydrogen, and leave oxygen for last.
  • Forgetting that each HX2​O holds two hydrogens, which gives a wrong water coefficient.
  • Using coefficients directly on masses. Coefficients give a mole ratio, not a gram ratio.
  • Thinking incomplete combustion makes only COX2​. It makes CO or carbon together with water.
Summary
  • Complete combustion: a hydrocarbon with plenty of oxygen gives COX2​, HX2​O and heat.
  • Incomplete combustion: little oxygen gives CO or carbon, with a yellow sooty flame.
  • Balance carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen.
  • Coefficients are a mole ratio; for gases at the same conditions they are a volume ratio.
  • Calculation path: mass, moles, coefficient ratio, mass.

Substitution and Alkyl Halides

What you will learn
  • Describe the substitution of alkanes by halogens.
  • Name alkyl halides.
  • Tell substitution from addition.

Substitution reaction

In substitution an atom or group takes the place of an atom in the molecule. Alkanes are saturated so they cannot add; they substitute. Methane reacts with chlorine in light: CHX4​+ClX2​​CHX3​Cl+HCl.

A chlorine atom replaces a hydrogen atom, giving two products: an alkyl halide and a hydrogen halide. With plenty of chlorine the substitution continues up to CClX4​.

  • CHX3​Cl: chloromethane.
  • CHX2​ClX2​: dichloromethane.
  • CHClX3​: trichloromethane (chloroform).
  • CClX4​: tetrachloromethane.

Alkyl halides

An alkyl halide is an alkane in which a halogen atom (fluorine, chlorine, bromine or iodine) has taken the place of a hydrogen atom. We name it by writing the position number, then the halogen prefix (fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo), then the alkane name. Example: CHX3​−CHBr−CHX3​ is 2-bromopropane.

An alkyl halide also forms when a hydrogen halide adds to an alkene, giving one product: CHX2​=CHX2​+HBr​CHX3​−CHX2​Br (bromoethane).

Substitution versus addition

This is the contrast that keeps appearing in questions:

  • Substitution: a saturated compound (alkane), two products, and light is needed.
  • Addition: an unsaturated compound (alkene or alkyne), one product, and the multiple bond opens.

Worked examples

Classify each reaction as substitution or addition: (a) CX2​HX6​+BrX2​​CX2​HX5​Br+HBr in light. (b) CX2​HX4​+BrX2​​CX2​HX4​BrX2​.
Show the solution
  1. In (a): the reactant is a saturated alkane, there are two products, and a bromine atom took the place of a hydrogen.
  2. In (b): the reactant is an unsaturated alkene, there is one product, and the double bond opened.
Answer: (a) substitution. (b) addition.
Name CHX3​−CHBr−CHX3​ and CHX3​−CHX2​−CHX2​−Cl.
Show the solution
  1. The first has 3 carbons (propane) with bromine on the middle carbon, which is carbon 2 from either end.
  2. The second has 3 carbons with chlorine on carbon 1, because we number from the end nearest the chlorine.
Answer: 2-bromopropane, and 1-chloropropane.
How many moles of ClX2​ are needed to convert one mole of methane fully to CClX4​? How many moles of HCl form?
Show the solution
  1. Methane has 4 hydrogens and all are replaced.
  2. Each replacement uses one ClX2​ molecule and gives one HCl molecule.
  3. Equation: CHX4​+4ClX2​​CClX4​+4HCl.
Answer: 4 mol of ClX2​ are needed and 4 mol of HCl form.
Common mistakes
  • Writing an addition equation for an alkane. An alkane is saturated, so it only substitutes.
  • Forgetting the second product, the hydrogen halide HX.
  • Ignoring the halogen when numbering. The halogen is a branch, so we number from the end that gives the first branch the smaller number.
  • Thinking an alkyl halide can only come from substitution. It also forms by adding HX to an alkene.
Summary
  • Substitution: an atom replaces a hydrogen atom; it is the typical alkane reaction.
  • Products of substitution: an alkyl halide and HX.
  • An alkyl halide also forms by adding HX to an alkene.
  • Alkyl halide name: number, halogen prefix, alkane name.
  • Addition is for unsaturated compounds and gives one product.

Alcohols and Ethers

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Recognise the hydroxyl group and classify alcohols.
  • Name alcohols.
  • Write the products of oxidation and dehydration.
  • Understand functional isomers of alcohols and ethers.

Alcohols and their names

An alcohol has a hydroxyl group −OH attached to a saturated carbon; its general formula is R−OH. The simplest are methanol CHX3​OH and ethanol CX2​HX5​OH.

We name it by changing the alkane ending -e to -ol. The longest chain must contain the carbon that carries the OH, and we number from the end nearest it: 1-propanol and 2-propanol.

Classifying alcohols

Count the other carbons attached to the carbon that carries the OH:

  • Primary: attached to one carbon (or none, as in methanol), such as 1-propanol.
  • Secondary: attached to two carbons, such as 2-propanol.
  • Tertiary: attached to three carbons, such as 2-methyl-2-propanol.

Oxidation

A strong oxidising agent (such as acidified potassium dichromate) oxidises an alcohol. The result depends on the type, because oxidation removes hydrogen from the carbon that carries the OH: a primary alcohol has two such hydrogens, a secondary one has one, and a tertiary one has none.

  • Primary becomes an aldehyde and then a carboxylic acid: CHX3​CHX2​OH​CHX3​CHO​CHX3​COOH.
  • Secondary becomes a ketone: CHX3​CH(OH)CHX3​​CHX3​COCHX3​.
  • Tertiary does not oxidise easily.

Dehydration

Heating an alcohol with concentrated sulfuric acid removes a molecule of water and gives an alkene: CHX3​CHX2​OH​CHX2​=CHX2​+HX2​O. This is the reverse of hydration of an alkene.

Ethers and functional isomers

An ether has an oxygen atom between two carbons, R−O−RX′. We name it by giving the two alkyl groups and then the word ether: dimethyl ether CHX3​OCHX3​ (systematic name methoxymethane) and ethyl methyl ether CHX3​OCHX2​CHX3​ (methoxyethane).

Ethanol and dimethyl ether both have the formula CX2​HX6​O but different functional groups, so they are functional isomers. Common pairs are alcohol and ether, aldehyde and ketone, acid and ester.

Functional isomers have very different properties. The alcohol boils higher because its molecules form hydrogen bonds; an ether has no hydrogen bonded to oxygen, so it cannot form them between its molecules.

Worked examples

Classify and name CHX3​CHX2​CHX2​OH, CHX3​CH(OH)CHX3​ and (CHX3​)X3​C−OH.
Show the solution
  1. First: the carbon with OH is attached to one carbon, so it is primary, with a 3-carbon chain: 1-propanol.
  2. Second: the carbon with OH is attached to two carbons, so it is secondary: 2-propanol.
  3. Third: the carbon with OH is attached to three carbons, so it is tertiary: 2-methyl-2-propanol.
Answer: 1-propanol (primary), 2-propanol (secondary), 2-methyl-2-propanol (tertiary).
What is the product of oxidising ethanol (with plenty of oxidiser) and 2-propanol?
Show the solution
  1. Ethanol is a primary alcohol: it oxidises to ethanal and then on to ethanoic acid.
  2. 2-propanol is a secondary alcohol: it oxidises to a ketone.
  3. The ketone has 3 carbons: CHX3​COCHX3​ is propanone.
Answer: Ethanol gives ethanoic acid, and 2-propanol gives propanone.
What organic product forms when 1-propanol is heated with concentrated sulfuric acid? Write the equation.
Show the solution
  1. The acid removes a water molecule: OH leaves one carbon and H leaves the neighbouring carbon.
  2. A double bond forms between those two carbons.
  3. Equation: CHX3​CHX2​CHX2​OH​CHX3​−CH=CHX2​+HX2​O.
  4. Check: CX3​HX8​O on the left, and CX3​HX6​ plus HX2​O on the right.
Answer: Propene.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the OH of an alcohol makes it a base. It is a hydroxyl group bonded covalently and it does not ionise.
  • Oxidising a tertiary alcohol. It does not oxidise easily.
  • Mixing up the oxidation results: primary gives an aldehyde or acid, secondary gives a ketone.
  • Treating an alcohol and an ether as the same in physical properties. They share a formula but their properties differ.
Summary
  • An alcohol carries OH and is named with -ol and a position number.
  • Primary is attached to one carbon, secondary to two, tertiary to three.
  • Primary gives aldehyde then acid, secondary gives ketone, tertiary does not oxidise.
  • Dehydration with concentrated sulfuric acid gives an alkene.
  • An alcohol and an ether with the same formula are functional isomers.

Aldehydes and Ketones

What you will learn
  • Recognise the carbonyl group.
  • Tell an aldehyde from a ketone.
  • Name aldehydes and ketones.

The carbonyl group

The carbonyl group is a carbon double-bonded to an oxygen, C=O. Where it sits in the chain decides the type of compound.

  • Aldehyde: the carbonyl is at the end of the chain, so that carbon also holds a hydrogen; the group is −CHO.
  • Ketone: the carbonyl is inside the chain, between two carbons.

Naming aldehydes

Change the alkane ending -e to -al. The carbonyl carbon is always carbon 1, so no number is needed. Examples: methanal HCHO, ethanal CHX3​CHO, propanal CHX3​CHX2​CHO.

Naming ketones

Change the ending to -one, and number from the end nearest the carbonyl. The simplest ketone is propanone (acetone) CHX3​COCHX3​, because the carbonyl cannot be at the end. Other examples are 2-pentanone and 3-pentanone. Propanone and butanone need no number because the carbonyl has only one possible position.

How they differ

An aldehyde oxidises easily to a carboxylic acid, but a ketone does not. An aldehyde and a ketone with the same number of carbons share a formula, so they are functional isomers: propanal and propanone are both CX3​HX6​O. In the lab, Tollens' reagent tells them apart: an aldehyde gives a silver mirror and a ketone does not.

Laws and rules

Hydrogens in an aldehyde or ketonehydrogens=2nn is the number of carbons; for a compound with one oxygen atom and one C=O bond.

Worked examples

Name CHX3​CHX2​CHO and CHX3​−CO−CHX2​CHX2​CHX3​.
Show the solution
  1. First: the carbonyl is at the end with a hydrogen, so it is an aldehyde with 3 carbons: propanal.
  2. Second: the carbonyl is inside the chain, so it is a ketone with 5 carbons.
  3. Numbering from the left puts the carbonyl on 2; from the right, on 4. Choose 2.
Answer: Propanal, and 2-pentanone.
Write the formulas of the two 3-carbon compounds that contain a carbonyl, and state how they are related.
Show the solution
  1. Aldehyde: CHX3​CHX2​CHO, propanal.
  2. Ketone: CHX3​COCHX3​, propanone.
  3. Count atoms: each has 3 carbons, 6 hydrogens and 1 oxygen, so CX3​HX6​O.
Answer: They share the formula CX3​HX6​O but have different groups, so they are functional isomers.
Find the molar mass of any aldehyde or ketone with 4 carbons. (C = 12, H = 1, O = 16)
Show the solution
  1. Each has one C=O bond, so the hydrogens are 2n, as in an alkene: 2(4)=8.
  2. The formula is CX4​HX8​O.
  3. Mass: 4(12)+8(1)+16=48+8+16=72g/mol.
Answer: 72g/mol (butanal and butanone).
Common mistakes
  • Placing a ketone carbonyl at the end of the chain. A carbonyl at the end means an aldehyde.
  • Writing a number before an aldehyde name. Its carbonyl is always carbon 1.
  • Thinking propanone is an aldehyde because it looks like propanal. The difference is the carbonyl position.
  • Numbering a ketone from the end far from the carbonyl.
Summary
  • Both contain the carbonyl C=O.
  • Aldehyde: carbonyl at the end (-al); ketone: carbonyl inside (-one).
  • Number a ketone from the end nearest the carbonyl.
  • An aldehyde and a ketone with the same carbon count are functional isomers.

Carboxylic Acids, Esters and Amines

What you will learn
  • Recognise the carboxyl group and name acids.
  • Write the esterification reaction and name the ester.
  • Recognise amines and their basic character.

Carboxylic acids

A carboxylic acid has the carboxyl group −COOH. We name it with the ending -oic acid: methanoic acid HCOOH, ethanoic acid (vinegar acid) CHX3​COOH and propanoic acid CHX3​CHX2​COOH. The carboxyl carbon is carbon 1.

They are weak acids that ionise partly in water, they react with sodium carbonate to release COX2​ gas, and they form by oxidising primary alcohols.

Esterification

Heating a carboxylic acid with an alcohol and a little concentrated sulfuric acid gives an ester and water. The reaction is reversible: CHX3​COOH+CHX3​CHX2​OH​CHX3​COOCHX2​CHX3​+HX2​O.

The sulfuric acid acts as a catalyst and removes water, so more ester forms.

Esters and their names

An ester contains the group −COO− and often has a pleasant fruity smell. Its name has two parts: the alkyl group that comes from the alcohol is written first, and the acid name changes to -oate. From ethanoic acid and methanol we get methyl ethanoate.

The name starts with the alcohol part, but in the formula the acid part is written first. Find the acid part from the carbonyl side.

Amines

An amine is derived from ammonia NHX3​ by replacing one or more hydrogens with alkyl groups. The simplest are methylamine CHX3​NHX2​ and ethylamine CHX3​CHX2​NHX2​.

In a primary amine the functional group is −NHX2​ (the amino group). The nitrogen atom has a lone pair of electrons, so it accepts a proton from water and the amine is a weak base. Amines often smell like fish.

An amine has no C=O. If a carbonyl is bonded to the nitrogen atom, the compound is an amide, not an amine.

Worked examples

Name CHX3​COOCHX3​ and CHX3​CHX2​COOCHX2​CHX3​.
Show the solution
  1. First: the acid part, the side with C=O, has two carbons: ethanoate; the other part CHX3​ is methyl.
  2. Second: the acid part has 3 carbons: propanoate; the other part CHX2​CHX3​ is ethyl.
Answer: Methyl ethanoate, and ethyl propanoate.
Write the reaction of methanoic acid with ethanol and name the ester.
Show the solution
  1. The acid is HCOOH and the alcohol is CHX3​CHX2​OH.
  2. OH from the acid and H from the alcohol leave as water.
  3. Equation: HCOOH+CHX3​CHX2​OH​HCOOCHX2​CHX3​+HX2​O.
  4. The acid part is methanoate and the other part is ethyl.
Answer: The ester is ethyl methanoate, HCOOCHX2​CHX3​.
6g of ethanoic acid reacts with excess ethanol with a 100% yield. What mass of ethyl ethanoate forms? (C = 12, H = 1, O = 16)
Show the solution
  1. Molar mass of CHX3​COOH: 2(12)+4(1)+2(16)=60g/mol.
  2. Moles: 606​=0.1 mol.
  3. The ratio is 1 to 1, so 0.1 mol of ester CX4​HX8​OX2​ forms.
  4. Its molar mass is 4(12)+8+2(16)=88g/mol, so the mass is 0.1×88=8.8 g.
Answer: 8.8 g.
Common mistakes
  • Reversing the ester name. The acid part comes from the acid, the alkyl part from the alcohol, and the alkyl part is read first.
  • Thinking esterification goes to completion. It is reversible and reaches equilibrium with water.
  • Thinking every compound with −OH is an alcohol. In an acid the −OH is bonded to the carbonyl carbon (−COOH), so it is an acid, not an alcohol.
  • Calling an amine an acid. An amine is a weak base because of the lone pair on nitrogen.
Summary
  • A carboxylic acid carries −COOH and is named with -oic acid.
  • Esterification: acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water, catalysed by concentrated sulfuric acid.
  • Ester name: alkyl from the alcohol, then -oate from the acid.
  • An amine is derived from ammonia and is a weak base.
  • An amine has no carbonyl; if a carbonyl is bonded to nitrogen, the compound is an amide.
