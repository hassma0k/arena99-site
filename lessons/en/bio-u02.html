ATP and Cell Energy

What you will learn
  • Know why ATP is called the energy currency
  • Understand how ATP is used and rebuilt
  • Tell breakdown pathways from building pathways
  • Trace energy from the Sun to the cell

ATP, the energy currency

Every cell job needs energy: moving substances across a membrane, contracting a muscle, building protein. The cell does not spend glucose directly on these jobs. It first moves glucose energy into a small ready molecule, ATP (adenosine triphosphate).

ATP is like small change: you can spend it at once anywhere in the cell. Glucose is like a gold bar that must be broken up before it can be spent.

How ATP releases energy

ATP is made of adenine, the sugar ribose and three phosphate groups. When the last bond breaks, one phosphate leaves, the molecule becomes ADP (adenosine diphosphate), and energy is released for the cell to use at once.

The cell then rebuilds ATP by adding a third phosphate to ADP, using energy from food breakdown (respiration) or from light (photosynthesis). One molecule is used and renewed thousands of times.

Metabolic pathways

Metabolism is all the chemical reactions in a cell. They run in chains called metabolic pathways. There are two kinds: breakdown pathways take large molecules apart and release energy (like cellular respiration); building pathways make large molecules from small ones and use energy (like photosynthesis).

Each pathway runs in steps, each catalysed by its own enzyme, and the product of one step is the starting material of the next. A breakdown pathway releases energy, and a building pathway absorbs it.

From the Sun to the cell

Autotrophs, such as plants, make their own food from simple inorganic substances, mostly using light energy. Heterotrophs, such as a lion, get food by eating other organisms.

So the energy in ATP inside a lion muscle came from the prey, the prey got it from the plant it ate, and the plant stored it from sunlight by photosynthesis. The Sun is the original source of energy for most living things.

Laws and rules

Using ATPATP→ADP+P+energyOne phosphate group (P) leaves and the energy is used for cell work.
Renewing ATPADP+P+energy→ATPThe energy comes from cellular respiration, or from light in photosynthesis.

Worked examples

Which gives a cell instant energy: glucose or ATP? Why?
Show the solution
  1. Glucose stores energy but must be broken down first in several steps.
  2. ATP releases its energy in one step by breaking the last phosphate bond.
  3. So the ready, instant energy is in ATP.
Answer: ATP, because it is a ready energy currency that is spent directly.
A muscle cell uses 5 ATP molecules every second. How many ADP molecules form in 10 seconds, and how many phosphate groups are needed to rebuild all of them?
Show the solution
  1. Each used ATP becomes one ADP.
  2. ADP molecules =5×10=50.
  3. Each ADP needs one phosphate to become ATP again, so 50 phosphates.
Answer: 50 ADP molecules and 50 phosphate groups.
Trace the energy that moves a gazelle muscle back to its source.
Show the solution
  1. The muscle uses ATP.
  2. The ATP was built with energy from breaking down food, and the food came from plants the gazelle ate.
  3. The plant stored that energy by photosynthesis from sunlight.
Answer: The original source is sunlight.
Common mistakes
  • Saying glucose gives the cell energy for work directly. Correct: glucose stores energy, and ATP is what powers the work.
  • Thinking ATP is used up and disappears. Correct: it becomes ADP and is rebuilt by adding phosphate.
  • Mixing up breakdown and building. Breakdown takes apart and releases energy; building assembles and uses energy.
Summary
  • ATP is the cell's ready energy currency.
  • Breaking the last phosphate turns ATP into ADP and releases energy.
  • Adding phosphate to ADP with energy rebuilds ATP.
  • Breakdown releases energy; building uses it.
  • Sunlight is the original energy source for most life.

Enzymes

What you will learn
  • Explain how an enzyme speeds up a reaction
  • Link the active site to enzyme specificity
  • Know how heat and pH affect enzymes
  • Understand that an enzyme is not used up

What is an enzyme?

Enzymes are proteins that act as catalysts: they speed up biological reactions without being used up. The substance an enzyme works on is the substrate, and what forms in the end is the product.

Every reaction needs a first push of energy called activation energy, like a hill you must climb before going down. The enzyme does not change the final energy or the amount of product. It lowers the hill, so the reaction is much faster.

Active site and specificity

An enzyme has a small pocket whose shape fits its substrate exactly, called the active site. The substrate binds like a key in a lock, forming an enzyme-substrate complex, which then turns into product and frees the enzyme. On binding, the site changes shape slightly to grip the substrate more tightly.

Because the active site fits only one shape, each enzyme is specific: amylase breaks down starch but not protein, since its site does not fit protein.

An enzyme is not used up

The enzyme leaves the reaction unchanged, binds a new substrate and works again. So a small amount of enzyme can convert a very large number of substrates.

Heat and pH

An enzyme is a protein, and its work depends on its exact shape. Each enzyme has an optimum temperature (about 37 °C for human enzymes) and an optimum pH (pepsin in the stomach about 2, small-intestine enzymes about 8).

The rate rises with temperature up to the optimum because molecules collide more; below the optimum the enzyme is only slower and speeds up again on warming.

But very high heat, or a pH far from the optimum, breaks the bonds that hold the protein in shape, so it denatures: its shape changes, and so does the active site, and the substrate no longer fits. Strong denaturation does not reverse by cooling or by restoring pH, so the enzyme does not work again.

Laws and rules

How an enzyme worksenzyme+substrate→complex→enzyme+productThe enzyme comes out as it was, ready for a new substrate.

Worked examples

Pepsin works in the stomach at pH about 2. What happens to it in a medium of pH 8?
Show the solution
  1. Pepsin's optimum pH is about 2.
  2. A pH of 8 is far away, so the enzyme changes shape.
  3. A new shape changes the active site, so the substrate does not fit.
Answer: Its activity drops or stops.
One enzyme molecule converts 1000 substrates per second. How many does it convert in 5 seconds, and how many enzyme molecules remain?
Show the solution
  1. Substrates converted =1000×5=5000.
  2. The enzyme is not used up, so the number after equals the number before.
Answer: 5000 substrates, and one intact enzyme molecule remains.
A human enzyme is heated to 80 °C and cooled to 37 °C. Does it work? Explain.
Show the solution
  1. 80 °C is far above its optimum, so the bonds keeping its shape break.
  2. The enzyme denatures and its active site changes shape.
  3. Denaturation is permanent: cooling does not restore the old shape.
Answer: No, because its active site has changed shape permanently.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the enzyme is used up in the reaction. Correct: it comes out intact and works again and again.
  • Saying the enzyme increases the final amount of product. Correct: it only speeds up reaching it, by lowering activation energy.
  • Thinking cooling restores a denatured enzyme. Correct: denaturation by strong heat is permanent, while cold alone only slows an enzyme and does not denature it.
  • Forgetting each enzyme has its own optimum pH. Pepsin, for example, prefers acid, not base.
Summary
  • Enzymes are protein catalysts that lower activation energy.
  • The substrate fits the active site like a key in a lock.
  • Each enzyme has its own substrate: this is specificity.
  • Strong heat or a wrong pH denatures an enzyme.
  • An enzyme is not used up and is reused.

Photosynthesis and the Light Reactions

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Write the photosynthesis equation and the source of each substance
  • Know where each stage occurs in the chloroplast
  • Explain why leaves are green
  • Follow the electrons and products of the light reactions
  • Identify the limiting factor of photosynthesis rate

The overall equation

Photosynthesis is a building pathway: the plant turns light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. The plant takes in COX2​ from the air through the stomata and water from the soil, makes glucose, and releases OX2​.

The released oxygen does not come from COX2​. It comes from water split by light. From the balanced equation, every glucose molecule made releases 6 oxygen molecules.

Two stages inside the chloroplast

The light reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes, where the pigments, the two photosystems and the electron transport chain are. The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma, the fluid around the thylakoids, where its enzymes are.

Why are leaves green?

Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light strongly and reflects green light more than it absorbs it. The reflected green reaches our eyes, so the leaf looks green.

Inside the light reactions

Photosystem II works first (it is called second because it was discovered after photosystem I). When it absorbs light, its electrons are excited into the electron transport chain, and it replaces them with new electrons from water splitting, which also gives protons and oxygen.

The electrons move down the chain, and their energy pumps protons into the thylakoid; the protons then flow back through ATP synthase, which builds ATP. After photosystem I re-energises the electrons, the final acceptor NADPX+ receives them and becomes NADPH. So the products of the light reactions are ATP, NADPH and oxygen.

The limiting factor

The rate of photosynthesis is set by whichever of light, COX2​ and temperature is in shortest supply. If COX2​ is very low, more light does not help; raise the COX2​. If light is weak, more COX2​ does not help.

Laws and rules

Photosynthesis equation6COX2​+6HX2​O​CX6​HX12​OX6​+6OX2​It occurs in the presence of light. Six CO2 give one glucose and six oxygen molecules.
Splitting of water by light2HX2​O​4HX++4eX−+OX2​Source of electrons and protons, and the source of the released oxygen.

Worked examples

A plant builds 3 glucose molecules by photosynthesis. How many COX2​ molecules does it use and how many OX2​ does it release?
Show the solution
  1. From the equation: 6 COX2​ give one glucose and 6 OX2​.
  2. Multiply by 3: COX2​ =6×3=18.
  3. Likewise OX2​ =6×3=18.
Answer: 18 COX2​ molecules and 18 OX2​ molecules.
A plant is in strong light but the COX2​ around it is very low. What is the best change to raise its photosynthesis rate?
Show the solution
  1. Find the scarcest factor: light is plentiful, COX2​ is scarce.
  2. The scarce factor is the limiting one, so raise it.
Answer: Raise the COX2​ concentration. More light does not help.
A plant is given water containing radioactive oxygen atoms and normal COX2​. In which product does the radioactive oxygen appear after photosynthesis?
Show the solution
  1. The released oxygen comes from splitting water by light, not from COX2​.
  2. So the radioactive oxygen atoms in the water leave in the OX2​ molecules.
  3. The oxygen of COX2​ ends up in glucose and in new water.
Answer: In the released OX2​ gas.
Common mistakes
  • Saying the released oxygen comes from COX2​. Correct: it comes from splitting water.
  • Mixing up where the stages occur. Light reactions are in the thylakoid; Calvin is in the stroma.
  • Forgetting that chlorophyll reflects green and does not absorb it well.
  • Believing that raising any factor raises the rate. Only raising the limiting factor does.
Summary
  • Photosynthesis turns light energy into glucose energy.
  • Inputs: COX2​, water, light. Outputs: glucose and oxygen.
  • The oxygen comes from splitting water.
  • Light reactions are in the thylakoid; Calvin is in the stroma.
  • The light reactions make ATP, NADPH and oxygen.

The Calvin Cycle

What you will learn
  • Identify the inputs and product of the Calvin cycle
  • Know the carbon-fixing enzyme
  • Explain why the cycle stops in the dark
  • Predict what happens when COX2​ is blocked

What the cycle does

The Calvin cycle is a set of reactions in the stroma that builds sugar from COX2​. It does not use light directly, but it needs the ATP and NADPH made by the light reactions.

Inputs: COX2​ as raw material, ATP as energy, and NADPH, which gives the electrons that reduce carbon.

Three steps

First, carbon fixation: the enzyme rubisco joins COX2​ to a five-carbon compound, ribulose bisphosphate.

Second, reduction: the unstable product splits into two three-carbon molecules, and the cycle uses ATP and NADPH to reduce them to a three-carbon sugar.

Third, regeneration: some of the three-carbon sugar, with ATP, rebuilds the five-carbon compound so a new turn can begin.

The final product

The product is a three-carbon sugar. Two of them join to form glucose. To build one glucose the cycle turns 6 times and fixes 6 COX2​. Every three turns give one net three-carbon sugar.

Darkness and blocked COX2​

When light is switched off the light reactions stop, so ATP and NADPH are not renewed. Calvin continues briefly on what is left, then runs out and stops. That is why it depends on light indirectly.

If COX2​ is blocked while light is present, Calvin stops and no longer uses ATP and NADPH, while the light reactions keep making them, so they build up in the chloroplast.

Laws and rules

Needed to build one glucose6COX2​+18ATP+12NADPH→glucoseThe cycle turns six times to build one glucose; each turn needs one COX2​, 3 ATP and 2 NADPH.

Worked examples

How many turns of the Calvin cycle build 3 glucose molecules, and how much ATP and NADPH are needed?
Show the solution
  1. One glucose needs 6 turns (each fixes one COX2​).
  2. Turns =6×3=18.
  3. Each turn uses 3 ATP and 2 NADPH.
  4. ATP =3×18=54 and NADPH =2×18=36.
Answer: 18 turns, 54 ATP and 36 NADPH.
COX2​ is blocked from an illuminated plant. What happens first to ATP and NADPH in the chloroplast?
Show the solution
  1. Without COX2​ the Calvin cycle stops.
  2. So it no longer uses ATP and NADPH.
  3. Light keeps making them in the light reactions.
Answer: They build up.
Why does the Calvin cycle stop soon after the light is switched off, though it does not use light directly?
Show the solution
  1. Calvin uses ATP and NADPH.
  2. These are made in the light reactions, which need light.
  3. Without light their supply runs out.
Answer: Because its supply of ATP and NADPH runs out.
Common mistakes
  • Saying Calvin "works in the dark" without limit. Correct: it stops once ATP and NADPH run out.
  • Thinking blocked COX2​ stops the light reactions at once. Correct: their products build up first.
  • Mixing rubisco with other enzymes. Rubisco joins COX2​ to the five-carbon compound.
Summary
  • Calvin occurs in the stroma and builds sugar from COX2​.
  • It needs ATP and NADPH from the light reactions.
  • Rubisco is the carbon-fixing enzyme.
  • The product is a three-carbon sugar, from which glucose is built.
  • Without light it stops because ATP and NADPH run out.

Aerobic Cellular Respiration

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Follow glucose through the four stages of respiration
  • Know where each stage occurs and what it makes
  • Count COX2​ and ATP
  • Understand chemiosmosis and the final electron acceptor

The big picture

Cellular respiration is a breakdown pathway that takes glucose apart and releases its energy, storing part of it in ATP. The balanced equation: glucose plus 6 oxygen gives 6 COX2​, 6 water and energy.

Stage 1: Glycolysis

It occurs in the cytoplasm and needs no oxygen. Glucose (6 carbons) splits into two pyruvate (3 carbons each). It makes 4 ATP and uses 2, so the net is 2 ATP, plus 2 NADH.

Stage 2: Pyruvate oxidation

Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion. It loses one carbon atom as COX2​, forming the two-carbon acetyl coenzyme A, together with NADH.

Stage 3: Krebs cycle

It occurs in the mitochondrial matrix. The acetyl is oxidised and releases two carbons as COX2​, and the cycle forms NADH, FADH2 and one ATP per turn.

So COX2​ is released in only two stages: pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs cycle. The electron transport chain does not make COX2​; it makes water.

Stage 4: Electron transport chain and chemiosmosis

At the inner membrane, NADH and FADH2 hand their electrons to a series of carriers. The chain uses the electrons' energy to pump protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space, where they collect at high concentration.

The protons then flow back into the matrix through ATP synthase, which releases their energy and builds ATP. This is chemiosmosis.

The final electron acceptor is oxygen: it joins the electrons and protons to make water. Without oxygen, electrons pile up and the chain stops.

The total

From one glucose: 2 ATP from glycolysis, 2 ATP from the Krebs cycle, and the rest from the chain, which oxidises the electron carriers collected earlier: 10 NADH and 2 FADH2 per glucose. The total is about 30 to 38 ATP (the figure differs between references), so most energy is made at the chain.

Laws and rules

Aerobic respiration equationCX6​HX12​OX6​+6OX2​​6COX2​+6HX2​OWith energy, part of which is stored in ATP. All the carbon leaves as COX2​.
Glycolysisglucose→2 pyruvate+2 ATP (net)+2 NADHIn the cytoplasm, without oxygen.

Worked examples

How many COX2​ molecules come from completely oxidising 5 glucose molecules? How many are from the Krebs cycle?
Show the solution
  1. Each glucose has 6 carbons, all becoming COX2​: 6 per glucose.
  2. Total =6×5=30.
  3. Pyruvate oxidation gives 2 per glucose, Krebs gives 4 per glucose.
  4. Krebs =4×5=20.
Answer: 30 COX2​ molecules; 20 from the Krebs cycle and 10 from pyruvate oxidation.
A cell runs glycolysis on 4 glucose molecules. How much net ATP and how many pyruvate form?
Show the solution
  1. Each glucose: two pyruvate, 4 ATP made and 2 used.
  2. Net per glucose =4−2=2 ATP.
  3. Net ATP =2×4=8 and pyruvate =2×4=8.
Answer: 8 net ATP and 8 pyruvate.
Where do protons collect before passing through ATP synthase? What is their final fate?
Show the solution
  1. The chain pumps them out of the matrix.
  2. They collect in the intermembrane space.
  3. They return through ATP synthase, making ATP, then join oxygen and electrons.
Answer: In the intermembrane space; they end up in water with oxygen.
Common mistakes
  • Saying COX2​ comes from the chain. Correct: it comes from pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs cycle; the chain makes water.
  • Thinking glycolysis gives 4 net ATP. Correct: 4 are made and 2 used, so net 2.
  • Saying glycolysis needs oxygen or occurs in the mitochondrion. Correct: cytoplasm, no oxygen.
  • Forgetting oxygen is the final electron acceptor and water results from it.
Summary
  • Glycolysis is in the cytoplasm: 2 pyruvate and 2 net ATP.
  • Pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs cycle are the sources of COX2​.
  • One glucose gives 6 COX2​ when fully oxidised.
  • Chemiosmosis makes most ATP, with oxygen the final acceptor.
  • The total is about 30 to 38 ATP per glucose.

Fermentation and Comparing the Two Processes

What you will learn
  • Explain why fermentation happens and what it renews
  • Tell lactic fermentation from alcoholic fermentation
  • Compare photosynthesis with respiration
  • Predict what happens without oxygen, or at night

Why does fermentation happen?

When oxygen is absent the electron transport chain stops, so NADX+ is not renewed from NADH, and glycolysis stops because it needs NADX+. The Krebs cycle is also disrupted because it needs NADX+ too.

Fermentation solves this: it turns NADH back into NADX+, so glycolysis continues and gives 2 ATP without oxygen. That is its main purpose; it makes no extra ATP.

Two kinds of fermentation

Lactic fermentation: in human muscle when oxygen is short, pyruvate becomes lactic acid. Its build-up causes muscle tiredness and soreness.

Alcoholic fermentation: in yeast, pyruvate becomes ethanol and COX2​. The gas is trapped in dough and makes it rise.

Photosynthesis versus respiration

Photosynthesis stores light energy in glucose, uses COX2​ and water, and releases oxygen. Respiration releases that energy into ATP, uses oxygen, and releases COX2​ and water. The products of one are the inputs of the other.

Photosynthesis needs light, so it stops at night. Respiration runs day and night in every living cell to supply ATP, including plant leaves.

  • Place: photosynthesis in the chloroplasts; respiration in the cytoplasm and mitochondria.
  • Light: photosynthesis needs it; respiration does not.
  • Energy: photosynthesis is a building pathway that absorbs energy; respiration is a breakdown pathway that releases it.

Laws and rules

Lactic fermentationglucose→2 lactic acid+2 ATPIn muscle when oxygen is short.
Alcoholic fermentationglucose→2 ethanol+2 COX2​+2 ATPIn yeast; the gas makes dough rise.

Worked examples

Yeast ferments 5 glucose molecules. How much ethanol, COX2​ and ATP form?
Show the solution
  1. Each glucose gives 2 ethanol, 2 COX2​ and 2 ATP.
  2. Ethanol =2×5=10.
  3. Likewise COX2​ =10 and ATP =10.
Answer: 10 ethanol, 10 COX2​ and 10 ATP.
Compare the ATP from 10 glucose by fermentation and by complete aerobic respiration (about 30 per glucose).
Show the solution
  1. Fermentation: 2×10=20 ATP.
  2. Aerobic: 30×10=300 ATP.
  3. 300÷20=15.
Answer: Aerobic gives about 15 times more (about 300 against 20).
In the dark of night, which process continues in the leaves of a plant in a sealed room: photosynthesis or respiration? What happens to the OX2​ and COX2​ levels in the room?
Show the solution
  1. Photosynthesis needs light, so it stops.
  2. Respiration needs no light and always supplies ATP, so it continues.
  3. Respiration uses OX2​ and releases COX2​, and no photosynthesis replaces them.
Answer: Cellular respiration only; OX2​ falls and COX2​ rises in the room.
Common mistakes
  • Saying fermentation aims to make lots of ATP. Correct: its purpose is to renew NADX+ so glycolysis can continue.
  • Mixing the products of yeast (ethanol and COX2​) with those of muscle (lactic acid).
  • Thinking plants do not respire at night. Correct: they respire day and night.
  • Saying respiration stores energy and photosynthesis releases it. It is the other way round.
Summary
  • Fermentation renews NADX+ so glycolysis continues without oxygen.
  • Muscle makes lactic acid; yeast makes ethanol and COX2​.
  • Fermentation gives only 2 ATP per glucose.
  • Photosynthesis stores energy; respiration releases it.
  • Respiration runs day and night; photosynthesis stops at night.
