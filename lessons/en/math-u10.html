Antiderivatives

What you will learn
  • Define an antiderivative and check it by differentiating
  • Understand why a function has infinitely many antiderivatives
  • Find the antiderivative that satisfies a given condition

What is an antiderivative?

Differentiation goes from f(x) to f′(x). An antiderivative goes backwards: if F′(x)=f(x), then F(x) is an antiderivative of f(x).

For example x2 is an antiderivative of 2x because the derivative of x2 is 2x.

Why is there not just one?

The derivative of any constant is zero, so x2, x2+5 and x2−7 all have the same derivative 2x. They are all antiderivatives of 2x.

The curves differ only in height, and their slope is the same at every x. So we write F(x)+C, where C is an arbitrary constant.

Finding the constant C

If we are given a point on the curve or a value of the function, we substitute it to get C, and then exactly one antiderivative remains.

Steps: find the general antiderivative, substitute the given condition, then solve a simple equation for C.

Laws and rules

Definition of an antiderivativeF′(x)=f(x)F(x) is an antiderivative of f(x). All antiderivatives are F(x)+C where C is a constant.

Worked examples

Is x3+7 an antiderivative of f(x)=3x2?
Show the solution
  1. Differentiate the proposed function: the derivative of x3 is 3x2.
  2. The derivative of the number 7 is zero.
  3. So the total derivative is 3x2, which is f(x).
Answer: Yes, it is an antiderivative.
If f′(x)=4x−3 and f(2)=5, find f(x).
Show the solution
  1. We need a function whose derivative is 4x−3: f(x)=2x2−3x+C.
  2. Substitute x=2: f(2)=2(4)−3(2)+C=8−6+C=2+C.
  3. Set equal to the given value: 2+C=5, so C=3.
Answer: f(x)=2x2−3x+3
If f′(x)=3x2−2 and f(1)=4, what is f(2)?
Show the solution
  1. General function: f(x)=x3−2x+C.
  2. Substitute x=1: 1−2+C=4, so C=5.
  3. The function is f(x)=x3−2x+5.
  4. Substitute x=2: 8−4+5=9.
Answer: f(2)=9
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting the constant C and giving only one antiderivative, when there are infinitely many.
  • Mixing up the derivative and the antiderivative: the antiderivative is the one that you differentiate to get the given function.
  • Substituting into f′ instead of f when finding C; the condition f(1)=4 is about the function itself.
  • Substituting the condition before finding the general function; first find the function with C, then substitute.
Summary
  • An antiderivative of f is a function whose derivative is f.
  • The antiderivatives of one function are many and differ only by a constant: F(x)+C.
  • To check your answer, differentiate it and compare with the given function.
  • A condition such as f(1)=4 fixes the single value of C.

Indefinite integrals and the power rule

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Write an indefinite integral with the correct notation
  • Use the power rule and the sum and constant-multiple rules
  • Simplify before integrating (expand, divide, roots)

What the symbol means

The symbol ∫f(x)dx means "all antiderivatives of f(x)". The dx tells us the variable is x.

The answer always contains the constant C because an indefinite integral gives a whole family of functions.

The power rule

To integrate xn, add one to the exponent and divide by the new exponent. This undoes the differentiation rule, which brings the exponent down and subtracts one.

The rule works for every n=−1 (while ∫x1​dx=ln∣x∣+C). It also works for roots and fractions once written with exponents: x​=x21​ and x21​=x−2.

Term by term

The integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals, and a constant multiplier moves outside. There is no simple rule for a product or a quotient, so expand or divide first, then integrate each term.

Laws and rules

Power rule∫xndx=n+1xn+1​+Cwhere n=−1 and C is the constant of integration.
Integral of a constant∫kdx=kx+Ck is a constant.
Sum and constant multiple∫(kf(x)±g(x))dx=k∫f(x)dx±∫g(x)dxIntegrate each term separately and take out the constant multiplier.

Worked examples

Find ∫(8x3−6x2+5)dx.
Show the solution
  1. Integrate each term: ∫8x3dx=48x4​=2x4.
  2. ∫6x2dx=36x3​=2x3.
  3. ∫5dx=5x.
  4. Add the terms and the constant.
Answer: 2x4−2x3+5x+C
Find ∫x−3x2−9​dx.
Show the solution
  1. Factor the numerator: x2−9=(x−3)(x+3).
  2. Cancel the common factor: the result is x+3 (for x=3).
  3. ∫(x+3)dx=2x2​+3x.
Answer: 2x2​+3x+C
Find ∫(3x​−x22​)dx.
Show the solution
  1. Write with exponents: 3x21​−2x−2.
  2. First term: 3×23​x23​​=3×32​x23​=2x23​.
  3. Second term: −2×−1x−1​=2x−1=x2​.
Answer: 2x23​+x2​+C
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting to divide by the new exponent: ∫3x2dx is not 3x3; it is x3 because 33​=1.
  • Differentiating instead of integrating: multiplying the exponent by the coefficient (giving 6x from 3x2) is the differentiation step; integration raises the exponent and divides by it.
  • Writing ∫x21​dx as 3x31​; the correct way is to rewrite as x−2, and the result is −x1​.
  • Integrating the numerator and denominator separately: there is no rule that makes the integral of a quotient equal the quotient of the integrals, so simplify the fraction first.
Summary
  • Add one to the exponent and divide by it: ∫xndx=n+1xn+1​+C.
  • Turn roots and fractions into exponents before integrating.
  • Expand brackets and cancel fractions before you start.
  • Never forget C in an indefinite integral, and check by differentiating.

Approximating area with rectangles

What you will learn
  • Estimate the area under a curve with rectangles
  • Tell the right-endpoint and left-endpoint methods apart
  • Understand why more rectangles give a value closer to the true area

The idea

We cannot easily find the area of a curved region, but we do know the area of a rectangle. So we split the interval into equal-width pieces and draw a rectangle over each piece.

The area of each rectangle is width times height, and the total is the approximate area (such sums are called Riemann sums).

Where do we take the height?

For an increasing function the right endpoint gives a value that is too big and the left endpoint one that is too small. For a decreasing function it is the other way round.

  • Right endpoint: the height is the value of the function at the right end of the piece.
  • Left endpoint: the height is the value of the function at the left end of the piece.
  • Midpoint: the height is the function value at the middle of the piece; it is usually more accurate than either endpoint.
  • Width of each rectangle = length of the interval divided by the number of rectangles.

More rectangles

As the number of rectangles grows, each gets narrower and the total gets closer to the true area. With infinitely many rectangles the total equals the exact area. This is the idea of the definite integral.

Laws and rules

Approximate areaarea≈width×(sum of heights)width = number of rectanglesinterval length​, and each height is the function value at the chosen endpoint.

Worked examples

Approximate the area under y=x2 from x=0 to x=4 using four rectangles of width 1 and right endpoints.
Show the solution
  1. The right endpoints are 1,2,3,4.
  2. The heights are 1,4,9,16.
  3. Approximate area: 1×(1+4+9+16)=30.
Answer: 30 square units (larger than the true area 364​)
Approximate the area under y=2x+1 from x=0 to x=3 using three rectangles of width 1 and left endpoints.
Show the solution
  1. The left endpoints are 0,1,2.
  2. The heights are 1,3,5.
  3. Area: 1×(1+3+5)=9.
Answer: 9 square units (the true area is 12)
Approximate the area under y=4−x2 from x=0 to x=2 using two rectangles of width 1, once with right endpoints and once with left endpoints.
Show the solution
  1. Right: x=1,2, heights 3,0, sum 3.
  2. Left: x=0,1, heights 4,3, sum 7.
  3. The function is decreasing, so right is too small and left is too big.
Answer: Right 3, left 7 (true area 316​)
Common mistakes
  • Using the wrong endpoint: read "right" or "left" carefully and list the x-values for the heights.
  • Forgetting to multiply the sum of heights by the width.
  • Computing the width wrongly; width = interval length divided by the number of rectangles, not a function value.
  • Thinking more rectangles move the answer away or toward infinity; they bring it closer to the true area.
Summary
  • Split the interval into equal-width rectangles and add their areas.
  • The height is the function value at the right or left endpoint, as asked.
  • For an increasing function, right is too big and left is too small.
  • More rectangles bring the total closer to the exact area.

Definite integrals and the Fundamental Theorem

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Evaluate a definite integral by substituting into an antiderivative
  • Use the properties of definite integrals
  • Understand both parts of the Fundamental Theorem: integration and differentiation undo each other

What is a definite integral?

The definite integral ∫ab​f(x)dx is a number (not a function) that represents the signed area between the curve and the x-axis from x=a to x=b. It is the limit of the total area of very many rectangles.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

To evaluate a definite integral, find an antiderivative F(x), compute its value at the upper limit, and subtract its value at the lower limit. We do not need C because it cancels in the subtraction.

An important consequence: ∫ab​f′(x)dx=f(b)−f(a); integrating a rate of change gives the total change.

The other part of the theorem: if F(x)=∫ax​f(t)dt then F′(x)=f(x); differentiation undoes integration. For example the derivative of ∫1x​(t2+3t)dt is x2+3x, with no integral to compute.

Properties of definite integrals

For example ∫−33​(x3+2)dx=0+2×6=12 because x3 is odd.

With an absolute value, split the interval where the sign changes and integrate each piece.

  • Swapping the limits changes the sign: ∫ba​=−∫ab​.
  • Splitting: ∫ac​=∫ab​+∫bc​.
  • A constant multiplier moves out, a sum splits, and the integral of a constant k is k(b−a).
  • If the function is odd (f(−x)=−f(x)), its integral from −b to b is zero; if it is even (f(−x)=f(x)), it is 2∫0b​f(x)dx.

Laws and rules

Fundamental Theorem∫ab​f(x)dx=F(b)−F(a)F(x) is any antiderivative of f(x).
Derivative of an integraldxd​∫ax​f(t)dt=f(x)The derivative of an integral whose upper limit is x is the integrand itself with x in place of t.
Total change∫ab​f′(x)dx=f(b)−f(a)The integral of the derivative gives the difference of the function values at the limits.
Swapping limits∫ba​f(x)dx=−∫ab​f(x)dxSwapping the limits changes the sign.
Splitting∫ac​f(x)dx=∫ab​f(x)dx+∫bc​f(x)dxb is a point between a and c.

Worked examples

Evaluate ∫13​(3x2−2x)dx.
Show the solution
  1. Antiderivative: F(x)=x3−x2.
  2. At the upper limit 3: 27−9=18.
  3. At the lower limit 1: 1−1=0.
  4. Subtract: 18−0=18.
Answer: 18
If ∫05​f(x)dx=8 and ∫02​f(x)dx=3, find ∫25​f(x)dx and then ∫05​(3f(x)−2)dx.
Show the solution
  1. Splitting: ∫05​=∫02​+∫25​, so 8=3+∫25​ and ∫25​=5.
  2. ∫05​3f(x)dx=3×8=24.
  3. ∫05​2dx=2×5=10.
  4. Result: 24−10=14.
Answer: The first is 5 and the second is 14
Evaluate ∫02​∣x−1∣dx.
Show the solution
  1. The sign changes at x=1: from 0 to 1 we have ∣x−1∣=1−x, and from 1 to 2 it equals x−1.
  2. ∫01​(1−x)dx=[x−2x2​]01​=1−21​=21​.
  3. ∫12​(x−1)dx=[2x2​−x]12​=0−(21​−1)=21​.
  4. Total: 21​+21​=1.
Answer: 1
Common mistakes
  • Subtracting in the wrong order: take the antiderivative at the upper limit and subtract its value at the lower limit, or the sign flips (the same happens when the limits are swapped: ∫ba​=−∫ab​).
  • Adding the constant C in a definite integral; it is not needed because it cancels.
  • Evaluating the integral and then differentiating when asked for the derivative of ∫1x​f(t)dt; the theorem gives the answer directly: f(x).
  • Integrating ∣x−1∣ over the whole interval without splitting; you must split where the sign changes.
Summary
  • Definite integral = antiderivative at the upper limit minus antiderivative at the lower limit.
  • It does not need the constant C.
  • ∫ab​f′(x)dx=f(b)−f(a).
  • Useful properties: swapping limits flips the sign, and splitting adds the pieces.
  • The derivative of ∫ax​f(t)dt is f(x).

Area under a curve

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Find the area between a curve and the x-axis using integration
  • Handle the part that lies below the axis
  • Find integration limits from the points where the curve meets the axis

Area and integration

If f(x)≥0 on the interval from a to b, the area between the curve and the x-axis equals ∫ab​f(x)dx.

When the curve is below the axis

Where f(x)<0 the integral gives a negative number, but an area is never negative. So we take the absolute value of the integral of each part.

If the curve crosses the axis inside the interval, find the crossing by solving f(x)=0, split the interval, compute each part separately and add the positive values.

An area fixed by two crossing points

When a question says "enclosed by the curve and the x-axis" with no limits, the limits are the roots of f(x)=0 that enclose the region.

Laws and rules

Area under a curveA=∫ab​∣f(x)∣dxSplit the interval at the zeros, compute each part, take its absolute value and add.

Worked examples

Find the area enclosed by y=x2 and the x-axis from x=0 to x=3.
Show the solution
  1. The curve is above the axis, so integrate directly.
  2. ∫03​x2dx=[3x3​]03​.
  3. 327​−0=9.
Answer: 9 square units
Find the area enclosed by y=4−x2 and the x-axis.
Show the solution
  1. No limits are given, so find them where the curve meets the axis: 4−x2=0, so x=−2 or x=2.
  2. At x=0 the value is 4>0, so the curve is above the axis between the roots and we integrate directly.
  3. Antiderivative: 4x−3x3​.
  4. At 2: 8−38​=316​.
  5. At −2: −8+38​=−316​.
  6. Subtract: 316​−(−316​)=332​.
Answer: 332​ square units
Find the area enclosed by y=x2−1 and the x-axis from x=0 to x=2.
Show the solution
  1. x2−1=0 at x=1; the curve is below the axis from 0 to 1 and above it from 1 to 2.
  2. ∫01​(x2−1)dx=[3x3​−x]01​=31​−1=−32​, so the area is 32​.
  3. ∫12​(x2−1)dx=(38​−2)−(31​−1)=32​+32​=34​.
  4. Total: 32​+34​=2.
Answer: 2 square units
Common mistakes
  • Treating area as negative: area is always positive, while the integral can be negative below the axis.
  • Integrating the whole function in one go when the curve crosses the axis; the positive and negative parts cancel (for example ∫−11​x3dx=0).
  • Not finding the limits from the roots of the function when none are given.
  • Slips when substituting a negative lower limit: (−2)3=−8, not 8, and subtracting a negative number means adding.
Summary
  • Above the axis: area = ∫ab​f(x)dx.
  • Below the axis: the integral is negative, so take its absolute value.
  • If the curve crosses inside the interval, split at the root and add the areas.
  • If no limits are given, they are the roots of the function.

Area between two curves

What you will learn
  • Find the intersection points to get the limits
  • Decide which curve is on top
  • Compute the area by integrating the difference

The idea

The area between two curves is the area under the upper curve minus the area under the lower curve. So we integrate the difference between the two functions.

Steps

The result is always positive because we subtract the lower from the upper, and the rule stays valid even if part of the region lies below the x-axis.

If the curves cross inside the given interval, split the interval at the crossing point and swap which curve is upper and which is lower in the other part.

  • Solve f(x)=g(x) to find the intersection points; these are the limits.
  • Decide which function is higher on the interval by substituting a value between the limits.
  • Integrate (upper minus lower) from the smaller limit to the larger.

Laws and rules

Area between two curvesA=∫ab​(f(x)−g(x))dxf(x)≥g(x) on the interval from a to b, and a and b are the intersection points.

Worked examples

Find the area enclosed by y=x and y=x2.
Show the solution
  1. Set equal: x=x2, so x(1−x)=0, giving x=0 or x=1.
  2. Between 0 and 1 substitute x=21​: 21​>41​, so the line is on top.
  3. ∫01​(x−x2)dx=[2x2​−3x3​]01​=21​−31​=61​.
Answer: 61​ square unit
Find the area enclosed by y=2x and y=x2.
Show the solution
  1. Set equal: x2=2x, so x(x−2)=0, giving x=0 or x=2.
  2. At x=1: 2>1, so the line is on top.
  3. ∫02​(2x−x2)dx=[x2−3x3​]02​=4−38​=34​.
Answer: 34​ square units
Find the area enclosed by y=x+2 and y=x2.
Show the solution
  1. Set equal: x2=x+2, so x2−x−2=0, i.e. (x−2)(x+1)=0, giving x=−1 or x=2.
  2. At x=0: 2>0, so the line is on top.
  3. An antiderivative of the difference: 2x2​+2x−3x3​.
  4. At 2: 2+4−38​=310​. At −1: 21​−2+31​=−67​.
  5. Subtract: 310​+67​=620​+67​=627​=29​.
Answer: 29​ square units
Common mistakes
  • Reversing the order (lower minus upper), which gives a negative area.
  • Integrating each curve with different limits; use the same two intersection points for both.
  • Guessing the limits from a sketch instead of solving the equation for the exact intersection points.
  • Not checking which curve is higher; the order can swap on different intervals.
Summary
  • Solve f(x)=g(x) to get the two limits.
  • Find the upper curve by testing a point between the limits.
  • Area = integral of (upper minus lower).
  • The result is always positive.

Distance and speed by integration

What you will learn
  • Find distance from a velocity function
  • Tell displacement and distance apart
  • Interpret the area under a velocity-time graph

Velocity and distance

Velocity is the rate of change of position with respect to time. So the integral of velocity over a time interval gives the change in position, and if the velocity stays positive it is the distance travelled.

Geometrically, the distance is the area under the velocity-time graph.

Displacement and distance

Displacement =∫v(t)dt keeps direction (backward motion is subtracted), while the total distance is ∫∣v(t)∣dt.

If the sign of the velocity changes, the object turned around at the moment v=0; split the interval there.

From acceleration to velocity

In the same way, the integral of the acceleration a(t) gives the change in velocity, so v(t)=∫a(t)dt and the constant is fixed by the initial velocity.

Laws and rules

Displacementdisplacement=∫t1​t2​​v(t)dtVelocity in metres per second and time in seconds give displacement in metres.
Total distancedistance=∫t1​t2​​∣v(t)∣dtIt equals the displacement if the velocity never changes sign.

Worked examples

The velocity of an object is v=2t+1 m/s where t is in seconds. What distance does it cover in the first 3 seconds?
Show the solution
  1. The velocity is always positive, so the distance =∫03​(2t+1)dt.
  2. Antiderivative: t2+t.
  3. At 3: 9+3=12, and at 0: 0.
Answer: 12 m
The velocity of an object is v=6−2t m/s. What distance does it cover in the first 3 seconds?
Show the solution
  1. v=0 at t=3, so the velocity is positive before that.
  2. ∫03​(6−2t)dt=[6t−t2]03​.
  3. 18−9=9.
Answer: 9 m
The velocity of an object is v=t−2 m/s. Find the displacement and total distance from t=0 to t=4.
Show the solution
  1. Antiderivative: 2t2​−2t.
  2. Displacement: (8−8)−0=0.
  3. v=0 at t=2. From 0 to 2: (2−4)−0=−2, so the distance is 2.
  4. From 2 to 4: 0−(2−4)=2, so the distance is 2.
  5. Total distance: 2+2=4.
Answer: Displacement 0 m and total distance 4 m
Common mistakes
  • Using displacement instead of distance when the velocity changes sign.
  • Forgetting that the lower time limit may not be zero; substitute both limits.
  • Mixing units: velocity in metres per second and time in seconds give metres.
  • Multiplying velocity by time directly when the velocity changes; that works only for constant velocity.
Summary
  • Distance = integral of velocity over the time interval.
  • It is the area under the velocity-time graph.
  • Displacement subtracts backward motion; distance adds it.
  • When the velocity changes sign, split at v=0.
