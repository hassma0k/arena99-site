SI Base Units and Prefixes

What you will learn
  • Know the SI base units used in motion
  • Convert units with the prefixes kilo, centi and milli

Why one system?

To measure is to compare a quantity with a standard unit. If everyone used their own unit, no two people would agree, so scientists agreed on the International System of Units (SI).

A base quantity is not built from others. In motion we use three: length (metre), mass (kilogram) and time (second).

  • Length: metre (m)
  • Mass: kilogram (kg)
  • Time: second (s)
  • Other base units: ampere for current, kelvin for temperature, mole for amount of substance, candela for luminous intensity

Prefixes shorten the zeros

A prefix placed before a unit multiplies it by a power of ten, so very large and very small numbers are easy to write.

  • kilo = 103, so 1 kilometre is 1000 metres
  • centi = 10−2, so 1 metre is 100 centimetres
  • milli = 10−3, so 1 gram is 1000 milligrams
  • mega = 106, a million times; micro = 10−6, a millionth
  • nano = 10−9, a billionth, so 1 metre is 109 nanometres

Converting without mistakes

Ask: is the unit I want smaller or larger? A smaller unit needs more of them, so multiply; a larger unit needs fewer, so divide.

Example: a metre is larger than a centimetre, so going from metres to centimetres we multiply by 100.

Laws and rules

Converting with prefixes1km=1000m,1m=100cm=1000mmkm: kilometre, m: metre, cm: centimetre, mm: millimetre, mg: milligram, g: gram.

Worked examples

Convert 3.5km to metres.
Show the solution
  1. A kilometre is larger than a metre, so there are more metres.
  2. Multiply: 3.5×1000=3500.
Answer: 3500m
Convert 250mg to grams.
Show the solution
  1. A gram is 1000 times larger than a milligram, so divide by 1000.
  2. 250÷1000=0.25.
Answer: 0.25g
How many centimetres are in 0.6km?
Show the solution
  1. km to m: 0.6×1000=600m.
  2. m to cm: 600×100=60000.
  3. Check: 0.6km is 600m and each metre is 100cm, so the result is sensible.
Answer: 60000cm
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up "multiply" and "divide": first ask whether the target unit is smaller (number grows) or larger (number shrinks).
  • Thinking the gram is a base unit: the SI base unit of mass is the kilogram; the gram is one thousandth of it.
  • Skipping a middle step: from km to cm go through metres first.
Summary
  • Base units for motion: metre, kilogram, second.
  • Kilo means 1000, centi means one hundredth, milli means one thousandth, nano means one billionth.
  • A smaller unit means a bigger number.

Distance and Displacement: Scalar vs Vector

What you will learn
  • Tell scalar quantities from vector quantities
  • Find distance and displacement for a straight-line path

Scalar or vector?

A scalar needs only a size and a unit, such as mass 5kg or time 3s. A vector needs size and direction together: "moved 5m" is incomplete, "5m east" is complete.

In motion, distance, time, mass and speed are scalars. Displacement, velocity and acceleration are vectors.

Distance

Distance is the length of the path the object actually travelled. It is never negative and never decreases, because every step is added whatever its direction.

Displacement

Displacement is the change in position: from the start point to the end point, ignoring the route. We find it by subtracting the starting position from the final position.

On a line we choose a positive direction (east or right). Displacement is negative if the object ends up on the other side. It can even be zero while the distance is large, if the object returns to its start. The size of the displacement is never greater than the distance.

Laws and rules

Displacementdisplacement=xf​−xi​xf​: final position, xi​: initial position, in metres. The sign gives direction.
Distancedistance=sum of all segment lengthsIn metres, never negative.

Worked examples

A man walks 10m right, then 4m left, then 2m right. Find the distance and the displacement.
Show the solution
  1. Distance: add all segments, 10+4+2=16m.
  2. Take right as positive. Final position =10−4+2=8m.
  3. Displacement =8−0=8m to the right.
Answer: Distance 16m, displacement 8m right
A runner does one full lap of a 400m track and returns to the start. Find the distance and the displacement.
Show the solution
  1. Distance is the whole path: 400m.
  2. Final position equals initial position, so displacement =0.
Answer: Distance 400m, displacement 0
A car drives 30km east then 50km west. Find the distance and displacement (east is positive).
Show the solution
  1. Distance =30+50=80km.
  2. Final position =30−50=−20km.
  3. Displacement =−20−0=−20km, that is 20km west.
Answer: Distance 80km, displacement 20km west
Common mistakes
  • Treating distance and displacement as the same: distance counts the whole road, displacement looks only at start and end.
  • Believing displacement cannot be zero once the object has moved: if it returns to the start, it is zero.
  • Writing displacement with no direction: it is a vector, so give a direction or a sign.
Summary
  • Scalar: size only. Vector: size and direction.
  • Distance is a scalar and adds the path length.
  • Displacement is a vector: final position minus initial position.
  • Displacement can be zero or negative; distance cannot.

Speed and Velocity

What you will learn
  • Find average speed and average velocity and tell them apart
  • Understand constant speed
  • Convert between km/h and m/s

Speed (scalar)

Average speed tells how many metres an object covers each second on average. We divide the total distance by the total time. It is a scalar and is never negative.

Velocity (vector)

Velocity depends on displacement, not distance: divide displacement by time. It has a direction and can be negative. So an object can have an average speed of 4m/s and an average velocity of zero, if it returns to its start.

Constant speed means equal distances in equal times. Velocity is constant only if the size and the direction both stay the same.

Instantaneous and average

Instantaneous speed is the speed at one moment, like the number on a car speedometer. Average speed sums up the whole trip in one number, even if the speed changed along the way.

If the speed changes steadily (constant acceleration), the average velocity is the mean of the two velocities: add the initial and final values and divide by two. This rule fails when acceleration is not constant.

Converting units

One kilometre per hour is 1000 metres in 3600 seconds. So 1m/s equals 3.6km/h. To go from m/s to km/h multiply by 3.6; to go back, divide.

Laws and rules

Average speedaverage speed=total timetotal distance​Distance in metres, time in seconds, so speed is in metres per second.
Average velocityaverage velocity=total timedisplacement​Displacement in metres; the sign of the result gives the direction.
Distance at constant speeddistance=speed×timeDistance in metres, speed in m/s, time in seconds. Hence time =speeddistance​.
Average velocity under constant accelerationaverage velocity=2v0​+v​In m/s; valid only when the acceleration is constant. v0​: initial velocity, v: final velocity.

Worked examples

A bus travels 150km in 2h. What is its average speed, and how far does it go in 3h at the same speed?
Show the solution
  1. Distance 150km, time 2h.
  2. 2150​=75km/h.
  3. Distance =75×3=225km.
Answer: 75km/h and 225km
Convert 72km/h to m/s, then 15m/s to km/h.
Show the solution
  1. km/h to m/s: divide by 3.6: 72÷3.6=20.
  2. m/s to km/h: multiply: 15×3.6=54.
Answer: 20m/s and 54km/h
A boy runs 100m east in 20s, then 100m west in 30s. Find his average speed and average velocity.
Show the solution
  1. Total distance =100+100=200m, total time =20+30=50s.
  2. Average speed =50200​=4m/s.
  3. Total displacement =0 because he is back at the start.
  4. Average velocity =500​=0.
Answer: Average speed 4m/s, average velocity 0
Common mistakes
  • Always averaging two speeds by adding and halving: that works only under constant acceleration. If a car goes 60km at 30km/h (2 h) then 60km at 60km/h (1 h), the average is 3120​=40km/h, not 45. Divide total distance by total time.
  • Using distance in velocity: velocity uses displacement.
  • Flipping the conversion factor: going from m/s to km/h the number gets bigger, because km/h is the smaller unit.
Summary
  • Average speed = distance ÷ time.
  • Average velocity = displacement ÷ time.
  • Round trip: speed is positive, velocity is zero.
  • 1m/s=3.6km/h.
  • Under constant acceleration: average velocity = (initial + final) ÷ 2.

Graphs of Motion

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Read velocity from the slope of a position–time graph
  • Read acceleration from the slope of a velocity–time graph and displacement from the area under it

Position–time graph

Time is on the horizontal axis and position on the vertical axis. A sloped straight line means constant velocity, a horizontal line means the object is at rest, and a curve means the velocity is changing.

The slope is the change in position divided by the change in time, which is exactly the velocity. A positive slope means motion in the positive direction; a negative slope means the opposite.

The slope between two points gives the average velocity between them. If the graph is curved, the velocity at one moment is the slope of the tangent to the curve at that moment.

Velocity–time graph

Here the vertical axis is velocity. A horizontal line means constant velocity and zero acceleration. A sloped line means constant acceleration, and its slope is the acceleration.

The area between the line and the time axis equals the displacement. Split the shape into triangles and rectangles and add their areas.

Area above the time axis is positive displacement and below it is negative displacement, because a negative velocity means motion against the positive direction. For the total distance, add all the areas without signs. If the velocity is positive and the slope is negative, the object is slowing down.

Reading a graph fast

Remember these four rules; they solve most graph questions.

  • Slope of position–time = velocity
  • Slope of velocity–time = acceleration
  • Area under velocity–time = displacement
  • Where the line crosses the time axis on velocity–time, the object is momentarily at rest

Laws and rules

Velocity from slopev=ΔtΔx​From a position–time graph. Δx in metres, Δt in seconds, v in m/s.
Acceleration from slopea=ΔtΔv​From a velocity–time graph, in m/s2.

Worked examples

On a position–time graph the object is at 2m at 1s and at 10m at 5s. What is its velocity?
Show the solution
  1. Change in position =10−2=8m.
  2. Change in time =5−1=4s.
  3. v=48​=2m/s.
Answer: 2m/s
In the figure the velocity rises from 0 to 6m/s in 3s, then stays at 6m/s until 7s. Find the acceleration in the first 3s and the total displacement.
Show the solution
  1. a=36−0​=2m/s2.
  2. Triangle area (0 to 3s) =21​×3×6=9m.
  3. Rectangle area (3 to 7s, width 4s) =4×6=24m.
  4. Displacement =9+24=33m.
Answer: a=2m/s2, displacement 33m
A car slows steadily from 10m/s to 0 in 5s. Find the acceleration and the distance covered.
Show the solution
  1. a=50−10​=−2m/s2; the negative sign means slowing down.
  2. The shape is a triangle with base 5s and height 10m/s.
  3. Area =21​×5×10=25m.
Answer: a=−2m/s2, distance 25m
Common mistakes
  • Mixing the two graphs: the slope of position–time is velocity; the slope of velocity–time is acceleration.
  • Thinking a horizontal line on position–time means a large constant speed: it means the object is at rest, velocity zero.
  • Reading a horizontal line on velocity–time as rest: it is motion at constant velocity; rest is at velocity zero.
  • Using base times height for a triangle: the area is half of that.
Summary
  • Slope on position–time is velocity.
  • Slope on velocity–time is acceleration.
  • Area under velocity–time is displacement.
  • Horizontal line: rest on position–time, constant velocity on velocity–time.

Acceleration and the Equations of Motion

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Define acceleration and decide its sign
  • Tell velocity from acceleration
  • Choose the right equation of motion and solve with it

What is acceleration?

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity with time. If a car speeds up from 5 to 20m/s in 5s, it gains 3m/s every second, so its acceleration is 3m/s2.

Acceleration is a vector, so it has a sign. We choose a positive direction and compare with it.

Sign of acceleration

If acceleration points the same way as velocity, the object speeds up. If it points the opposite way, the object slows down. The sign alone does not decide this; its relation to the sign of the velocity does.

For example, velocity +10 with acceleration −2 is slowing down; velocity −10 with acceleration −2 is speeding up in the negative direction.

When acceleration is opposite to velocity we call it deceleration and quote its size as a positive number: "slowing at 4m/s2".

Velocity and acceleration are different

Velocity says where and how fast the object moves; acceleration says how the velocity changes. Velocity can be zero for an instant while acceleration is not, like a ball at the top of its flight. Velocity can be large while acceleration is zero if it is constant.

Equations of motion (constant acceleration)

When acceleration is constant, three equations link time, displacement and the two velocities. Write the knowns and the unknown first, then choose the equation that lacks the quantity you neither know nor need.

  • No displacement in the question: use the first equation (velocity and time)
  • No time in the question: use the third equation (velocity and displacement)
  • No final velocity: use the second equation (displacement and time)

Laws and rules

Accelerationa=tv−v0​​v: final velocity (m/s), v0​: initial velocity (m/s), t: time (s), a: acceleration (m/s2).
First equationv=v0​+atLinks velocity and time.
Second equationΔx=v0​t+21​at2Δx: displacement (m). Links displacement and time.
Third equationv2=v02​+2aΔxLinks velocity and displacement with no time.
Average of the two velocitiesΔx=2v+v0​​tValid when the acceleration is constant.

Worked examples

A car speeds up from 5m/s to 20m/s in 5s. What is its acceleration?
Show the solution
  1. Change in velocity =20−5=15m/s.
  2. a=515​=3m/s2.
Answer: 3m/s2
An object starts from rest with constant acceleration 2m/s2. Find its velocity and displacement after 6s.
Show the solution
  1. From rest: v0​=0.
  2. v=0+2×6=12m/s.
  3. Δx=0+21​×2×62=36m.
  4. Check with the average: 212+0​×6=36m.
Answer: Velocity 12m/s, displacement 36m
A car moving at 20m/s brakes to a stop after covering 50m. Find its acceleration and the stopping time.
Show the solution
  1. Knowns: v0​=20, v=0, Δx=50. Time is not given, so use the third equation.
  2. 0=202+2a(50), that is 0=400+100a.
  3. a=−4m/s2 (negative because it slows down).
  4. Time: t=−40−20​=5s.
  5. Check: Δx=220+0​×5=50m.
Answer: a=−4m/s2, time 5s
Common mistakes
  • Thinking negative acceleration always means slowing down: the object slows only if acceleration is opposite to velocity.
  • Thinking zero velocity means zero acceleration: at the top of a thrown ball the velocity is zero but the acceleration is not.
  • Forgetting that v0​=0 when starting from rest, or v=0 when stopping.
  • Using the equations of motion when acceleration is not constant.
Summary
  • Acceleration = change in velocity ÷ time.
  • Acceleration and velocity agreeing: speeding up. Opposite: slowing down.
  • Zero velocity does not mean zero acceleration.
  • Pick the equation that lacks the quantity you neither know nor need.

Free Fall

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Understand that all objects fall with the same acceleration in a vacuum
  • Apply the equations of motion to a falling or vertically thrown object

What is free fall?

Free fall is motion under gravity alone, with no air resistance. The downward acceleration is constant, written g; near the Earth it is about 9.8m/s2, and we use 10m/s2 for quick calculations when told to.

In a vacuum a light stone and a heavy stone reach the ground together, so mass does not affect the acceleration. A feather lags in air only because of air resistance.

An object thrown upward

When an object is thrown up, its acceleration stays g downward the whole trip. It slows while rising until its velocity is zero at the top, then speeds up while falling.

The trip is symmetric: the time up equals the time down, and the object returns to the launch point with the same speed it left with, but in the opposite direction.

How to solve

Choose the positive direction first. Then use the same equations of motion with g in place of a. At the top set v=0. For a drop from rest set v0​=0.

If the object is thrown downward with an initial speed, take down as positive: v=v0​+gt, and it speeds up faster than a drop from rest.

Laws and rules

Dropped from restv=gt,Δy=21​gt2v: speed (m/s), t: time (s), Δy: distance fallen (m), g: free-fall acceleration (m/s2).
Impact speedv2=2gΔyFor an object dropped from rest through a distance.
Maximum height of an upward throwh=2gv02​​v0​: launch speed (m/s), because the velocity at the top is zero.
Time to riset=gv0​​The total time to return to the launch point is twice this.
Thrown downwardv=v0​+gtDown is positive; v0​ is the launch speed downward (m/s).

Worked examples

A stone is dropped from rest for 3s. Take g=10m/s2. Find its speed and the distance fallen.
Show the solution
  1. From rest v0​=0.
  2. v=10×3=30m/s.
  3. Δy=21​×10×32=21​×10×9=45m.
  4. Check: average speed 15m/s times 3s gives 45m.
Answer: Speed 30m/s, distance 45m
An object is thrown vertically up at 20m/s. Take g=10m/s2. Find the maximum height, the total time to return, and the speed on return.
Show the solution
  1. Time to rise: t=1020​=2s.
  2. Maximum height: h=2×10202​=20400​=20m.
  3. Total time is twice the rise time: 2×2=4s.
  4. The return speed is 20m/s downward.
Answer: Height 20m, time 4s, speed 20m/s downward
An object is released from a height of 80m. Take g=10m/s2. How long does it take to hit the ground and how fast is it going then?
Show the solution
  1. 80=21​×10×t2=5t2.
  2. t2=16, so t=4s.
  3. v=10×4=40m/s.
  4. Check: v2=2×10×80=1600 and 402=1600.
Answer: Time 4s, speed 40m/s
Common mistakes
  • Thinking a heavier object falls faster in a vacuum: all objects fall with the same acceleration.
  • Thinking acceleration is zero at the top: velocity is zero for an instant, but the acceleration is still g downward.
  • Forgetting to square the time in the displacement equation: distance grows with the square of time.
  • Sign confusion: if up is positive, the acceleration is −g.
Summary
  • In free fall the acceleration is a constant g≈10m/s2 downward.
  • All objects fall together in a vacuum.
  • At the top the velocity is zero but the acceleration is not.
  • Time up equals time down, and the return speed equals the launch speed.

Stopping Distance and Time

What you will learn
  • Separate reaction distance from braking distance
  • Find total stopping distance and stopping time
  • See why doubling the speed multiplies braking distance by four

Two stages, not one

From the moment a driver sees danger until the car stops there are two stages. First comes the reaction time: the driver thinks and presses the brake, and the car keeps its constant speed. Then braking: the car slows with constant negative acceleration until it stops.

Reaction distance

The speed is constant in this stage, so distance is speed times reaction time.

Braking distance and time

The speed ends at zero. Using the velocity–displacement equation, the distance is proportional to the square of the initial speed: double the speed and the braking distance becomes four times larger. The braking time only doubles.

Total stopping distance is the reaction distance plus the braking distance, and total stopping time is the reaction time plus the braking time.

What makes stopping distance longer?

More speed lengthens both stages. Tiredness or phone distraction increases the reaction time, so the reaction distance grows. On a wet road the possible deceleration is smaller because friction is weaker, so the braking distance grows.

Laws and rules

Reaction distanced1​=vt1​v: speed (m/s), t1​: reaction time (s).
Braking distanced2​=2av2​a: size of the deceleration (m/s2), without sign.
Braking timet2​=av​In seconds.
Total stopping distanced=d1​+d2​and the total time is t=t1​+t2​.

Worked examples

A car moves at 15m/s and the driver's reaction time is 0.4s. What is the reaction distance?
Show the solution
  1. The speed is constant during the reaction time.
  2. d1​=15×0.4=6m.
Answer: 6m
A car at 20m/s brakes with constant deceleration 5m/s2 until it stops. Find the braking distance and time.
Show the solution
  1. d2​=2×5202​=10400​=40m.
  2. t2​=520​=4s.
  3. Check: d=220+0​×4=40m.
Answer: Distance 40m, time 4s
A car moves at 20m/s, the driver's reaction time is 0.5s and the braking deceleration is 5m/s2. Find the total stopping distance and total stopping time.
Show the solution
  1. Reaction distance =20×0.5=10m.
  2. Braking distance =10400​=40m.
  3. Total distance =10+40=50m.
  4. Braking time =520​=4s, so total time =0.5+4=4.5s.
Answer: Distance 50m, time 4.5s
Common mistakes
  • Ignoring reaction distance and using braking distance alone: stopping distance includes both.
  • Thinking doubling the speed only doubles the braking distance: it becomes four times larger (for example, at deceleration 5m/s2 the braking distance is 10m at 10m/s and 40m at 20m/s).
  • Putting a negative sign in the braking-distance formula and getting a negative distance: use the deceleration as a positive size there.
  • Letting the speed change during reaction time: it stays constant.
Summary
  • Stopping distance = reaction distance + braking distance.
  • Reaction distance = speed × reaction time.
  • Braking distance is proportional to the speed squared.
  • Braking time = speed ÷ deceleration.
