Vectors: Adding, Resolving and Equilibrium

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell vector quantities from scalar quantities
  • Add two perpendicular vectors with the Pythagorean theorem
  • Resolve a vector into horizontal and vertical components
  • Solve two-dimensional force equilibrium problems

Vector quantities

A vector has both a size and a direction, like force, displacement and velocity. A scalar has size only, like mass and time.

So we cannot add vectors like plain numbers: the direction of each one changes the result.

Adding two perpendicular vectors

If you walk 3m east and then 4m north, you are not 7m from the start, because the two moves are not on one line. The vectors are the two legs of a right triangle and the resultant is the hypotenuse.

We find its size with the Pythagorean theorem. If the vectors point the same way we add their sizes; if they point opposite ways we subtract the smaller from the larger.

To find the direction we take the angle of the resultant from the first vector: tanθ=AB​. If the vectors are not perpendicular, resolve each into horizontal and vertical components, add the horizontal ones together and the vertical ones together, then apply Pythagoras to the two sums.

Resolving a vector into components

Resolving is the reverse of adding: we replace a slanted vector by two perpendicular ones, horizontal and vertical, with the same effect. If the vector makes an angle θ with the horizontal, the component next to the angle uses cosθ and the one opposite it uses sinθ.

  • Horizontal component =Fcosθ
  • Vertical component =Fsinθ
  • Check: the squares of the two components add up to the square of the vector.

Equilibrium in two dimensions

An object is in equilibrium when the net force on it is zero. In two dimensions this must hold in each direction: the horizontal forces add to zero and the vertical forces add to zero.

The equilibrant of a set of forces has the same size as their resultant and the opposite direction. So if an object is balanced by three forces, the third one cancels the resultant of the other two.

Laws and rules

Horizontal componentFx​=Fcosθθ is the angle from the horizontal. The component has the same unit as the vector (for example N).
Vertical componentFy​=Fsinθθ is the angle from the horizontal. If the angle is measured from the vertical, sine and cosine swap.
Resultant of two perpendicular vectorsR=A2+B2​A and B are the two sizes in one unit; R has the same unit.
Direction of the resultanttanθ=AB​θ is the angle between the resultant and vector A when the two vectors are perpendicular. The ratio has no unit.
Equilibrium conditionΣF=0Holds separately in the horizontal and in the vertical direction (N).

Worked examples

A force of 10N acts at 37∘ above the horizontal. Find its horizontal and vertical components. (Take sin37∘=0.6, cos37∘=0.8)
Show the solution
  1. The angle is measured from the horizontal, so the horizontal component uses cosθ and the vertical one uses sinθ.
  2. Horizontal =10×0.8=8N.
  3. Vertical =10×0.6=6N.
  4. Check: 82+62=64+36=100=102.
Answer: Horizontal 8N, vertical 6N.
A student walks 6m east and then 8m north. What is the size of the resultant displacement?
Show the solution
  1. The directions are perpendicular, so the resultant is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 6 and 8.
  2. R=62+82​=36+64​.
  3. 100​=10.
  4. Direction: tanθ=68​=34​, so the angle is about 53∘ north of east.
  5. Note the resultant is less than the sum 6+8=14.
Answer: The displacement is 10m, about 53∘ north of east.
An object is in equilibrium under three forces: 9N east, 12N north and a third force. Find the size and direction of the third force.
Show the solution
  1. The object is in equilibrium, so the three forces add to zero: the third cancels the resultant of the other two.
  2. Resultant of the two perpendicular forces: 92+122​=81+144​=225​=15N.
  3. That resultant points between east and north (north-east), and the equilibrant points opposite to it.
  4. Check on each axis: horizontally we need 9N west, vertically 12N south, and these combine to 15N.
Answer: It is 15N, opposite to the resultant: south-west (about 53∘ south of west).
Common mistakes
  • Adding the sizes of two perpendicular vectors directly (3+4=7). Use Pythagoras: the resultant is 5.
  • Mixing up sine and cosine. If the angle is from the horizontal, the horizontal component goes with cosθ; if it is from the vertical, the reverse. Draw the triangle first.
  • Thinking an object in equilibrium must be at rest. It may also move at constant velocity in a straight line; the net force is zero either way.
  • Forgetting that the equilibrant is opposite to the resultant, so giving only its size is not enough.
Summary
  • A vector has size and direction and is not added like numbers.
  • Two perpendicular vectors: R=A2+B2​.
  • Resolving: horizontal =Fcosθ, vertical =Fsinθ (angle from the horizontal).
  • Equilibrium means the forces add to zero in every direction.

Projectiles: Horizontal, Angled and Range

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Split projectile motion into independent horizontal and vertical parts
  • Find the time of flight and range of a horizontal projectile
  • Resolve a launch velocity and find the maximum height and range
  • Know which angle gives the greatest range

The idea of independence

A projectile is thrown and then only gravity acts on it (we ignore air resistance). Gravity is vertical only, so nothing changes the horizontal velocity.

So we study the two motions separately: horizontally at constant velocity with no acceleration, and vertically with constant downward acceleration g=10m/s2. Time is the only link between them, and it is the same for both.

Horizontal projectile

The object is thrown horizontally from some height, so its starting vertical velocity is zero. Its fall time depends only on the height, as if it were dropped from rest.

That is why a ball thrown horizontally and a ball dropped from the same height land together, whatever their horizontal speeds, and mass does not matter. Once we know the time, the horizontal range is the horizontal velocity times the time.

At any moment its vertical velocity is g×t downward while the horizontal velocity stays the same, so the direction of the velocity tilts more and more downward.

Angled projectile

The launch velocity is resolved into two parts: the horizontal one vcosθ stays constant, and the vertical one vsinθ drops by 10m/s every second while rising.

  • At the top the vertical component is zero, but the speed is not zero: the horizontal component remains.
  • Time to rise =vy​÷g, and the total flight time is twice that when the object returns to launch height.
  • Maximum height =vy2​÷(2g).

Range and launch angle

The range is the horizontal distance until the object returns to launch level: horizontal velocity times total flight time. It can be written gv2sin2θ​.

sin2θ is greatest (1) when 2θ=90∘, that is at 45∘. Two angles that add up to 90∘, such as 30∘ and 60∘ or 37∘ and 53∘, give the same range, but the larger one goes higher and stays up longer.

Height of a horizontal projectileh=21​gt2h in m, g=10m/s2, t in s. Used to find the fall time.
Horizontal rangeR=vx​tR in m, vx​ in m/s, t in s.
Horizontal velocity componentvx​=vcosθθ is the launch angle above the horizontal. It stays constant (m/s).
Vertical velocity componentvy​=vsinθIn m/s; it is zero at the top. Time to rise =vy​÷g.
Maximum heighthmax​=2gvy2​​In m, with g=10m/s2.
Range at an angleR=gv2sin2θ​For launch from level ground. The greatest range is at θ=45∘.

Worked examples

A stone is thrown horizontally at 12m/s from the edge of a 20m cliff. Find its time to reach the ground and its horizontal range. (g=10m/s2)
Show the solution
  1. The starting vertical velocity is zero, so 20=21​×10×t2.
  2. 20=5t2, so t2=4.
  3. t=2s.
  4. Range =12×2=24m.
  5. The vertical velocity on landing is 10×2=20m/s downward, and the horizontal one is still 12m/s.
Answer: Time 2s and range 24m.
A ball is kicked at 25m/s at 37∘ above the horizontal on level ground. Find its speed at the top, maximum height, time of flight and range. (sin37∘=0.6, cos37∘=0.8, g=10m/s2)
Show the solution
  1. Horizontal component =25×0.8=20m/s; vertical component =25×0.6=15m/s.
  2. At the top only the horizontal component remains: 20m/s.
  3. Time to rise =15÷10=1.5s, so the flight time is 3s.
  4. Maximum height =2×10152​=20225​=11.25m.
  5. Range =20×3=60m.
Answer: Speed at the top 20m/s, maximum height 11.25m, flight time 3s, range 60m.
Two balls are launched at the same speed 10m/s from level ground, one at 37∘ and one at 53∘. Compare their ranges and flight times. (sin37∘=cos53∘=0.6, cos37∘=sin53∘=0.8)
Show the solution
  1. Use sin2θ=2sinθcosθ and R=10100sin2θ​.
  2. First ball: sin74∘=2×0.6×0.8=0.96, so R=10×0.96=9.6m.
  3. Second ball: sin106∘=2×0.8×0.6=0.96, so R=9.6m too.
  4. Flight time: first 102×6​=1.2s, second 102×8​=1.6s.
  5. Check: 8×1.2=9.6 and 6×1.6=9.6.
Answer: Both ranges are 9.6m because the angles are complementary, but the 53∘ ball goes higher and stays up longer.
Common mistakes
  • Saying the projectile's speed is zero at the top. Only the vertical component is zero; the horizontal component remains.
  • Thinking the ball with the larger horizontal speed lands later or earlier. Fall time depends on height only, not on horizontal speed or mass.
  • Choosing 60∘ or 90∘ for the greatest range. Without air the greatest range is at 45∘; at 90∘ the object goes up and comes straight back.
  • Using the full speed in R=v×t. Use the horizontal component only.
Summary
  • Horizontal and vertical motions are independent; time is shared.
  • Horizontally the velocity is constant; vertically the acceleration is g downward.
  • At the top: the vertical component is zero and the horizontal one remains.
  • The greatest range is at 45∘, and complementary angles give equal ranges.

The Incline: Weight Components and Friction

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Resolve weight into a part along the surface and a part perpendicular to it
  • Find the acceleration on a smooth incline
  • Find acceleration or friction on a rough incline
  • Tell kinetic friction from static friction

Why resolve the weight?

On an incline the weight is neither along the surface nor perpendicular to it. So we choose two axes, one along the surface and one perpendicular to it, and resolve the weight onto them.

The incline angle θ is also the angle between the weight and the perpendicular to the surface. So the perpendicular component is next to this angle and uses cosθ, while the parallel component is opposite it and uses sinθ.

  • Component along the surface =Wsinθ
  • Component perpendicular to the surface =Wcosθ
  • A steeper slope means a bigger parallel part and a smaller perpendicular part. At θ=0 all of the weight is perpendicular; at θ=90∘ all of it is parallel.

The smooth incline

With no friction, the only force along the surface is the parallel component of the weight, mgsinθ. By Newton's second law ma=mgsinθ.

The mass cancels, so a=gsinθ. The acceleration does not depend on mass: two objects of different mass slide with the same acceleration. The normal force balances the perpendicular component of the weight and does not accelerate the object.

Friction on a slope

The normal force equals the perpendicular component of the weight when no other perpendicular forces act, that is mgcosθ. Kinetic friction =μN and opposes the motion, so it points up the slope for an object sliding down.

The net force along the surface is the parallel part minus friction, so a=g(sinθ−μcosθ). The mass cancels here too.

If the object slides up the slope, friction points down the slope because it opposes the motion, so it slows down at a rate g(sinθ+μcosθ).

An object at rest and static friction

If the object stays at rest, its net force is zero, so static friction equals the parallel component of the weight and points up the slope. We do not find it from μN, because that is only its maximum value.

The object slides only if the parallel component exceeds the maximum static friction, that is, if tanθ exceeds the static friction coefficient.

Laws and rules

Component along the surfaceW∥​=Wsinθθ is the incline angle. W=mg in N.
Component perpendicular to the surfaceW⊥​=WcosθEquals the normal force if no other perpendicular forces act (N).
Acceleration on a smooth inclinea=gsinθg=10m/s2. Independent of mass.
Friction forcef=μNIn N. μ has no unit, and friction opposes the sliding direction.
Acceleration on a rough inclinea=g(sinθ−μcosθ)In m/s2, for an object sliding down.

Worked examples

An object slides from rest down a smooth incline of 37∘. Find its acceleration and its speed after 3s. (g=10m/s2, sin37∘=0.6)
Show the solution
  1. The incline is smooth, so a=gsinθ.
  2. a=10×0.6=6m/s2.
  3. Speed after 3s from rest =6×3=18m/s.
Answer: Acceleration 6m/s2 and speed 18m/s.
A 2kg block slides down a 37∘ incline with kinetic friction coefficient 0.5. Find its acceleration. (g=10m/s2, sin37∘=0.6, cos37∘=0.8)
Show the solution
  1. Normal force =2×10×0.8=16N.
  2. Friction =0.5×16=8N, up the slope.
  3. Parallel component of the weight =2×10×0.6=12N, down the slope.
  4. Net force =12−8=4N, so a=4÷2=2m/s2.
  5. Check with the short formula: 10×(0.6−0.5×0.8)=10×0.2=2m/s2.
Answer: Acceleration 2m/s2.
A 5kg block rests on a 37∘ incline; the static friction coefficient is 0.9. What is the friction force on it, and does it slide? (g=10m/s2, sin37∘=0.6, cos37∘=0.8)
Show the solution
  1. Parallel component of the weight =5×10×0.6=30N, down the slope.
  2. Normal force =5×10×0.8=40N, so the maximum static friction =0.9×40=36N.
  3. 30 is less than 36, so friction can balance the parallel component and the block stays at rest.
  4. Since it is at rest, friction equals the parallel component exactly: 30N up the slope, not 36.
  5. Another check: tan37∘=0.80.6​=0.75, which is less than 0.9, so it does not slide.
Answer: Friction is 30N up the slope, and the block does not slide.
Common mistakes
  • Using cosθ for the sliding acceleration. The component that accelerates the object is the one along the surface, with sinθ.
  • Thinking a heavier object slides faster on a smooth incline. The mass cancels and every object has a=gsinθ.
  • Taking the normal force equal to the whole weight. On an incline it is only Wcosθ.
  • Always finding static friction as μN. For an object at rest it equals the parallel component of the weight; μN is only its maximum.
Summary
  • Along the surface =Wsinθ; perpendicular =Wcosθ.
  • Smooth incline: a=gsinθ, independent of mass.
  • Rough incline: a=g(sinθ−μcosθ).
  • At rest: static friction equals the parallel component of the weight.

Circular Motion and Centripetal Force

What you will learn
  • Understand why an object accelerates in circular motion even at constant speed
  • Find the period, frequency and speed
  • Find the centripetal acceleration and force
  • Recognise what supplies the centripetal force in different situations

Uniform circular motion

When an object goes round a circle at constant speed, the size of its velocity stays the same but its direction keeps changing. A change of direction means there is an acceleration.

At every moment the velocity is tangent to the circle, while the acceleration points toward the centre. So it is wrong to say the acceleration is zero because the speed is constant.

Period and frequency

The period is the time for one full turn, and the frequency is the number of turns per second. They are inverses: the higher the frequency, the shorter the period.

In one turn the object covers the circumference 2πr, so its speed is the circumference divided by the period.

Centripetal acceleration

It depends on the speed and the radius: the speed is squared and divided by the radius. If the speed doubles the acceleration becomes 4 times as large; if the radius doubles at the same speed, the acceleration halves.

Centripetal force

From Newton's second law, centripetal force =m×ac​. It is not a new force added to the others; it is the net force pointing toward the centre: the tension in a string, the friction on a car's tyres in a bend, or gravity for a moon orbiting a planet.

If the string breaks or this force disappears, the object moves off in a straight line along the tangent, not outward.

The centripetal force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it changes the direction of the velocity without changing its size.

Laws and rules

PeriodT=nt​t is the total time in s and n the number of turns; T in s.
Frequencyf=T1​In Hz (turns per second).
Speed on a circlev=T2πr​r in m and T in s, so v is in m/s.
Centripetal accelerationac​=rv2​In m/s2, directed toward the centre.
Centripetal forceFc​=mrv2​In N, with m in kg.

Worked examples

An object completes 30 turns in 12s on a circle of radius 0.4m. Find its period, frequency and speed.
Show the solution
  1. Period =3012​=0.4s.
  2. Frequency =1230​=2.5Hz.
  3. Speed =0.42π×0.4​=2π≈6.28m/s.
  4. Check: 0.41​=2.5.
Answer: Period 0.4s, frequency 2.5Hz and speed about 6.28m/s.
A 2kg object moves at 6m/s on a circle of radius 3m. Find its centripetal acceleration and force.
Show the solution
  1. ac​=362​=336​=12m/s2.
  2. Fc​=2×12=24N.
  3. Check with the direct formula: 2×336​=24N.
Answer: Acceleration 12m/s2 and force 24N.
A 1000kg car drives at 10m/s round a circular bend of radius 50m. What centripetal force is needed, and what does it become if the driver doubles the speed?
Show the solution
  1. Fc​=1000×50102​=1000×2=2000N, supplied by friction between the tyres and the road.
  2. At 20m/s: Fc​=1000×50202​=1000×8=8000N.
  3. 8000÷2000=4: doubling the speed made the force 4 times as large.
Answer: 2000N, which becomes 8000N (four times as large).
Common mistakes
  • Saying the acceleration is zero because the speed is constant. Velocity is a vector and its direction changes, so there is an acceleration toward the centre.
  • Believing a centrifugal force pushes the object outward. The object tends to continue in a straight line, and the only real force is toward the centre.
  • Thinking doubling the speed doubles the centripetal force. The speed is squared, so the force becomes 4 times as large.
  • Inverting the period: it is total time divided by the number of turns, not the reverse. The number of turns per second is the frequency.
Summary
  • Velocity is tangent to the circle; acceleration and force point to the centre.
  • Period =nt​ and frequency is its inverse.
  • Centripetal acceleration =rv2​.
  • Centripetal force is the net force toward the centre and grows with the square of the speed.

Gravitation and Kepler's Laws

What you will learn
  • Apply the law of gravitation and predict how the force changes
  • Understand how weight changes with distance from Earth
  • State Kepler's three laws and use them
  • Solve problems comparing two orbits

The law of universal gravitation

Any two bodies in the universe attract each other. The force is proportional to the product of the two masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres.

The two forces are equal in size and opposite in direction (Newton's third law): Earth pulls on the Moon exactly as hard as the Moon pulls on Earth.

How does the force change?

Compare with a new situation by multiplying the changes, squaring the distance change and inverting it:

  • One mass doubled: the force doubles.
  • Both masses doubled: the force becomes 4 times as large.
  • Distance doubled: the force becomes one quarter.
  • Distance tripled: the force becomes one ninth.
  • Distance is measured from centre to centre, not from the surface.

Gravity near Earth

An object's weight is Earth's pull on it. The acceleration g falls with the square of the distance from Earth's centre.

At a height equal to Earth's radius above the surface, the distance from the centre is twice the radius, so the weight (and g) becomes one quarter of its surface value.

Kepler's laws

Kepler described the motion of planets around the Sun with three laws:

  • First: each planet's orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus.
  • Second: the line from the planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times. So the planet moves fastest at its closest point to the Sun (perihelion) and slowest at its farthest (aphelion).
  • Third: the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the planet's mean distance from the Sun (the semi-major axis of the orbit). A farther planet has a longer period and a lower speed.

Laws and rules

Law of universal gravitationF=Gr2m1​m2​​F in N, masses in kg, r is the distance between centres in m, and G=6.67×10−11Nm2/kg2.
Gravitational accelerationg=Gr2M​In m/s2, with r measured from the planet's centre. It falls with the square of the distance.
Kepler's third lawr3T2​=constantThe same constant for all planets orbiting the same Sun.

Worked examples

Two bodies attract each other with a force of 36N. What does the force become if the distance between their centres is tripled?
Show the solution
  1. The force is inversely proportional to the square of the distance.
  2. The distance factor is 3, so we divide the force by 32=9.
  3. 36÷9=4N.
Answer: The force becomes 4N.
A 60kg student weighs 600N on Earth's surface (g=10m/s2). What is the weight at a height equal to Earth's radius above the surface?
Show the solution
  1. The distance from Earth's centre is now 2 times the radius.
  2. The force becomes 221​=41​ of its value.
  3. New weight =600÷4=150N.
  4. Check: new g=10÷4=2.5m/s2 and 60×2.5=150N; the mass did not change.
Answer: The weight is 150N.
A planet orbits the Sun at 4 times the radius of Earth's orbit. What is its period in years?
Show the solution
  1. Kepler's third law: the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the radius.
  2. For Earth: radius 1 (unit) and period 1 year. For the planet: radius 4.
  3. T2=43=64.
  4. T=64​=8 years.
Answer: Its period is 8 years.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the force changes with the distance itself. It changes with its square: double the distance gives one quarter of the force, not one half.
  • Saying Earth pulls on the Moon harder than the Moon pulls on Earth. The two forces are equal in size and opposite in direction.
  • Measuring the distance from Earth's surface. In the law it is measured from Earth's centre to the object's centre.
  • Treating a planet's orbit as a circle with the Sun at the centre and constant speed. The orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at a focus, and the planet is faster when closer.
Summary
  • Gravity is proportional to the product of the masses and inversely to the square of the distance.
  • Doubling the distance makes the force one quarter.
  • A planet's orbit is an ellipse with the Sun at one focus.
  • A planet is fastest when closest, and T2 is proportional to r3.
