Cell Theory and Types of Cells

★ Key
What you will learn
  • State the three principles of cell theory
  • Tell prokaryotic from eukaryotic cells
  • Know what makes a plant cell special, including the cell wall

Cell theory

Cell theory was built over generations of scientists with the help of the microscope. It gives the cell its place: the smallest unit that carries out all the functions of life.

It has three principles. First: all living things are made of one or more cells. Second: the cell is the basic unit of structure and function in a living thing. Third: every new cell comes from an existing cell by division, so cells do not appear from nothing.

  • An amoeba is one cell and a human is trillions of cells; both follow the same theory.
  • Levels of organisation start at the cell: cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism.
  • Hooke named cells when he saw them in cork; Schleiden studied plants, Schwann animals, and Virchow showed that every cell comes from a cell.

Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

Cells fall into two main types. A prokaryotic cell (such as a bacterium) has no nucleus surrounded by a membrane; its circular DNA molecule floats in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid.

In a eukaryotic cell (plants, animals, fungi, protists) a nuclear envelope surrounds the genetic material, and membrane-bound organelles divide the work inside the cell.

  • Shared features: plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
  • Key difference: a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles exist only in eukaryotes.
  • Prokaryotes are usually smaller and simpler.
  • Bacteria may have a capsule and a flagellum for movement, and they reproduce by binary fission.

The plant cell and the cell wall

A plant cell shares the nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes and Golgi apparatus with an animal cell, but it has three extra features: a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large central vacuole.

The cell wall is a rigid layer outside the plasma membrane, made of cellulose in plants. It gives the cell its shape, supports it and stops it bursting when water enters. It lets water and dissolved substances pass easily, so controlling what enters and leaves is the job of the membrane, not the wall.

Walls are not only for plants: bacteria have a wall of a different material (peptidoglycan), fungi have a chitin wall, and animal cells have no wall at all.

  • Plant cell: a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large central vacuole, and usually a regular shape.
  • Animal cell: no wall and no chloroplasts, small vacuoles, and a pair of centrioles that take part in division.

Laws and rules

Levels of organisationcell→tissue→organ→organ system→organismEach level is made of groups of the level before it; the cell is the base.

Worked examples

A cell has free DNA in the cytoplasm, ribosomes and a cell wall, but no nucleus and no mitochondria. What type is it?
Show the solution
  1. Look for a membrane-bound nucleus: there is none.
  2. Free DNA with no membrane matches a nucleoid region.
  3. The absence of membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria confirms it.
Answer: A prokaryotic cell, such as a bacterium.
A cell has a nucleus, chloroplasts, a cell wall and a large vacuole. Is it an animal or a plant cell?
Show the solution
  1. Look for features unique to plant cells.
  2. The wall, chloroplasts and large vacuole are all plant features.
  3. An animal cell has no wall and no chloroplasts.
Answer: A eukaryotic plant cell.
A student says: "A bacterial cell is not alive because it has no nucleus." Is this correct?
Show the solution
  1. Cell theory says the cell is the smallest unit that carries out life functions.
  2. A bacterium is a complete cell: it feeds, grows, reproduces and has DNA and ribosomes.
  3. No nucleus only means it is prokaryotic, not that it is non-living.
Answer: Wrong: a bacterial cell is alive, but it is prokaryotic.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking prokaryotes have no genetic material. They do have DNA, but it is not enclosed in a membrane.
  • Mixing up the wall and the membrane. The wall supports and protects; the membrane controls entry and exit.
  • Thinking only plants have a cell wall. Bacteria and fungi have walls too, made of different materials; it is the animal cell that has none.
  • Thinking cells appear spontaneously. Every cell comes from an existing cell.
Summary
  • The cell is the smallest structural and functional unit of life and arises from an earlier cell.
  • Prokaryotes have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles, and their DNA is free.
  • Eukaryotes have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.
  • A cell wall, chloroplasts and a large vacuole mark the plant cell.

The Nucleus, Ribosomes and Endoplasmic Reticulum

What you will learn
  • Describe the nucleus and how it controls the cell
  • Know that the ribosome is the protein factory
  • Tell rough ER from smooth ER and state the job of each

The nucleus: control centre

The nucleus is an organelle surrounded by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope, with pores that let messages out and materials in. Inside, DNA is arranged in threads called chromatin; when the cell divides, the chromatin condenses and shows up as chromosomes.

A dense body called the nucleolus makes the parts of ribosomes. The nucleus controls the cell because it holds the instructions for making proteins, and proteins do most of the cell's work.

Ribosomes: protein factories

A ribosome is a tiny particle that reads the mRNA message from the nucleus and joins amino acids together to make a protein. It has no membrane, so prokaryotic cells have ribosomes too.

Some ribosomes are free in the cytoplasm and make proteins the cell uses itself. Others are attached to the rough ER and make proteins that are exported, placed in membranes, or sent to lysosomes.

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER)

The ER is a maze of sacs and tubes connected to the nuclear envelope. It comes in two kinds.

Rough ER is covered with ribosomes. It takes in the protein the ribosome makes, folds and modifies it, then packs it into vesicles. It is abundant in cells that secrete a lot of protein, such as gland cells.

Smooth ER has no ribosomes. It makes lipids such as phospholipids and steroids, detoxifies some drugs and poisons in liver cells, and stores calcium ions in muscle cells.

Worked examples

Gland cells secrete large amounts of digestive enzymes. Which kind of ER is abundant in them, and why?
Show the solution
  1. Enzymes are proteins.
  2. Exported proteins are made by ribosomes on the rough ER.
  3. So the cell needs plenty of rough ER.
Answer: Rough ER, because it makes and modifies exported proteins.
Liver cells detoxify drugs. Which organelle is abundant in them?
Show the solution
  1. Detoxification is done by enzymes in the smooth ER.
  2. The rough ER deals with exported proteins, which is not what is asked.
Answer: Smooth ER.
Put in order the path of information to an exported protein: ribosome, nucleus, mRNA, rough ER.
Show the solution
  1. Start in the nucleus, where DNA is copied into mRNA.
  2. mRNA leaves through the nuclear pores to a ribosome.
  3. The attached ribosome makes the protein and feeds it into the rough ER.
Answer: Nucleus, then mRNA, then ribosome, then rough ER.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the ribosome is a membrane-bound organelle. It has no membrane.
  • Mixing up the two ERs: rough for protein, smooth for lipids and detox.
  • Thinking the nucleus builds the protein itself. It holds the instructions; the ribosome builds.
  • Thinking ribosomes exist only in eukaryotes. They are in both types.
Summary
  • The nucleus holds DNA and controls the cell; the nucleolus makes ribosome parts.
  • The ribosome makes protein and has no membrane.
  • Rough ER (with ribosomes) modifies exported proteins.
  • Smooth ER makes lipids and detoxifies poisons.

Golgi Apparatus, Lysosomes, Vacuoles and the Secretion Pathway

What you will learn
  • Describe how the Golgi apparatus modifies and packs materials
  • Know the jobs of the lysosome and the central vacuole
  • Put the steps of protein secretion in order

The Golgi apparatus: the post office

The Golgi apparatus is a stack of flat sacs. It receives vesicles from the rough ER on one side, modifies the contents, sorts them and packs them into new vesicles that leave from the other side for their destination.

It also makes lysosomes and secretory vesicles.

Lysosomes and vacuoles

A lysosome is a small membrane-bound sac full of digestive enzymes. It digests swallowed food and bacteria, and breaks down worn-out organelles so the cell can reuse the parts. Because a membrane isolates the enzymes, they do not digest the cell itself. Lysosomes are plentiful in cells that engulf things, such as white blood cells.

The central vacuole is a large sac in plant cells that stores water, salts, sugars and pigments. It fills most of the cell, and water pressure inside keeps the cell firm; when water runs short, the plant wilts. Animal cells have only small, numerous vacuoles, and some protists such as amoeba have a contractile vacuole that pumps out excess water.

The secretion pathway

A ribosome on the rough ER makes a protein such as insulin. The protein enters the ER, leaves in a transport vesicle for the Golgi apparatus, which modifies and packs it into a secretory vesicle. This vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane and releases its contents outside the cell.

Laws and rules

Order of the secretion pathwayribosome→rough ER→Golgi→membraneVesicles carry the material between stations; at the end it leaves by exocytosis.

Worked examples

A plant that has not been watered for days wilts and its leaves droop. Which organelle in its cells lost water, and why does the plant wilt?
Show the solution
  1. The large central vacuole stores most of a plant cell's water.
  2. The water pressure inside it pushes the membrane against the wall, so the cell stays firm.
  3. When water runs low the pressure drops and the cells lose their firmness.
Answer: The central vacuole; the plant wilts because its cells lose their firmness.
A cell can no longer digest worn-out organelles, and they pile up. Which organelle has failed?
Show the solution
  1. Internal digestion needs digestive enzymes.
  2. Digestive enzymes are carried in lysosomes.
Answer: Lysosomes.
Order these stations for insulin: plasma membrane, Golgi apparatus, ribosome, rough ER.
Show the solution
  1. Insulin is first made by a ribosome attached to the rough ER.
  2. It moves through the rough ER and then to the Golgi apparatus for modification.
  3. It leaves last through the plasma membrane.
Answer: Ribosome, then rough ER, then Golgi apparatus, then plasma membrane.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the Golgi makes protein. It only modifies and packs it.
  • Thinking the lysosome digests the cell. Its membrane keeps the enzymes apart.
  • Forgetting that animal cells have small vacuoles; the large central vacuole is the plant feature.
  • Swapping the order of the rough ER and the Golgi. The Golgi comes after the rough ER: a protein cannot be modified and packed before it is made and has entered the ER.
Summary
  • The Golgi receives, modifies, packs and ships materials.
  • Lysosomes digest food, bacteria and worn-out organelles with enzymes.
  • The central vacuole stores water and keeps plant cells firm.
  • Pathway: ribosome, rough ER, Golgi, membrane.

Mitochondria and Chloroplasts

What you will learn
  • Describe the structure and job of the mitochondrion
  • Describe the chloroplast and its role in photosynthesis
  • Know what the two organelles have in common

The mitochondrion: power station

A mitochondrion has two membranes: a smooth outer membrane and a heavily folded inner membrane whose folds are called cristae. The cristae increase surface area so many energy-making enzymes fit. The inner space is the matrix.

Mitochondria carry out most of the steps of aerobic cellular respiration (the first breakdown of glucose, glycolysis, happens in the cytoplasm), releasing the energy stored in glucose with oxygen and storing it in ATP, the cell's energy currency. They are found in animal and plant cells alike, and are most numerous in active cells such as muscle.

The chloroplast: sugar factory

A chloroplast has two membranes. Inside are flat sacs called thylakoids, stacked into piles called grana, bathed in a fluid called the stroma. It holds the pigment chlorophyll, which absorbs light.

It carries out photosynthesis, turning light energy, carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. Chloroplasts occur in plants, algae and some protists, never in animal cells.

What do they share?

Both are surrounded by two membranes, both convert one form of energy into another, each has its own circular DNA and its own ribosomes, and each divides by itself inside the cell. This makes them resemble bacteria, which is the basis for the idea that they were once small prokaryotes that came to live inside larger cells.

The two processes fit together: the products of photosynthesis (glucose and oxygen) are the inputs of respiration, and the products of respiration (carbon dioxide and water) are the inputs of photosynthesis.

Worked examples

Which cell has more mitochondria: a leg-muscle cell or a skin cell? Why?
Show the solution
  1. Mitochondria make ATP.
  2. A muscle works hard and needs more energy.
  3. So it needs more mitochondria.
Answer: The muscle cell.
Does a plant root cell have mitochondria? Does it have chloroplasts?
Show the solution
  1. Almost every eukaryotic cell needs ATP, so it has mitochondria.
  2. A root underground gets no light, so it usually does no photosynthesis.
Answer: Yes to mitochondria; usually no chloroplasts.
Check that the photosynthesis equation is balanced for carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.
Show the solution
  1. Carbon: 6 in carbon dioxide and 6 in glucose.
  2. Hydrogen: 6×2=12 in water and 12 in glucose.
  3. Oxygen: on the left 6×2=12 in carbon dioxide and 6 in water, so 18; on the right 6 in glucose and 6×2=12 in free oxygen, so 18.
Answer: The equation is balanced.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking plants have no mitochondria. Both plants and animals do.
  • Thinking mitochondria "create" energy. They release the energy stored in glucose and store it in ATP.
  • Thinking every plant cell has chloroplasts. Root cells, for example, get no light.
  • Mixing up the structures: cristae and the matrix belong to the mitochondrion; grana and the stroma belong to the chloroplast.
Summary
  • Mitochondria make ATP by respiration; their cristae increase surface area.
  • Chloroplasts make glucose by photosynthesis and contain grana and chlorophyll.
  • Both have two membranes, their own DNA and ribosomes, and divide by themselves.
  • The product of each process is the input of the other.

Structure of the Cell Membrane

What you will learn
  • Describe the fluid mosaic model
  • Explain why phospholipids form two layers
  • Know the roles of membrane proteins and selective permeability

A double layer of phospholipids

The plasma membrane surrounds every cell and separates it from its surroundings. It is built from phospholipid molecules, each with a water-loving (hydrophilic) head and two water-fearing (hydrophobic) tails.

In water, the molecules arrange themselves in two layers: heads outward facing the water, tails inward away from it. This leaves a fatty middle that water-loving substances such as ions cannot cross easily.

The fluid mosaic model

It is called a mosaic because different proteins are embedded among the phospholipids like pieces of a mosaic, and fluid because the molecules drift sideways and do not stay fixed. Some proteins span the whole membrane and others sit on its surface.

Some proteins are channels and carriers through which substances cross, others are receptors that catch signals such as hormones, enzymes, or identity markers. In animal cells cholesterol keeps the membrane flexible, and sugar chains on the surface help cells recognise each other.

  • Cholesterol in animal cells acts as a regulator of fluidity: it stops the membrane stiffening in the cold and becoming too runny in the heat.

Selective permeability

The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances through and blocks others. Small uncharged molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide cross easily. Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose need transport proteins. Water is small and crosses the lipid layer slowly, and water channels speed it up.

Worked examples

Why do phospholipid tails point into the middle of the membrane?
Show the solution
  1. The tails fear water.
  2. Water is on both sides of the membrane: inside and outside the cell.
  3. The tails turn away from it and gather in the middle.
Answer: They fear water, so they hide between the two layers.
Which of these need a transport protein to cross the membrane: oxygen, carbon dioxide, a sodium ion, glucose?
Show the solution
  1. Oxygen and carbon dioxide are small and uncharged, so they dissolve in the fatty middle and cross directly.
  2. A sodium ion is charged, so it cannot cross the fatty middle without a protein.
  3. Glucose is a large polar molecule, so it does not cross the lipid layer easily.
Answer: The sodium ion and glucose.
Cells were treated with a chemical that disables membrane proteins. Glucose and sodium ions stopped entering, but oxygen kept entering. Explain.
Show the solution
  1. Oxygen is small and uncharged, so it crosses the fatty layer with no protein.
  2. Glucose is a large polar molecule and sodium is a charged ion, so neither crosses the fatty layer.
  3. Both need channel or carrier proteins, and those are what was disabled.
Answer: Oxygen crosses the lipid layer directly; glucose and sodium need membrane proteins.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the membrane is a single layer. It has two phospholipid layers.
  • Putting the heads inside. Heads love water, so they face outward.
  • Thinking the membrane blocks everything or lets everything in. It is selectively permeable.
  • Mixing up the job of the membrane and that of the cell wall. The membrane controls entry and exit; the wall supports and protects and lets water and dissolved substances through.
Summary
  • The membrane is two phospholipid layers: water-loving heads, water-fearing tails.
  • Tails face the middle and heads face the water.
  • Embedded proteins act as channels, carriers and receptors.
  • The membrane is selectively permeable.

Passive Transport: Diffusion, Osmosis and Tonicity

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Explain simple and facilitated diffusion
  • Define osmosis and find the direction water moves
  • Predict what happens to a cell in hypotonic, hypertonic and isotonic solutions

Diffusion: from more to less

Molecules are always moving, so they spread from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated until the concentration is even. This is diffusion, and it is passive transport: the cell spends no ATP because movement is down the gradient, not against it. Once the concentration is even the molecules keep moving equally in both directions, so the concentration no longer changes.

In simple diffusion the substance crosses the phospholipid layer directly, as oxygen and carbon dioxide do. In facilitated diffusion the substance cannot cross the fatty layer and goes through a channel or carrier protein instead, as ions and glucose do; the direction is still from high to low and no energy is used.

  • Diffusion is faster with a steeper concentration gradient, a higher temperature and smaller particles.
  • In facilitated diffusion the number of carriers is limited, so the rate levels off at a maximum once they are all busy.

Osmosis: diffusion of water

Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane, and it is passive, needing no ATP. Water moves from the solution with less solute (more water) to the solution with more solute (less water). When the solute cannot cross the membrane, the water crosses to dilute the concentrated solution.

Tonicity: what happens to the cell

Tonicity compares the solute concentration of the outside solution with that inside the cell.

In a hypotonic solution the outside has less solute, so water enters; an animal cell swells and may burst, while a plant cell is stopped by its wall and becomes firm and full. In a hypertonic solution the outside has more solute, so water leaves and the cell shrinks; in a plant the membrane pulls away from the wall (plasmolysis). In an isotonic solution water enters and leaves equally and the cell size stays the same.

Laws and rules

Direction of waterwater: from lower solute→to higher soluteThe solute stays put and water crosses the membrane until the two sides balance.

Worked examples

Glucose enters a cell through a carrier protein with no energy, from high concentration outside to low inside. What kind of transport is this?
Show the solution
  1. The direction is high to low, so it is passive.
  2. It crosses through a protein, not directly through the fatty layer.
  3. No ATP is used.
Answer: Facilitated diffusion.
The salt concentration inside a red blood cell is 0.9%. What happens if it is placed in 0.3% salt solution? And what happens in 3% solution?
Show the solution
  1. First solution: the outside, 0.3%, is lower than the 0.9% inside, so it is hypotonic.
  2. Water moves toward the higher solute concentration, that is, into the cell.
  3. Second solution: the outside, 3%, is higher than the 0.9% inside, so it is hypertonic.
  4. Water leaves the cell toward the more concentrated solution.
Answer: In 0.3% water enters, so the cell swells and may burst; in 3% water leaves, so the cell shrinks.
Two chambers are separated by a membrane that lets water through but not sugar. The first holds sugar solution at 0.2mol/L and the second at 0.5mol/L. Which way does water move, and which solution is hypertonic to the other?
Show the solution
  1. Sugar cannot cross the membrane, so it is the water that moves.
  2. Water moves toward the higher solute concentration, that is, toward the second chamber (0.5mol/L).
  3. The solution with more solute is hypertonic to the other, so it is the second chamber's solution.
Answer: Water moves into the second chamber, whose solution is hypertonic, so its liquid level rises.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking water moves toward the lower solute concentration. It is the opposite: toward the higher solute concentration.
  • Mixing up hypertonic and hypotonic. Always compare the outside with the inside of the cell: hypertonic means more solute outside, so water leaves.
  • Thinking facilitated diffusion uses energy. It is passive, down the gradient.
  • Thinking osmosis is movement of the solute. Osmosis is movement of water only.
Summary
  • Diffusion is movement from high to low concentration with no energy.
  • Simple diffusion crosses the lipid layer; facilitated diffusion uses a protein.
  • Osmosis is diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane.
  • Hypotonic: swelling. Hypertonic: shrinking. Isotonic: no change.

Active Transport and Vesicle Transport

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Know that active transport goes against the gradient and needs ATP
  • Describe how the sodium-potassium pump works
  • Tell phagocytosis from exocytosis

Active transport

Sometimes a cell needs to collect a substance where its concentration is already high. This is the opposite of diffusion, so it needs energy. In active transport a carrier protein uses ATP to push a substance from lower to higher concentration, that is, against the gradient.

How it differs from facilitated diffusion: both use a protein, but facilitated diffusion follows the gradient with no energy, while active transport goes against it using energy.

The sodium-potassium pump

This pump is a membrane protein that uses one ATP molecule per cycle to push 3 sodium ions out of the cell and bring 2 potassium ions in. So sodium stays higher outside the cell and potassium stays higher inside.

Nerve cells depend on it to carry signals, and the sodium gradient it builds is used to bring in other substances such as glucose. Cells spend a large share of their energy running this pump.

Vesicle transport

Large materials cannot cross through proteins, so the cell moves them in vesicles joined to the membrane, which costs energy. Vesicle transport works in two directions: endocytosis brings material in and exocytosis sends it out.

Phagocytosis brings material in: the membrane wraps around a large particle such as a bacterium and swallows it into a food vacuole, and a lysosome fuses with it and digests it. Amoebas and white blood cells do this. Pinocytosis is similar but swallows tiny droplets of fluid instead of a solid particle.

Another kind of endocytosis is receptor-mediated endocytosis: receptors in the membrane catch one specific substance, such as cholesterol, and the membrane wraps it into a vesicle.

Exocytosis sends material out: a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane and empties its contents outside, as in the release of hormones and enzymes, and it adds new membrane area.

Laws and rules

One pump cycleATP→3 Na out+2 K inEach ATP pushes three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in.

Worked examples

Sodium is 140mmol/L outside a cell and 10mmol/L inside. What kind of transport moves sodium out?
Show the solution
  1. Moving from inside (10) to outside (140) is from lower to higher.
  2. That is against the gradient.
  3. So it needs ATP and a pump protein.
Answer: Active transport.
The pump runs 4 cycles. How many sodium ions leave, how many potassium ions enter, and how many ATP are used?
Show the solution
  1. Sodium: 4×3=12.
  2. Potassium: 4×2=8.
  3. ATP: one per cycle, so 4.
Answer: 12 sodium ions leave, 8 potassium ions enter, and 4 ATP are used.
A white blood cell swallows a bacterium by wrapping its membrane around it. What is the process called, and what happens to the bacterium?
Show the solution
  1. Wrapping around and swallowing a large particle is phagocytosis.
  2. The bacterium ends up in a food vacuole.
  3. A lysosome fuses with it and its enzymes digest it.
Answer: Phagocytosis; the bacterium is digested by the lysosome.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking active transport follows the gradient. It goes against it, which is why it needs energy.
  • Reversing the pump numbers: three sodium go out and two potassium come in.
  • Mixing up phagocytosis and exocytosis: the first brings material in, the second sends it out.
  • Thinking vesicle transport needs no energy. Forming, moving and fusing vesicles uses ATP.
Summary
  • Active transport goes against the gradient and needs ATP and a carrier protein.
  • The pump moves 3 sodium out and 2 potassium in per ATP.
  • Phagocytosis swallows large particles into the cell.
  • Exocytosis expels the contents of a vesicle from the cell.
