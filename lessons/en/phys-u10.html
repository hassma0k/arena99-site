Photon Energy and the Photoelectric Effect

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Calculate photon energy from frequency or wavelength
  • Explain why low-frequency light frees no electrons, however bright
  • Find the maximum kinetic energy, stopping potential and threshold frequency

Light arrives in packets: photons

Light sometimes behaves like small packets of energy called photons. The energy of one photon is proportional to the light's frequency: higher frequency means more energy.

So a blue photon carries more energy than a red photon, because blue light has the higher frequency. Wavelength is inversely proportional to frequency, so a shorter wavelength means a more energetic photon. Also, the speed of light = wavelength × frequency, so knowing one gives the other.

The electron-volt is a unit of energy suited to atoms: it is the energy one electron gains when accelerated through a potential difference of one volt, and 1eV=1.6×10−19J.

What happens when light hits a metal?

An electron inside a metal needs a minimum energy to escape, called the work function. One electron absorbs one whole photon; it cannot collect energy from several photons.

If the photon energy is below the work function, the electron never leaves. If it is above, the electron escapes, and the extra energy becomes its kinetic energy.

Intensity or frequency?

  • Frequency (the colour) sets the energy of each photon, so it sets the maximum kinetic energy of the electrons.
  • Intensity sets the number of photons per second, so it sets the number of electrons freed, that is, the current.
  • The threshold frequency is the lowest frequency that frees electrons from the metal; it belongs to the metal. Below it nothing is emitted, however intense the light or long the wait.

Stopping potential

If we reverse the voltage between the metal and the opposite plate until the current just stops, that voltage is the stopping potential. Its value in volts equals the maximum kinetic energy in electron-volts.

Reading the graph

Plotting maximum kinetic energy against light frequency gives a straight line, because the equation is first-degree in frequency.

  • Slope = Planck's constant, the same for every metal.
  • Where it meets the frequency axis = the threshold frequency.
  • Where its extension meets the vertical axis = minus the work function.
  • Light intensity does not change the line; a different metal only shifts it, so the new line is parallel to the first.

Laws and rules

Photon energyE=hfh=6.6×10−34J⋅s (Planck's constant), f in Hz, E in J.
Photon energy from wavelengthE=λhc​c=3×108m/s, λ in m.
Einstein's equationKEmax​=hf−ϕϕ is the work function. All three terms must use the same unit (all J or all eV).
Threshold frequencyϕ=hf0​At f0​ the photon energy equals the work function exactly, so the kinetic energy is zero.
Stopping potentialVs​(V)=KEmax​(eV)The numbers are equal; the units differ.

A metal has work function 4 eV. A photon of energy 6 eV strikes it. Find the maximum kinetic energy of the freed electron and the stopping potential.
Show the solution

  2. KEmax​=6−4=2eV.
  3. The stopping potential has the same number in volts: 2 V.
  4. Check: a 3 eV photon (below 4 eV) would free no electron at all.
Answer: 2 eV, and the stopping potential is 2 V
A metal has work function 3.3×10−19J. Find its threshold frequency. Then find the maximum kinetic energy when light of frequency 8×1014Hz falls on it. (h=6.6×10−34J⋅s)
Show the solution

  3. Photon energy: 6.6×10−34×8×1014=52.8×10−20=5.28×10−19J.
  4. KEmax​=5.28×10−19−3.3×10−19=1.98×10−19J.

Common mistakes
  • Thinking that a brighter beam below the threshold frequency will free electrons. Intensity raises the number of photons, not the energy of each, and a weak photon cannot free an electron however many there are.
  • Thinking that more intensity gives each electron more kinetic energy. Frequency does that; intensity only changes the number of electrons.
  • Subtracting joules from electron-volts. Convert everything to one unit before subtracting.
  • Confusing the maximum kinetic energy with the photon energy. The kinetic energy is what is left after paying the work function.
Summary
  • Photon energy is proportional to frequency: higher frequency, more energy.
  • The photon energy must exceed the work function (the frequency must exceed the threshold) for an electron to be emitted.
  • Maximum kinetic energy = photon energy - work function; it changes with frequency, not intensity.
  • Intensity changes only the number of electrons, and the stopping potential measures the maximum kinetic energy.

Matter Waves (de Broglie)

What you will learn
  • Understand that every moving particle has a wavelength
  • Calculate the de Broglie wavelength from momentum
  • Compare the wavelengths of different particles

A particle has a wave too

We know light is a wave that sometimes acts as particles called photons. De Broglie proposed the reverse: moving particles such as electrons have wave properties.

Experiments confirmed it: electrons diffract when they pass through a crystal, just as waves do. This is the wave-particle duality of matter. Diffraction shows up when the wavelength is comparable to the spacing between atoms in the crystal.

How do we find the wavelength?

A particle's wavelength equals Planck's constant divided by its momentum. Momentum is mass times velocity.

The relation is inverse: doubling the speed (or the mass) doubles the momentum and halves the wavelength. Two particles with the same momentum have the same wavelength, even if their masses differ.

Why do we not see a ball's wave?

Large objects have a wavelength too, but it is tiny. A 0.1kg ball moving at 10m/s has momentum 1kg⋅m/s, so its wavelength is 6.6×10−34m, far too small to measure. An electron is so light that its wavelength is close to the size of an atom and can be observed.

Laws and rules

Momentump=mvm in kg, v in m/s, p in kg⋅m/s.
de Broglie wavelengthλ=ph​h=6.6×10−34J⋅s, λ in m.

Worked examples

A particle has momentum 6.6×10−24kg⋅m/s. Find its de Broglie wavelength.
Show the solution
  1. Law: λ=ph​.
  2. Substitute: 6.6×10−246.6×10−34​m.
  3. Divide the numbers: 6.6÷6.6=1; subtract the exponents: −34−(−24)=−10.
Answer: 10−10m
A particle of mass 1.1×10−27kg moves at 103m/s. Find its de Broglie wavelength.
Show the solution
  1. p=mv=1.1×10−27×103=1.1×10−24kg⋅m/s.
  2. λ=1.1×10−246.6×10−34​.
  3. 6.6÷1.1=6 and −34−(−24)=−10.
Answer: 6×10−10m
Particle A has mass m and speed v. Particle B has mass 4m and speed v/2. Find the ratio of B's wavelength to A's.
Show the solution
  1. pA​=mv.
  2. pB​=4m×2v​=2mv, twice pA​.
  3. Wavelength is inversely proportional to momentum, so B's wavelength is half of A's.
  4. Check: double the momentum gives half the wavelength.
Answer: The ratio is one half (B's wavelength is half of A's)
Common mistakes
  • Thinking a higher speed gives a longer wavelength. The relation is inverse: more speed, shorter wavelength.
  • Saying large objects have no wave. They do, but it is far too small to notice.
  • Assuming an electron and a proton always differ in wavelength. If their momenta are equal, their wavelengths are equal.
  • Using the mass alone instead of the full momentum (mass times speed).
Summary
  • Every moving particle has a wave, which is the wave-particle duality of matter.
  • Wavelength = Planck's constant divided by momentum.
  • Greater momentum means a shorter wavelength (inverse relation).
  • Equal momenta mean equal wavelengths, whatever the masses.

Energy Levels and Bohr Transitions

What you will learn
  • Understand that an atomic electron has only certain energies
  • Calculate the energy of the photon emitted or absorbed in a transition
  • Explain line spectra and compare wavelengths

Fixed energy levels

Bohr proposed that an electron in an atom cannot have just any energy, only certain values called energy levels. Think of stairs: you can stand on the first or second step but not between them. The orbit radius grows with the level number (in proportion to its square).

Level energies are negative because the electron is bound to the nucleus. The lowest level is the ground state. In hydrogen the level energy follows the law below, giving about −13.6, −3.4 and −1.5eV for levels 1, 2 and 3.

Absorption and emission

An electron jumps to a higher level when it absorbs a photon whose energy equals exactly the energy gap between the two levels. When it drops to a lower level it emits a photon with energy equal to that gap.

Because the gaps are fixed, an excited atom emits only certain colours: the line spectrum that identifies each element.

Ionisation

The energy needed to free the electron from a level is that level's energy without the minus sign: 13.6 eV from the ground state and 3.4 eV from level 2. Any extra photon energy becomes the kinetic energy of the free electron.

Colour and wavelength

A bigger energy gap means a photon of higher frequency and shorter wavelength. The smallest gap gives the longest wavelength. So the longest wavelength in a series comes from the smallest jump between neighbouring levels.

Spectral series

Downward transitions that end on the same level form a series. Those ending on level 1 are the Lyman series (ultraviolet), on level 2 the Balmer series (with visible lines), and on level 3 the Paschen series (infrared).

Laws and rules

Level energy in hydrogenEn​=n2−13.6​eVn is the level number (1, 2, 3, ...).
Photon energy in a transitionEphoton​=Ehigh​−Elow​Take the gap between the two levels; it is always positive, for absorption and emission alike.
Photon wavelengthλ=Ephoton​hc​Energy in joules, wavelength in metres; a smaller energy means a longer wavelength.

Worked examples

An electron in hydrogen drops from level 4 (energy −0.85eV) to level 2 (energy −3.4eV). What is the energy of the emitted photon?
Show the solution
  1. The higher level is 4 (closer to zero); the lower is 2.
  2. Ephoton​=−0.85−(−3.4)eV.
  3. =−0.85+3.4=2.55eV.
Answer: 2.55eV (emitted)
An electron in level 2 of hydrogen (energy -3.4 eV) is hit by a 5 eV photon. What happens, and what is the electron's kinetic energy?
Show the solution
  1. Freeing an electron from level 2 needs 3.4 eV.
  2. The photon (5) exceeds 3.4, so the electron is freed.
  3. Kinetic energy = 5 - 3.4 = 1.6 eV.
  4. Check: 3.4 + 1.6 = 5 eV.
Answer: The atom is ionised and the electron leaves with 1.6 eV of kinetic energy
An electron in hydrogen is excited from level 1 to level 3 (energies −13.6, −3.4 and −1.5eV). How many different photon energies can be emitted as it returns? Which transition gives the longest wavelength?
Show the solution
  1. From level 3 it can drop straight to 1, or to 2 and then to 1: three transitions.
  2. 3 to 2: −1.5+3.4=1.9eV.
  3. 2 to 1: −3.4+13.6=10.2eV.
  4. 3 to 1: −1.5+13.6=12.1eV.
  5. The longest wavelength matches the smallest energy, 1.9 eV, the 3 to 2 transition.
Answer: Three energies: 1.9, 10.2 and 12.1 eV; the longest wavelength comes from the 3 to 2 transition
Common mistakes
  • Subtracting the wrong way and getting a negative energy. Subtract the lower level from the higher, remembering that -1.5 is higher than -3.4.
  • Thinking an atom absorbs any photon. It absorbs only a photon equal to a level gap exactly, except for ionisation, where any larger energy works.
  • Saying the longest wavelength comes from the biggest jump. The biggest jump gives the shortest wavelength.
  • Dropping the minus signs when substituting level energies.
Summary
  • Atomic electron energies come in fixed levels, which makes spectra line-like.
  • A downward jump emits a photon, an upward jump absorbs one, and its energy equals the gap.
  • Ionisation energy from a level is its energy without the minus sign.
  • The smallest gap gives the longest wavelength; the biggest gives the shortest.

Nuclear Structure and Radioactive Decay

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Find the numbers of protons and neutrons from a nuclear symbol
  • Write balanced alpha, beta and gamma decay equations
  • Compare how far each radiation penetrates

What is a nucleus made of?

A nucleus contains protons and neutrons, together called nucleons. The atomic number is the number of protons; the mass number is the total number of nucleons.

A nucleus is written like this: X614​X26214​C. The top number is the mass number (14) and the bottom is the atomic number (6). Neutrons = mass number - atomic number = 8. Isotopes are atoms of one element (same atomic number) with different numbers of neutrons.

Why do some nuclei decay?

Some nuclei are unstable, so they emit radiation to become more stable. This is radioactive decay, and it is random: we cannot tell when one particular nucleus will decay.

In every decay equation the total mass number before equals the total after, and the same holds for the atomic number.

Types of radiation

  • Alpha particle: a helium nucleus X24​X2224​He. The mass number falls by 4 and the atomic number by 2.
  • Beta-minus particle: an electron made when a neutron in the nucleus turns into a proton, together with an antineutrino. The mass number stays the same and the atomic number rises by 1.
  • Gamma rays: high-energy photons from an excited nucleus. The mass and atomic numbers do not change; only the nucleus loses energy.
  • Beta-plus (positron): emitted when a proton in the nucleus turns into a neutron. The mass number stays the same and the atomic number falls by 1.

Penetration, ionisation and deflection

Alpha is heavy and strongly charged, so even paper stops it. Beta is lighter and more penetrating; a thin aluminium sheet stops it. Gamma has no mass and no charge, so it penetrates most and needs a thick lead barrier.

Ionising power runs the other way: alpha ionises most because it loses its energy over a short distance, then beta, then gamma. In a magnetic field alpha and beta bend in opposite directions because their charges differ, while gamma, being neutral, is not deflected.

  2. Neutrons = 23 - 11 = 12.

Show the solution

  2. New mass number = 232 - 4 = 228.
  3. New atomic number = 90 - 2 = 88.
  4. Check: 228 + 4 = 232 and 88 + 2 = 90, so the equation balances: X90232​X2902232​Th​X88228​X2882228​Ra+X24​X2224​He.

A nucleus X92235​X2922235​U emits two alpha particles and one beta-minus particle. What are its mass and atomic numbers afterwards?
Show the solution
  1. Two alphas: mass falls by 2 × 4 = 8 and atomic by 2 × 2 = 4.
  2. After the alphas: mass 235 - 8 = 227, atomic 92 - 4 = 88.
  3. Beta-minus: mass unchanged (227), atomic rises by 1 to 89.
  4. Check: the mass fell by 8 only; the atomic number fell by 4 and rose by 1, a net fall of 3: 92 - 3 = 89.
Answer: Mass number 227, atomic number 89
Common mistakes
  • Thinking beta changes the mass number. A neutron becomes a proton, so the total number of nucleons stays and only the atomic number rises.
  • Thinking gamma changes either number. It is pure energy; the nucleus just loses energy.
  • Thinking alpha penetrates most because it is heavy. Its mass and large charge make it lose energy quickly, so a sheet of paper stops it; gamma penetrates most.
  • Mixing up the atomic and mass numbers when counting neutrons. Subtract the atomic number from the mass number.
Summary
  • Atomic number = protons, mass number = nucleons, neutrons = the difference.
  • Alpha: A falls 4, Z falls 2. Beta-minus: A same, Z rises 1. Gamma: neither changes.
  • In every equation the sums of A and of Z are equal before and after.
  • Penetration: gamma most, then beta, then alpha.

Half-Life

What you will learn
  • Understand what half-life means
  • Find the amount left after several half-lives
  • Find the half-life from the fraction remaining

What is half-life?

Half-life is the time in which half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample decay. In each half-life, half of what was present at its start remains, not half of the original amount.

After one half-life 1/2 remains, after two 1/4, after three 1/8, and so on. A sample never reaches zero in a finite number of steps.

Solving steps

  • Find the number of half-lives: total time ÷ half-life.
  • Multiply the starting amount by one half, once for every half-life.
  • In reverse: if you know the fraction left, count how many halvings reach it, then divide the time by that number.
  • If the time is what you need, multiply the number of halvings by the half-life.

Activity

The activity of a sample, its decays per second, falls by the same rule: it halves every half-life. Half-life is a property of the isotope and does not depend on the size of the sample.

Laws and rules

Number of half-livesn=T1/2​t​t and T1/2​ must use the same unit of time.
Amount remainingN=N0​(21​)nThe same law applies to mass, number of nuclei and activity.

Worked examples

A 64 g sample of an isotope has a half-life of 3 days. How much remains after 12 days?
Show the solution
  1. Number of half-lives = 12 ÷ 3 = 4.
  2. Fraction remaining = (21​)4=161​.
  3. Mass remaining = 64 ÷ 16 = 4 g.
  4. Check by halving: 64, 32, 16, 8, 4.
Answer: 4 g
25% of a radioactive sample remains after 14 hours. What is its half-life?
Show the solution
  1. 25% means 41​.
  2. 41​=(21​)2, so two half-lives have passed.
  3. Half-life = 14 ÷ 2 = 7 hours.
Answer: 7 hours
A 240 g sample has a half-life of 5 years. How many grams have decayed after 15 years?
Show the solution
  1. Number of half-lives = 15 ÷ 5 = 3.
  2. Remaining = 240×81​=30g.
  3. Decayed = original - remaining = 240 - 30 = 210 g.
  4. Check: the decayed fraction is 87​, and 240×87​=210.
Answer: 210 g
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the whole sample is gone after two half-lives. One quarter remains.
  • Answering for the decayed amount when the remaining amount is asked, or the reverse. Read what is asked first.
  • Thinking half-life changes with sample size or temperature. It is fixed for each isotope.
  • Halving only once when the number of half-lives is more than one.
Summary
  • Half-life is the time for half of the existing nuclei to decay.
  • After n half-lives, (1/2)n of the amount remains.
  • Number of half-lives = total time ÷ half-life.
  • The same rule applies to mass and activity, and the half-life of an isotope is constant.

Nuclear Fission and Fusion

What you will learn
  • Tell fission from fusion
  • Understand where nuclear energy comes from
  • Calculate the energy released from the mass lost

Nuclear fission

In fission a heavy nucleus such as uranium-235 is hit by a neutron and splits into two medium nuclei, releasing 2 or 3 neutrons and a great deal of energy.

The neutrons split other nuclei, causing a chain reaction. In a reactor it is controlled by rods that absorb neutrons, while a moderator (such as water or graphite) slows the neutrons so they split uranium-235 more readily.

Nuclear fusion

In fusion two light nuclei, such as isotopes of hydrogen, join to make a heavier nucleus such as helium, releasing energy. This is the energy source of the Sun and stars, and each kilogram of its fuel releases more energy than fission does.

The two nuclei have like charges and repel each other, so they approach only at extremely high temperature and pressure. That is why fusion is hard to achieve on Earth.

Where does the energy come from?

In an energy-releasing nuclear reaction the products have less mass than the reactants. The missing mass becomes energy according to Einstein's relation. The square of the speed of light is a huge number, so a tiny loss of mass gives a large amount of energy.

A nucleus has less mass than its protons and neutrons separately; this difference is the mass defect, and it corresponds to the binding energy that holds the nucleus together. The greater the binding energy per nucleon, the more stable the nucleus.

Binding energy per nucleon is greatest for nuclei near iron (about 8.8MeV). So heavy nuclei release energy when they split into two nuclei nearer iron, and light nuclei release it when they fuse; splitting nuclei lighter than iron, or fusing nuclei heavier than it, needs energy.

Nuclear masses are usually measured in atomic mass units, and a mass loss of one unit is equivalent to about 931.5MeV of energy.

Laws and rules

Energy from mass lossE=Δmc2Δm in kg, c=3×108m/s so c2=9×1016, and E in J.

  2. E=1×10−30×9×1016.
  3. Multiply the numbers: 1×9=9; add the exponents: −30+16=−14.

Common mistakes
  • Mixing up the names. Fission is a heavy nucleus splitting; fusion is two light nuclei joining.
  • Thinking mass is conserved here. The products have slightly less mass, and energy appears.
  • Forgetting to square the speed of light in the law.
  • Thinking fusion happens at any temperature. It needs extreme temperature and pressure to overcome the repulsion between the nuclei.
Summary
  • Fission: a heavy nucleus splits; fusion: two light nuclei join; both release energy.
  • The products have less mass, and the lost mass is the source of the energy.
  • Energy = mass lost × (speed of light)2.
  • Fusion needs very high temperature and pressure, and powers the Sun.

Semiconductors and the Diode

What you will learn
  • Understand how silicon becomes n-type or p-type
  • Identify the majority charge carriers in each type
  • Explain how a diode works in forward and reverse bias

What is a semiconductor?

A semiconductor such as silicon has four valence electrons and is a weak conductor when pure. Its conductivity rises with temperature, that is, its resistance falls, because heat frees more charge carriers. This is the opposite of metals, whose resistance rises with temperature.

In a metal the valence band overlaps the conduction band, so electrons move freely. In an insulator a large energy gap separates them and electrons cannot cross it. In a semiconductor the gap is small (about 1.1eV for silicon), so some electrons cross it when heated.

Doping: n-type and p-type

  • n-type: add atoms with five valence electrons. Each leaves one extra free electron, so the majority carriers are electrons.
  • p-type: add atoms with three valence electrons. Each added atom leaves a bond missing one electron; the gap is called a hole, so the majority carriers are holes.
  • Both types are electrically neutral overall; the name tells the majority carrier, not the total charge.

The junction and the diode

When p-type and n-type crystals are joined, electrons and holes near the boundary spread across and cancel, leaving a region with few carriers called the depletion region. This junction is the diode.

Forward bias: the battery's positive terminal on the p side. The depletion region narrows and a large current flows once the source voltage reaches the threshold voltage (about 0.7V for silicon). Reverse bias: the opposite, the region widens and almost no current flows.

The diode symbol is a triangle ending in a bar; its arrow points along the conventional current, from the p side to the n side.

If a diode and a resistor are connected in series to an alternating source, current flows in one direction only. This is rectification.

Laws and rules

Current through a forward-biased diodeI=RVsource​−Vdiode​​Vdiode​ (about 0.7V for silicon) is given in the problem; I in A and R in Ω.

Worked examples

Which type of semiconductor results from adding atoms with three valence electrons to silicon? What are the majority carriers?
Show the solution
  1. Silicon has four valence electrons; the added atom has three.
  2. Each added atom leaves a bond missing an electron, creating holes.
  3. Holes are the majority carriers, so it is p-type.
Answer: p-type; the majority carriers are holes
A forward-biased diode is connected in series with a 100Ω resistor and a 5.7V battery. The diode drops 0.7V. Find the current.
Show the solution
  1. Voltage across the resistor = battery - diode drop = 5.7 - 0.7 = 5 V.
  2. I=1005​=0.05A.
  3. Check: 0.05 × 100 = 5 V.
Answer: 0.05A
A diode and a resistor in series are connected to a 50 Hz alternating source. How does current flow in the resistor? How many pulses of current are there each second?
Show the solution
  1. In the positive half of the cycle the diode is forward biased and current flows.
  2. In the negative half the diode is reverse biased and almost no current flows.
  3. So the current flows in one direction only, in pulses.
  4. There are 50 cycles each second, with one pulse per cycle.
Answer: One-direction current in 50 pulses per second
Common mistakes
  • Thinking n-type carries a net negative charge. It is neutral; it is called n-type because its majority carriers are electrons.
  • Thinking a semiconductor's resistance rises with temperature like a metal's. It falls.
  • Reversing the bias: forward means the positive terminal on the p side, and the diode conducts.
  • Saying reverse bias stops the current completely. A very small current flows and is neglected.
Summary
  • A semiconductor's resistance falls as temperature rises.
  • Five valence electrons added gives n-type (electrons); three gives p-type (holes).
  • A diode passes a large current in forward bias and almost none in reverse bias.
  • On an alternating source it lets current flow one way only (rectification).
