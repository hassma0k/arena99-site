Linear Momentum and Impulse

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Calculate the momentum of an object from its mass and velocity.
  • Understand impulse as force multiplied by the time it acts.
  • Link impulse to change in momentum and find the average force in a collision.

What is momentum?

Momentum is the "quantity of motion" an object carries. The larger the mass or the speed, the larger the momentum, so a slow truck can have far more momentum than a small bullet.

We find it by multiplying mass by velocity; its unit is kg·m/s. Momentum is a vector: it points the way the velocity points, so motion to the right is positive and motion to the left is negative.

Impulse: force × time

To change an object's momentum, a momentary force is not enough; the force must act for some time. The product of force and the time it acts is called impulse, measured in N·s.

It equals the area under a force–time graph. For a constant force that area is a rectangle: height is the force, width is the time. Impulse is a vector that points the way the force points.

The impulse–momentum theorem

The impulse acting on an object equals the change in its momentum: final momentum minus initial momentum. It is Newton's second law written in terms of momentum.

That is why we lengthen collision times to reduce force: airbags and crumple zones stretch the stopping time, so the change in momentum stays the same but the force on the body is smaller.

  • A longer time with the same change in momentum means a smaller force.
  • When a ball bounces back, the direction reverses, so the change in momentum is the sum of the two magnitudes, not the difference.
  • When you catch a fast ball you pull your hand back with it: the stopping time gets longer and the force on your hand gets smaller.

Laws and rules

ImpulseJ=FtF is force in N, t is time in s, and J is impulse in N·s (the same as kg·m/s).
Impulse–momentum theoremFt=Δp=mvf​−mvi​vf​ and vi​ are final and initial velocities; keep the signs for direction.
Average forceF=tΔp​F in N, t in s, Δp in kg·m/s. Use this form to find the force in a collision.

Worked examples

A car of mass 1200kg travels at 20m/s. What is its momentum?
Show the solution
  1. Write the law: p=mv.
  2. Substitute: p=1200×20.
  3. Calculate: 1200×20=24000.
Answer: 24000kg⋅m/s
A constant force of 10N pushes a 0.5kg ball, initially at rest, for 0.5s. What is its speed afterwards?
Show the solution
  1. Impulse: 10×0.5=5N⋅s.
  2. The ball started at rest, so its initial momentum is zero and its final momentum is 5kg⋅m/s.
  3. Speed =mp​=0.55​=10m/s.
Answer: 10m/s
A 0.2kg ball hits a wall at 10m/s and rebounds at 6m/s. The contact lasts 0.01s. What is the average force of the wall on the ball?
Show the solution
  1. Take the direction toward the wall as positive: initial momentum =0.2×10=2kg⋅m/s.
  2. After rebounding the velocity is reversed: final momentum =0.2×(−6)=−1.2kg⋅m/s.
  3. Change in momentum =−1.2−2=−3.2kg⋅m/s, so the impulse is 3.2N⋅s opposite to the original motion.
  4. Force =0.013.2​=320N.
Answer: 320N, opposite to the ball's original direction
Common mistakes
  • Adding mass and velocity instead of multiplying, or using 21​mv2 (that is kinetic energy, not momentum).
  • Forgetting the sign at a rebound: the velocity after the bounce is negative, so the change in momentum is the sum of the two magnitudes, not their difference.
  • Mixing up impulse and force: impulse is force × time, so a small force acting for a long time can give more impulse than a big force acting briefly.
Summary
  • Momentum = mass × velocity, and it is a vector.
  • Impulse = force × time = area under the force–time graph.
  • Impulse = change in momentum.
  • Lengthening a collision time lowers the force because the impulse stays fixed.

Conservation of Momentum and Collisions

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Apply conservation of momentum to colliding or separating objects.
  • Tell elastic, inelastic and perfectly inelastic collisions apart.
  • Calculate the kinetic energy lost in a collision.

When is momentum conserved?

In an isolated system with no net external force, the total momentum stays constant. Momentum may pass from one object to another, but the total does not change.

The reason is Newton's third law: two objects push on each other with equal and opposite forces for the same time, so the impulses are opposite, and what one gains the other loses.

How to solve

Choose a positive direction, then write: total momentum before = total momentum after. An object moving against the positive direction gets a negative velocity.

  • An object at rest before the collision has zero momentum.
  • In an explosion or recoil (gun and bullet, boat and jumper) the total starts at zero, so the two parts move in opposite directions with equal momentum magnitudes.
  • The lighter object ends up with the larger speed.

Types of collision

In an elastic collision both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. In an inelastic collision only momentum is conserved, and some kinetic energy turns into heat, sound and deformation.

In a perfectly inelastic collision the objects stick together and move with one velocity; this loses the most kinetic energy. Remember: momentum is conserved in every collision, kinetic energy only in elastic ones.

  • When a ball hits an equal-mass ball at rest in a head-on elastic collision, the first stops and the second moves off with the first one's velocity: they exchange velocities.

Laws and rules

Conservation of momentumpbefore​=pafter​The sum of the momenta of all objects before the collision equals the sum after it (with signs for direction).
Perfectly inelastic collisionm1​v1​+m2​v2​=(m1​+m2​)vm is mass in kg, v is velocity in m/s, subscripts 1 and 2 label the objects, and the last v is their common velocity after sticking.

Worked examples

A 2kg cart moving at 3m/s sticks to a 1kg cart at rest. What is their common speed?
Show the solution
  1. Momentum before: 2×3+1×0=6kg⋅m/s.
  2. Mass after sticking =2+1=3kg, with unknown speed v.
  3. Momentum after =3v, and by conservation 3v=6.
  4. So v=2m/s.
Answer: 2m/s, in the direction of the first cart's motion
A 4kg gun fires a 0.01kg bullet at 400m/s. What is the recoil speed of the gun?
Show the solution
  1. Momentum before firing is zero because everything is at rest.
  2. Bullet momentum =0.01×400=4kg⋅m/s forward.
  3. For the total to stay zero, the gun must have 4kg⋅m/s backward.
  4. Recoil speed =44​=1m/s.
Answer: 1m/s, opposite to the bullet
A 3kg object moving right at 4m/s hits a 1kg object moving left at 4m/s, and they stick together. Find their speed afterwards and the kinetic energy lost.
Show the solution
  1. Take right as positive: momentum before =3×4+1×(−4)=12−4=8kg⋅m/s.
  2. After sticking: (3+1)v=8, so v=2m/s to the right.
  3. Kinetic energy before =21​×3×16+21​×1×16=24+8=32J.
  4. Kinetic energy after =21​×4×4=8J.
  5. Lost =32−8=24J.
Answer: 2m/s to the right, and 24J of kinetic energy lost
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting the negative sign for an object moving against the positive direction, which gives a wrong final speed.
  • Assuming kinetic energy is conserved in every collision; momentum is always conserved, kinetic energy only in elastic ones.
  • Adding two velocities instead of two momenta, or using one object's mass after they stick instead of the total mass.
Summary
  • In an isolated system, total momentum before a collision equals total momentum after.
  • Elastic: momentum and kinetic energy are both conserved.
  • Inelastic: momentum is conserved, some kinetic energy is lost.
  • In a recoil from rest, the two parts have equal and opposite momenta.

Work and Power

What you will learn
  • Calculate work when the force is along or at an angle to the motion.
  • Know when work is zero.
  • Calculate power from work and time or from force and speed.

Work

Work is done when a force acts on an object and moves it a distance. Work equals the force (along the motion) times the displacement, measured in joules: one joule is the work of one newton moving an object one metre.

If the force makes an angle θ with the motion, only its component along the motion counts: force × cos θ. If the force changes during the motion, the work equals the area under the force–displacement graph.

When is work zero?

No work is done if there is no displacement, however large the force (like pushing on a wall). No work is done if the force is perpendicular to the motion, because cos 90° is zero: gravity on an object moving horizontally, or a person carrying a bag while walking level.

Work is negative when the force points against the motion, like friction.

Power

Power is the rate of doing work: the work done each second. Its unit is the watt; one watt means one joule per second.

Two machines may do the same work, but the more powerful one does it in less time. When an object moves at constant speed under a steady force, power equals force × speed.

Laws and rules

WorkW=FdcosθW in J, F in N, d in m, and θ is the angle between force and displacement.
PowerP=tW​P in W, W in J, t in s. One kilowatt is 1000W.
Power and speedP=FvFor constant speed: F in N and v in m/s.

Worked examples

A worker pushes a crate with a horizontal force of 20N for 5m along the force. What is the work?
Show the solution
  1. The force is along the motion, so cos0°=1.
  2. W=20×5=100.
Answer: 100J
A force of 10N pulls a crate 8m with a rope at 60° above the horizontal (cos60°=0.5). What is the work?
Show the solution
  1. Horizontal component of the force =10×0.5=5N.
  2. W=5×8=40.
Answer: 40J
A crane lifts a 20kg object 3m in 4s at constant speed. What is its power? (g=10m/s2)
Show the solution
  1. Force needed equals the weight =20×10=200N.
  2. Work =200×3=600J.
  3. Power =4600​=150W.
Answer: 150W
Common mistakes
  • Always multiplying force by distance without checking the angle: if the force is perpendicular to the motion, the work is zero.
  • Thinking that carrying an object level does work because you are exerting a force; that force is perpendicular to the displacement.
  • Mixing up work and power: work is in joules, power is in watts and needs the time. Also forgetting to convert minutes to seconds.
Summary
  • Work =Fdcosθ, measured in joules.
  • Work is zero when there is no displacement or when the force is perpendicular to the motion.
  • Power = work ÷ time, measured in watts (J/s).
  • At constant speed, power = force × speed.

Kinetic and Potential Energy

What you will learn
  • Calculate kinetic energy and see how it changes with speed.
  • Calculate gravitational potential energy.
  • Apply the work–kinetic energy theorem.

Kinetic energy

Kinetic energy is the energy an object has because it is moving. It grows with mass, and grows much faster with speed, because speed appears squared in the formula.

If the speed doubles, kinetic energy becomes four times as large; if it triples, nine times. If the mass doubles, the energy only doubles. Kinetic energy is never negative and does not depend on direction.

Gravitational potential energy

It is the energy stored in an object because of its height above a reference level we choose. Lifting an object takes work against gravity, and that work is stored in it.

It depends only on the vertical height, not on the length of the path: a rise of 2m stores the same energy whether you lift straight up or push up a long ramp.

Another kind is elastic potential energy, stored in a compressed or stretched spring; it grows the more the spring is compressed or stretched.

The work–energy theorem

The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy: final minus initial. If the speed increases the net work is positive; if it decreases the net work is negative.

Laws and rules

Kinetic energyKE=21​mv2m in kg, v in m/s, KE in J.
Gravitational potential energyPE=mghg≈10m/s2, h is the vertical height in m, PE in J.
Work–energy theoremWnet​=ΔKE=KEf​−KEi​Net work and kinetic energy are both in joules.

Worked examples

What is the kinetic energy of a 2kg object moving at 5m/s?
Show the solution
  1. 21​×2=1.
  2. 1×52=25.
Answer: 25J
What is the potential energy of a 3kg stone at a height of 4m? (g=10m/s2)
Show the solution
  1. PE=3×10×4.
  2. =120.
Answer: 120J
A 1000kg car speeds up from 10m/s to 20m/s over 100m. Find the net work and the average net force.
Show the solution
  1. Initial KE=21​×1000×100=50000J.
  2. Final KE=21​×1000×400=200000J.
  3. Net work =200000−50000=150000J.
  4. Force =100150000​=1500N.
Answer: Net work 150000J, net force 1500N
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting to square the speed: kinetic energy is proportional to v2, so doubling the speed gives four times the energy, not two.
  • Dropping the factor 21​ in kinetic energy.
  • Using the length of the ramp instead of the vertical height for potential energy.
Summary
  • KE=21​mv2, and it is never negative.
  • Doubling the speed multiplies kinetic energy by four.
  • PE=mgh with h the vertical height.
  • Net work = change in kinetic energy.

Conservation of Energy and Friction

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Apply conservation of mechanical energy to falls, ramps and projectiles.
  • Explain where energy goes when there is friction.
  • Calculate the energy lost and the work done by friction.

Energy is neither created nor destroyed

Energy is never created or destroyed; it changes from one form to another. Mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic energy and potential energy.

If only gravity does work (no friction, no air resistance), mechanical energy stays constant at every moment: what the object loses in potential energy while falling, it gains as kinetic energy, and the reverse on the way up.

How to solve problems

Compare two points: the mechanical energy at the first equals that at the second, that is 21​mv12​+mgh1​=21​mv22​+mgh2​. The mass cancels, so the speed at a point does not depend on the mass.

A useful result: an object dropped from rest through a height h reaches speed 2gh​, whatever the shape of the path.

  • At the top of a vertical throw the speed is zero, so all the energy is potential.
  • Just before hitting the ground the height is zero, so all the energy is kinetic.
  • On a smooth track (a ramp or a pendulum) the speed depends only on the height difference between the two points, not on the shape of the path.

When there is friction

Friction opposes the motion and does negative work, turning some mechanical energy into thermal energy in the two surfaces. So mechanical energy decreases, but total energy (including heat) stays constant.

The mechanical energy lost equals the work done by friction: friction force × the distance slid. The object therefore ends up slower than it would be without friction.

  • Mechanical energy lost = initial mechanical energy − final mechanical energy = work done by friction.

Laws and rules

Conservation of mechanical energyKE1​+PE1​=KE2​+PE2​Valid when there is no friction or air resistance; energies in joules, subscripts 1 and 2 for the two points.
Speed after a free fallv=2gh​g=10m/s2, h in m, v in m/s.
Energy lost to frictionElost​=fdf is the friction force in N, d is the distance slid in m, and the result is in J.

Worked examples

A ball is dropped from rest from 20m. Ignoring air resistance, how fast is it moving just before it hits the ground? (g=10m/s2)
Show the solution
  1. At the top, all energy is potential: m×10×20=200m.
  2. At the ground, all energy is kinetic =21​mv2.
  3. Equate and cancel m: 21​v2=200, so v2=400.
  4. v=20m/s.
Answer: 20m/s
A ball is thrown straight up at 10m/s. What maximum height does it reach? (g=10m/s2)
Show the solution
  1. At launch the energy is kinetic only =21​m×100=50m.
  2. At the top the speed is zero and the energy is potential only =m×10×h.
  3. 10h=50, so h=5m.
Answer: 5m
A 2kg box slides from rest down a rough ramp 3m high and 6m long, reaching the bottom at 6m/s. Find the energy lost and the friction force. (g=10m/s2)
Show the solution
  1. Potential energy at the top =2×10×3=60J.
  2. Kinetic energy at the bottom =21​×2×36=36J.
  3. Energy lost =60−36=24J, turned into heat by friction.
  4. Work of friction =f×6=24, so f=4N.
Answer: Energy lost 24J, friction force 4N
Common mistakes
  • Applying conservation of mechanical energy when there is friction without adding the lost energy; what is conserved is the total energy, not the mechanical energy.
  • Using the length of the ramp instead of the vertical height for potential energy.
  • Thinking a heavier object falls faster or arrives faster; the mass cancels out (ignoring air resistance).
  • Saying energy "disappeared" because of friction, when it was turned into heat.
Summary
  • Mechanical energy = kinetic + potential, constant when there is no friction.
  • Potential energy lost equals kinetic energy gained.
  • Friction turns some mechanical energy into heat, so mechanical energy decreases.
  • Energy lost to friction = friction force × distance.

Simple Machines and Efficiency

What you will learn
  • Understand that a machine changes force or direction, not work.
  • Calculate mechanical advantage.
  • Calculate a machine's efficiency and see why it is always below 100%.

What does a simple machine do?

The lever, pulley, inclined plane, wheel and axle, wedge and screw are simple machines. They give us no free energy; they make the needed force smaller at the cost of a longer distance, or change the direction of the force.

A small effort moving a long way can lift a big load a short way, but the work we put in is never less than the work done on the load.

Mechanical advantage

It tells how many times the machine multiplies our force: the force the machine exerts on the load divided by the effort force we apply. For an ideal machine (no friction) it also equals the effort distance divided by the load distance.

For a lever it equals the effort arm divided by the load arm. Mechanical advantage has no unit.

The advantage found from the two forces is the actual mechanical advantage; the one found from the two distances or arms is the ideal mechanical advantage. The actual one is smaller because of friction.

Efficiency

In practice some of the input work is lost as heat through friction, so the output work is less than the input work. Efficiency is the ratio of useful output work (or energy) to input work, written as a percentage.

Efficiency of a real machine never reaches 100%, because friction cannot be removed completely. Reducing friction, for example by oiling, raises efficiency.

  • Efficiency can also be found from energy or power: useful output ÷ input × 100.
  • Efficiency = actual mechanical advantage ÷ ideal mechanical advantage × 100.

Laws and rules

Mechanical advantageMA=Feffort​Fload​​Both forces in N; the result is a pure number. For an ideal machine it also equals effort distance ÷ load distance.
Efficiencyefficiency=Win​Wout​​×100Both works in J; efficiency is a percentage, always below 100 for real machines.

Show the solution

A 600N crate is raised 2m using a 5m ramp, pushed with a force of 300N parallel to the ramp. Find the output work, input work, efficiency and mechanical advantage.
Show the solution
  1. Output work (on the load) =600×2=1200J.
  2. Input work =300×5=1500J.
  3. Efficiency =15001200​×100=80%.
  4. MA=300600​=2 (the ideal value is 25​=2.5, which is why efficiency =2.52​=80%).
Answer: Output 1200J, input 1500J, efficiency 80%, MA 2
Common mistakes
  • Believing a machine produces more work than is put into it; output work never exceeds input work.
  • Inverting the efficiency fraction: efficiency is output ÷ input, so it cannot exceed 100%.
  • Computing input work with the load's distance instead of the distance the effort force moves (the ramp length, for instance).
  • Thinking a large mechanical advantage means less work; it only reduces the force needed, not the work.
Summary
  • A simple machine changes force or direction; it does not increase work.
  • Mechanical advantage = load force ÷ effort force.
  • Efficiency = output work ÷ input work × 100.
  • Real efficiency is always below 100% because of friction.
