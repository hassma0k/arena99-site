Magnets and Field Lines

What you will learn
  • Know the attraction and repulsion rule for poles.
  • Draw the direction of magnetic field lines and understand what they mean.
  • Tell permanent magnets from temporary ones.

Poles

Every magnet has two poles, north and south, and you cannot separate one from the other. If you break a magnet in two, each half becomes a complete magnet with two poles.

Unlike poles attract and like poles repel. This rule underlies every use of magnets.

The magnetic field and its lines

Around every magnet there is a region where its force acts, called the magnetic field. We draw it with lines that leave the north pole and enter the south pole outside the magnet.

The lines are closer together where the field is stronger, at the poles, and farther apart where it is weaker. Field lines never cross. A compass needle lines up with the field line it sits on.

Where does magnetism come from?

A magnetic material contains tiny regions called domains, and each domain is a small magnet. In an unmagnetised piece the domains point in random directions and cancel each other; when it is magnetised they line up in one direction and the magnet appears. Hammering or strong heating scatters the domains, so the magnet loses its strength.

The Earth itself is a huge magnet. The north end of a compass needle is attracted towards geographic north, so a magnetic south pole lies near the geographic north.

Permanent and temporary magnets

A permanent magnet (such as magnetised steel) keeps its magnetism for a long time. Soft iron becomes a magnet only while it is near another magnet or inside a current-carrying coil, and loses it quickly when removed, so it is a temporary magnet.

That is why an iron nail is attracted to either pole: the end near the magnet becomes the opposite pole, so the two attract.

Laws and rules

Pole ruleunlike poles→attractUnlike poles attract; like poles repel.

Worked examples

A long magnet is cut in half. What do we get?
Show the solution
  1. The poles cannot be separated, so new poles form on each piece.
  2. The first half has a north and a south pole, and so does the second half.
Answer: Two smaller magnets, each with two poles.
The north pole of a magnet is brought near a soft iron nail and the nail is attracted. What pole forms at the nearby end of the nail?
Show the solution
  1. Attraction means the near end is opposite to the north pole.
  2. The opposite of north is south, so a south pole forms at the near end.
  3. When the magnet is removed the nail loses nearly all its magnetism, because it is a temporary magnet.
Answer: A south pole; the nail is a temporary magnet.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking a magnet can have only one pole. Poles always come in pairs.
  • Drawing the lines inside the magnet from north to south as well. Outside the magnet they run from north to south, and inside they run from south to north, forming closed loops.
  • Mixing up attraction and repulsion: like poles repel, unlike poles attract.
Summary
  • Unlike poles attract and like poles repel.
  • Field lines leave north, enter south, and never cross.
  • Closer lines mean a stronger field.
  • Steel is a permanent magnet; soft iron is temporary.
  • Aligned domains make a magnet, and the Earth is a huge magnet.

The Magnetic Field of a Current

What you will learn
  • Know that an electric current creates a magnetic field.
  • Find the field direction around a wire and the poles of a solenoid with the right-hand rule.
  • Calculate the field of a straight wire and of a solenoid.

A straight wire

Any electric current creates a magnetic field around it, in circles around the wire. Oersted discovered this when a compass needle turned near a current-carrying wire.

Right-hand rule: point your thumb along the current, and your curled fingers show the direction of the field around the wire.

What decides the strength?

The field grows with the current and weakens with distance from the wire. Double the distance and the field halves; triple it and the field is one third.

The reason is that the field spreads over a bigger circle as you move away.

The solenoid

A solenoid is a wire wound into many loops. The fields of the loops add up inside, giving a strong, uniform field like that of a bar magnet.

To find the north pole, curl the fingers of your right hand along the current in the loops; the thumb points to the north pole of the coil. The field is stronger with more current, more turns per metre, or a soft iron core inside.

Laws and rules

Field of a long straight wire in airB=r2×10−7I​B field in T, I current in A, r distance from the wire in m.
Field of a solenoidB=4π×10−7nIB field in T, n = turns per metre (number of turns divided by length in m), I current in A.

Worked examples

A long straight wire carries 5A. What is the field 0.1m from it?
Show the solution
  1. Write the law: B=r2×10−7I​.
  2. Substitute: 0.12×10−7×5​=0.110×10−7​.
  3. So 10−6÷0.1=10−5.
Answer: 10−5T.
The field 0.05m from a wire is 4×10−6T. What is it 0.2m from the same wire?
Show the solution
  1. The new distance is 0.2÷0.05=4 times the first.
  2. The field is inversely proportional to distance, so it becomes one quarter.
  3. New field: 4×10−6÷4=1×10−6.
Answer: 1×10−6T.
A solenoid has 500 turns over 0.25m and carries 2A. Estimate the field inside. (Take π=3.14.)
Show the solution
  1. Turns per metre: 500÷0.25=2000.
  2. Substitute: 4π×10−7×2000×2=4π×10−7×4000.
  3. =16000π×10−7=1.6π×10−3.
  4. With π=3.14: 1.6×3.14≈5.0, so B≈5×10−3.
Answer: About 5×10−3T.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the field stays constant far from the wire. It falls with distance: double the distance, half the field.
  • Mixing the hands: for a wire the thumb is the current and the fingers the field; for a coil the fingers are the current and the thumb is the north pole.
  • Using the coil length alone instead of turns per metre. What matters is turns divided by length.
Summary
  • A current makes a circular magnetic field around the wire.
  • The field is proportional to the current and inversely proportional to distance.
  • A solenoid gives a strong, uniform field inside.
  • The right-hand rule gives the field direction and the coil poles.

Magnetic Force on a Wire and on a Charge

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Calculate the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field, including a tilted wire.
  • Calculate the force on a moving charge and find its direction.
  • Understand why a charge moves in a circle and how its radius changes.

Force on a current-carrying wire

A current is moving charge, so a wire in a magnetic field feels a force. The force is greatest when the wire is perpendicular to the field and zero when the wire is parallel to it.

If the wire makes an angle with the field, only the perpendicular part counts, so we multiply by the sine of the angle between the wire and the field.

Direction of the force

Right-hand rule: fingers along the field, thumb along the current, and the force comes out of the palm. The force is always perpendicular to both the wire and the field.

Force on a moving charge

A charge at rest feels no magnetic force. A moving charge feels a force proportional to its charge, speed and the field, and it is zero if it moves parallel to the field. A negative charge is pushed the opposite way to a positive one.

The circular path

The force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it changes the direction of the velocity but not its size, and it does no work. The charge therefore moves in a circle, with the magnetic force acting as the centripetal force.

The radius is proportional to the mass and speed and inversely proportional to the charge and field: doubling the speed doubles the radius.

Laws and rules

Force on a wireF=BILsinθF in N, B in T, I in A, L length in m, θ angle between wire and field. At 90∘, sinθ=1.
Force on a chargeF=qvBsinθF in N, q in C, v in m/s, B in T, θ angle between velocity and field.
Radius of the pathr=qBmv​r in m, m in kg, v in m/s, q in C, B in T (velocity perpendicular to the field).

Worked examples

A 0.5m wire is perpendicular to a 2T field and carries 3A. Find the force.
Show the solution
  1. Perpendicular means sin90∘=1.
  2. F=2×3×0.5=3.
Answer: 3N.
A 0.4m wire carrying 5A makes 30∘ with a 0.5T field. Find the force.
Show the solution
  1. sin30∘=0.5.
  2. F=0.5×5×0.4×0.5.
  3. 0.5×5=2.5, then 2.5×0.4=1, then 1×0.5=0.5.
Answer: 0.5N.
A charge of 1×10−6C and mass 2×10−8kg moves at 100m/s perpendicular to a 0.5T field. Find the force and the radius of its path.
Show the solution
  1. Force: 1×10−6×100×0.5=5×10−5N.
  2. Radius: 1×10−6×0.52×10−8×100​=5×10−72×10−6​.
  3. =4m.
  4. Check: centripetal force rmv2​=42×10−8×104​=5×10−5N, which matches.
Answer: Force 5×10−5N, radius 4m.
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting the sine of the angle for a tilted wire, or using the angle from the normal instead of the angle between the wire and the field.
  • Believing the magnetic force speeds the charge up. It is perpendicular to the motion, so it only turns it.
  • Saying a charge at rest feels a magnetic force. No motion (or current), no magnetic force.
  • Reversing the relation: doubling the speed doubles the radius; it does not shrink it.
Summary
  • Force on a wire = B × I × L × sin of the angle.
  • Force on a charge = q × v × B × sin of the angle.
  • The force is perpendicular to both field and motion, so speed stays the same.
  • The radius of the circular path is proportional to the speed.

Electromagnetic Induction and Faraday's Law

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Understand when an induced current appears in a coil.
  • Calculate the induced emf with Faraday's law and for a moving rod.
  • Find the direction of the induced current with Lenz's law.

Electromagnetic induction

When the magnetic flux through a coil changes, an emf and an induced current appear in it. The flux can change by moving a magnet towards or away from the coil, by changing the current in a nearby coil, or by rotating the coil.

Magnetic flux measures how much field passes perpendicularly through a surface. If the flux stays constant, nothing is induced, even in a very strong field.

Faraday's law

The induced emf is proportional to the rate of change of flux, the change in flux divided by the time, and to the number of turns. So it grows if the magnet moves faster or the coil has more turns.

On a graph of flux against time, the emf is proportional to the slope of the line.

Motional emf

When a conducting rod moves at a steady speed perpendicular to a magnetic field, it cuts field lines and an emf appears across its ends. This is a special case of Faraday's law because the area of the circuit that the field passes through changes with time.

Lenz's law

The induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change that caused it. When a north pole approaches a coil, the near face becomes a north pole and repels it; when the magnet is pulled away, the face becomes a south pole and attracts it.

This opposition comes from conservation of energy: if the induced current helped the change we would get energy for nothing.

Laws and rules

Faraday's lawE=NΔtΔΦ​E emf in V, N number of turns, ΔΦ change of flux in Wb, Δt time in s. (The minus sign in the full law expresses Lenz’s law.)
FluxΦ=BAcosθΦ in Wb, B in T, A area in m2, θ angle between the field and the normal to the surface.
Motional emfE=BLvE in V, B in T, L rod length in m, v in m/s (motion perpendicular to the field).

Worked examples

A coil of 100 turns has its flux change by 0.02Wb in 0.1s. Find the induced emf.
Show the solution
  1. Rate of change of flux: 0.02÷0.1=0.2Wb/s.
  2. Multiply by the turns: 100×0.2=20.
Answer: 20V.
A 0.5m rod moves at 4m/s perpendicular to a 0.3T field in a circuit of resistance 2Ω. Find the emf and the current.
Show the solution
  1. emf: 0.3×0.5×4=0.6V.
  2. Current: 20.6​=0.3A.
Answer: 0.6V and 0.3A.
A coil with 50 turns and area 0.02m2 is perpendicular to a field that rises from 0.1T to 0.5T in 0.2s. Find the induced emf.
Show the solution
  1. Change in field: 0.5−0.1=0.4T.
  2. Change in flux: 0.4×0.02=0.008Wb.
  3. Rate: 0.008÷0.2=0.04Wb/s.
  4. emf: 50×0.04=2.
Answer: 2V.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking a strong field alone induces a current. The flux must change with time.
  • Forgetting to multiply by the number of turns.
  • Using the flux itself instead of the change in flux.
  • Reading Lenz's law as preventing the change. It only opposes it; it does not cancel it.
Summary
  • A changing flux induces an emf and a current.
  • emf = number of turns × rate of change of flux.
  • Moving rod: emf = B × L × v.
  • Lenz's law: the induced current opposes its cause.

Generators, Motors and Transformers

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell an electric generator from an electric motor.
  • Understand how a transformer works and why it needs alternating current.
  • Solve ideal transformer problems.

The generator

A generator turns kinetic energy into electrical energy. A coil spins in a magnetic field, so the flux through it keeps changing and an alternating induced emf appears, reversing every half turn. The current reaches the external circuit through two slip rings.

The emf increases with faster rotation, more turns, or a stronger field.

The motor

A motor works the other way: it turns electrical energy into motion. A current passes through a coil in a magnetic field, and two opposite forces on the sides of the coil create a torque that turns it.

The commutator reverses the current every half turn so the coil keeps spinning the same way.

The transformer

A transformer changes the value of an alternating voltage. Two coils are wound on an iron core: the primary is connected to the source and the secondary supplies the device. The alternating current in the primary changes the flux in the core, which induces a voltage in the secondary.

With steady current the flux would not change and nothing would be induced. More turns on the secondary makes a step-up transformer; fewer makes a step-down transformer.

Power and current

In an ideal transformer no energy is lost, so power in equals power out. If the voltage rises, the current falls by the same factor.

This is why voltage is stepped up for long-distance transmission: the loss in the wires is proportional to the square of the current, so halving the current cuts the loss to a quarter.

Laws and rules

Voltage ratioVp​Vs​​=Np​Ns​​Vs​,Vp​ secondary and primary voltages in V; Ns​,Np​ numbers of turns (ideal transformer).
Power conservationVp​Ip​=Vs​Is​Voltage in V, current in A, power in W. So currents are inversely proportional to voltages.

Worked examples

A transformer has 200 primary turns, 1000 secondary turns and a 20V primary voltage. Find the secondary voltage.
Show the solution
  1. Turns ratio: 2001000​=5.
  2. Vs​=20×5=100.
Answer: 100V (step-up).
A step-down transformer has a 220V, 2000-turn primary and an 11V secondary. How many secondary turns?
Show the solution
  1. Voltage ratio: 22011​=201​.
  2. Ns​=2000×201​=100.
Answer: 100 turns.
An ideal transformer raises 100V to 400V. The primary current is 8A. Find the secondary current.
Show the solution
  1. Input power: 100×8=800W.
  2. Output power is equal: 400×Is​=800.
  3. Is​=800÷400=2.
Answer: 2A (one quarter, because the voltage is four times larger).
Common mistakes
  • Thinking a transformer works on steady battery current. It needs alternating current so the flux changes.
  • Believing a step-up transformer increases power. Power stays the same (or drops); the current is what falls.
  • Inverting the turns ratio when finding the secondary voltage.
  • Mixing the generator (motion to electricity) with the motor (electricity to motion).
Summary
  • Generator: motion to electricity. Motor: electricity to motion.
  • A transformer works only with alternating current.
  • Voltage ratio equals turns ratio.
  • Power is conserved, so higher voltage means lower current.

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

What you will learn
  • Order the types of electromagnetic waves by wavelength and frequency.
  • Use the wave speed equation to find wavelength or frequency.
  • Know that all of them travel at the speed of light in a vacuum.

One family of waves

Electromagnetic waves are oscillating electric and magnetic fields travelling together. They need no material medium, so they travel through a vacuum, and all of them travel at the speed of light, 3×108m/s.

They differ only in wavelength and frequency, which decide their type and use.

The order of the spectrum

From longest wavelength to shortest: radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays. Moving from radio towards gamma, frequency and energy increase.

Visible light is a tiny part of the spectrum. X-rays and gamma rays carry a lot of energy and can damage living cells.

Wavelength and frequency

The wave speed in a vacuum is fixed, so if the frequency goes up the wavelength goes down by the same factor. A higher-frequency wave has a shorter wavelength and more energy.

What are they used for?

  • Radio waves: radio and television broadcasting and communication.
  • Microwaves: radar, communication and heating food in a microwave oven.
  • Infrared: remote controls and thermal imaging.
  • Visible light: sight; it runs from red (longest wavelength) to violet (shortest).
  • Ultraviolet: sterilising and making vitamin D in the skin; too much causes sunburn.
  • X-rays: imaging bones and finding fractures.
  • Gamma rays: treating tumours and sterilising medical instruments.

Laws and rules

Wave speedc=fλc=3×108m/s, f frequency in Hz, λ wavelength in m.

Worked examples

A radio wave has frequency 1×109Hz. Find its wavelength in a vacuum.
Show the solution
  1. λ=fc​.
  2. =1×1093×108​=0.3.
Answer: 0.3m.
Find the frequency of light of wavelength 6×10−7m.
Show the solution
  1. f=6×10−73×108​.
  2. 3÷6=0.5, and for the powers 108÷10−7=1015.
  3. 0.5×1015=5×1014.
Answer: 5×1014Hz (visible light).
A radio station broadcasts at 100MHz, that is 1×108Hz. What is its wavelength?
Show the solution
  1. 1×1083×108​=3.
Answer: 3m.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking some electromagnetic waves are faster than others. In a vacuum they all travel at the same speed.
  • Mixing up the order: radio has the longest wavelength and lowest frequency; gamma is the opposite.
  • Counting sound as part of the electromagnetic spectrum. Sound is a mechanical wave that needs a medium.
Summary
  • All electromagnetic waves travel at the speed of light in a vacuum.
  • Order: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma.
  • Speed = frequency × wavelength.
  • Higher frequency means shorter wavelength and more energy.
