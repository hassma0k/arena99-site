The Nature of Science: Hypothesis, Theory and Law

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Know the main branches of biology and what each studies
  • Tell a scientific question from other questions
  • Recognise a testable hypothesis
  • Distinguish a hypothesis, a theory and a law

What is biology and what are its branches?

Biology is the science of living things: their structure, functions, growth and interaction with the environment. Life is so wide that biology is split into branches, each focusing on one side.

  • Cell biology: the structure and functions of cells.
  • Genetics: how traits pass from parents to offspring.
  • Ecology: how organisms relate to each other and to their environment.
  • Taxonomy: grouping and naming organisms by their features.
  • Physiology: how body organs work.
  • Anatomy: the structure and shape of organs.
  • Microbiology: organisms seen only with a microscope, such as bacteria.
  • Embryology: the stages of development before birth.
  • Botany and zoology: the study of plants and of animals.

The scientific question and the hypothesis

Science starts with an observation that raises a question. A scientific question can be answered by observing, measuring or experimenting, for example: "How does the amount of light affect the height of a bean plant?" By contrast, "Which plant is prettiest?" is a personal opinion that cannot be measured.

A hypothesis is a tentative explanation or proposed answer to the question. It is scientific only if an experiment can test it and give a measurable result.

How do we know a hypothesis is testable?

A good hypothesis names what we will change and what we will measure, and is often written as "If … then …". Example: "If the amount of light increases, then the plant height increases."

A vague or taste-based statement such as "Plants love calm music" cannot be tested, because there is no exact way to measure "love".

Hypothesis, theory and law

A hypothesis is an educated guess that has not been tested yet. A theory is a broad explanation supported by much varied evidence from independent tests, and it can be revised if new evidence appears. A scientific law describes what happens regularly without explaining why.

A common error is to call a theory "an unproven hypothesis" or to say it becomes a law when its evidence grows. Neither is true: a theory answers "why?", a law answers "what happens?", and one never turns into the other. Examples: the cell theory and the law of gravity.

Laws and rules

Writing a hypothesisIf we change … then …A useful pattern: name the variable you change and the result you will measure.

Worked examples

Classify each statement as a hypothesis, theory or law. (a) Objects that are released fall toward the Earth. (b) If light increases, the plant will grow faster. (c) All living things are made of cells, which are the unit of structure and function, supported by much microscope evidence.
Show the solution
  1. For each statement ask: does it describe what happens, propose an untested explanation, or explain a broad phenomenon with much evidence?
  2. (a) It describes what regularly happens and does not say why, so it is a law.
  3. (b) It is an "If … then …" guess that has not been tested, so it is a hypothesis.
  4. (c) It is a broad explanation backed by much varied evidence, so it is a theory.
Answer: (a) law, (b) hypothesis, (c) theory.
A researcher studies how eye colour passes from parents to children. Which branch is that? Which branch studies the relationships among fish and plants in a lake?
Show the solution
  1. Look at the topic of the study, not at the kind of organism.
  2. The passing of traits between generations is the topic of genetics.
  3. The relationships between organisms and their environment are the topic of ecology.
Answer: Genetics for the first, ecology for the second.
Which hypothesis is testable? (a) Fertilisers are good for plants. (b) If 5mL of fertiliser is added weekly to a bean plant, its height after 3 weeks will be greater than that of an unfertilised plant. (c) Red flowers are prettier than yellow ones.
Show the solution
  1. Look for a hypothesis that states what will be changed and what will be measured, exactly.
  2. (a) is general and vague: "good" is not defined or measured.
  3. (c) is a matter of taste that no experiment can measure.
  4. (b) states the change (5mL of fertiliser), the measurement (height) and the time (3 weeks), so it can be tested.
Answer: Hypothesis (b).
Common mistakes
  • Thinking a theory is only a guess. In fact a theory is an explanation backed by much evidence; an untested guess is a hypothesis.
  • Thinking a theory becomes a law once it is proven. In fact each has its own job: a law describes, a theory explains.
  • Calling every "I think that…" statement a scientific hypothesis. It is scientific only if it can be tested by measurement.
  • Mixing up cell biology and anatomy. Cell biology studies the inside of the cell; anatomy studies the shape and structure of organs.
Summary
  • Branches of biology divide the study of life by topic.
  • A scientific question can be answered by measurement or experiment.
  • A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation made before the experiment.
  • A theory explains "why" with much evidence; a law describes "what happens".
  • A theory never turns into a law.

The Scientific Method, Observations and Data

What you will learn
  • Put the steps of the scientific method in order
  • Tell an observation from an inference
  • Tell quantitative data from qualitative data

Steps of the scientific method

The scientific method is an organised way of answering questions with evidence. The usual order is: observation, question, hypothesis, an experiment that gives data, then a conclusion. If the data do not support the hypothesis, the scientist revises it and tests again.

Scientists do not always follow a rigid order, but the hypothesis comes before the experiment, and the conclusion comes after the data are collected and analysed.

Observation versus inference

An observation is information we get directly with our senses or with measuring tools, such as "the plant leaf is yellow". An inference is an explanation we build from the observation using what we already know, such as "the plant needs water".

An inference can be wrong because one observation has several possible explanations: yellow leaves may come from too little water, light or minerals. That is why we test an inference with an experiment.

Quantitative and qualitative data

Quantitative data are numbers with units, obtained by measuring or counting: root length 7cm, 12 leaves. Qualitative data are descriptions in words, not numbers: the leaf is dark green, the stem feels rough.

Measuring gives more precision and makes comparing and graphing easy, while qualitative description is useful for shape, colour and behaviour.

Worked examples

Put these steps in scientific order: experiment, hypothesis, observation, conclusion, question.
Show the solution
  1. It always starts with an observation that raises curiosity.
  2. From the observation comes a scientific question.
  3. We propose a tentative answer, the hypothesis, before any experiment.
  4. We run the experiment and collect data, then write the conclusion.
Answer: Observation, question, hypothesis, experiment, conclusion.
A student sees that the leaves of a plant in the room are yellow and says, "This plant needs water." Which part is the observation and which is the inference? How can the student test the inference?
Show the solution
  1. "The leaves are yellow" is something we see directly, so it is an observation.
  2. "It needs water" is an explanation of the cause that has not been measured, so it is an inference.
  3. To test it, give extra water to one similar plant, leave another unchanged, and compare leaf colour.
Answer: The first is an observation, the second an inference; test it with a comparison experiment.
Classify each statement as quantitative or qualitative. (a) The root length is 7cm. (b) The stem feels rough. (c) The room temperature is 25∘C. (d) The plant bloomed with a purple flower.
Show the solution
  1. Ask: does the statement contain a measured number with a unit?
  2. (a) number and unit: quantitative. (c) number and unit: quantitative.
  3. (b) a description in words: qualitative.
  4. (d) a description of colour: qualitative.
Answer: (a) and (c) quantitative; (b) and (d) qualitative.
Common mistakes
  • Treating an explanation as an observation. If a statement contains "because" or a guessed cause, it is an inference.
  • Putting the experiment before the hypothesis. The hypothesis is written first and then tested.
  • Calling colour "quantitative" because it is obvious. Quantitative data are numbers with units; colour is a qualitative description.
  • Saying a hypothesis was "proven" or "disproven" for good. The data support the hypothesis or they do not.
Summary
  • Order: observation, question, hypothesis, experiment, data, conclusion.
  • An observation is direct; an inference is an explanation built on it.
  • Quantitative data are numbers with units; qualitative data are words.
  • If the data do not support the hypothesis, revise it and test again.

Designing an Experiment: Variables and the Control Group

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Identify the independent, dependent and controlled variables
  • Recognise the control group and its purpose
  • Spot a flaw in an experiment design
  • Explain why we repeat experiments and use large samples

The three kinds of variable

A good experiment changes only one thing and measures its effect. The independent variable is the single thing the researcher deliberately changes (for example, the amount of fertiliser). The dependent variable is what we measure to show the effect (for example, plant height); it changes in response to the independent variable.

Controlled variables are everything kept the same in all groups: seed type, soil, pot, water, light and temperature. If any of them changes, we cannot tell whether the independent variable or that change caused the result. Do not confuse them with the control group: controlled variables are factors we hold constant, while the control group is the group we compare with.

The control group

The control group is the group that does not receive the tested treatment (or gets the usual one) and is treated the same as the others in every other way. It is the baseline for comparison. The group that receives the treatment is the experimental group.

To identify it, ask: in which group does the independent variable not change? In a fertiliser test the unfertilised plants are the control. Without it we would not know whether the plants would have grown anyway.

Flaws in design

Common flaws: changing more than one variable at once, having no control group, not keeping other variables constant, using too small a sample, measuring only once without repeating, or measuring something unrelated to the hypothesis.

If two plants differ in both light colour and amount of water, the difference cannot be blamed on either one.

Repeating and sample size

Sample size is the number of individuals or trials in each group. A large sample reduces the effect of individual differences and chance, so the result is more reliable. Repeating an experiment means doing it several times: if the results are close, scientists trust them; if they differ widely, there may be an error or chance at work.

So a reliable test has one independent variable, a control group, controlled variables, a large sample and repetition.

Laws and rules

Rule of a fair testreliable test=one independent variable+control group+controlled variables+repeatsChange one thing, compare with a control group, keep the rest constant, and repeat with a large sample.

Worked examples

A student tests the effect of fertiliser on bean growth. Three groups get 0, 5 and 10mL of fertiliser weekly. Every plant has the same soil, pot, light and water (100mL), and height is measured after 3 weeks. Identify the variables and the control group.
Show the solution
  1. The independent variable is what the student deliberately changed: amount of fertiliser (0, 5, 10mL).
  2. The dependent variable is what was measured: plant height after 3 weeks.
  3. Controlled variables: soil, pot, light, water (100mL) and seed type.
  4. The control group is the one in which the independent variable did not change: the group given 0mL.
Answer: Independent: amount of fertiliser. Dependent: height. Control: the 0mL group.
A student tests the effect of light colour on growth. Plant A is under red light and given 200mL of water daily; plant B is under blue light and given 50mL daily. There is one plant per colour. What is the flaw, and how can it be fixed?
Show the solution
  1. Only one thing should differ between the plants: light colour.
  2. But the amount of water also differs (200 and 50mL), so we cannot tell whether light or water caused the difference.
  3. Also, one plant per light means we cannot be sure the difference is not chance.
  4. Fix: give all plants the same water, use several plants per group, and add a control group under normal light.
Answer: Two variables changed together (light and water) and the sample is small; keep water constant and enlarge the sample.
Two students ran the same fertiliser test. The first used 2 plants per group; the second used 30 plants per group and repeated the test three times. Whose result is more reliable, and why?
Show the solution
  1. Individual differences always exist: some plants grow faster by chance.
  2. With only 2 plants the whole difference could be chance.
  3. With 30 plants the individual differences balance out and chance matters less.
  4. Repeating three times shows the result can be reproduced.
Answer: The second student's result, because the sample is larger and the test was repeated.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the control group is the one that gets the treatment. It is the one that does not (or gets the usual one) so we can compare.
  • Mixing up dependent and independent. We change the independent variable; we measure the dependent one, which changes because of it.
  • Believing that changing several things is better because it is faster. Changing more than one variable stops us knowing what caused the result.
  • Relying on one test on one plant. Repeats and a large sample increase confidence.
Summary
  • Change the independent variable, measure the dependent, keep the controlled ones constant.
  • The control group does not receive the treatment and is the comparison.
  • Changing more than one variable at once ruins the test.
  • A large sample and repeats make the result more reliable.

Analysing Data and Graphs

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Put the independent and dependent variables on the correct axes
  • Read line graphs and bar graphs
  • Identify a data pattern and draw a conclusion
  • Calculate a mean and a percent change

Graph axes

The independent variable goes on the horizontal axis (x), and the dependent variable goes on the vertical axis (y). Each axis is labelled with the variable name and its unit and is divided into equal steps.

Example: in a graph showing plant growth over the days, time (days) is on the horizontal axis and height (cm) is on the vertical axis.

Line graphs and bar graphs

A line graph suits something that changes continuously over time or over ordered values. To read a value, go up from the horizontal value to the line, then across to the vertical axis. The slope of the line shows the trend.

A bar graph suits comparing separate categories, such as plant types or groups. The height of a bar is its value, so bars can be compared directly: if the bar of group A is 12cm and that of group B is 18cm, then B is higher by 6cm.

Data patterns and conclusions

A pattern is the general trend of the data: steady increase, decrease, no change, increase then levelling off, or a repeating cycle. We describe the pattern from the graph and then write a conclusion based on the data only, such as: "The more light, the greater the height."

A sound conclusion goes no further than the data. It says the hypothesis was "supported" or "not supported", and never claims absolute proof.

Mean and percent change

The mean sums up a set of measurements with one value: add the values and divide by how many there are. Percent change is useful when we want the change relative to the starting value; adding 5g to 20g matters more than adding 5g to 100g.

Always divide by the old (original) value, not the new one.

Laws and rules

MeanMean=number of valuessum of values​The values are measurements in the same unit, and the mean has that same unit.
Percent changePercent change=old valuenew value−old value​×100The result is in percent (%); positive means an increase, negative a decrease.

Worked examples

The heights of five seedlings were 8, 10, 12, 10 and 10cm. What is the mean?
Show the solution
  1. Rule: mean = sum of values ÷ number of values.
  2. Add: 8+10+12+10+10=50.
  3. There are 5 values, so divide: 50÷5=10.
  4. Check: 10 lies between the smallest (8) and the largest (12).
Answer: 10cm.
A plant's mass rose from 20g to 25g. What is the percent change? What changes if it falls from 50g to 40g?
Show the solution
  1. First change: 25−20=5g.
  2. Divide by the old value: 205​=0.25, then multiply by 100 to get 25%.
  3. Second change: 40−50=−10g, divided by 50: −5010​=−0.2.
  4. Multiply by 100: −20%; the negative sign means a decrease.
Answer: A 25% increase in the first case and a 20% decrease in the second.
A line graph shows plant height (cm) after 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 days: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. What is the height at day 4? What is the pattern? What height is expected at day 10?
Show the solution
  1. From day 4 on the horizontal axis go up to the line, then across to the vertical axis: 6cm.
  2. From day 0 to day 8 the height rose from 2 to 10, that is 8cm in 8 days, a rate of 1cm per day.
  3. The rise is the same every two days (2cm), so the pattern is a steady increase.
  4. Extend the pattern: from day 8 to 10 add 2cm, giving 12cm.
Answer: 6cm; a steady increase of 1cm per day; expected 12cm.
Common mistakes
  • Putting the dependent variable on the horizontal axis. The independent variable goes horizontally, the dependent vertically.
  • Forgetting the units on the axes or using uneven scales.
  • Dividing the percent change by the new value. Divide by the original, old value.
  • Concluding more than the data say, such as treating a prediction beyond the measured range as certain. Predict carefully and say it is a prediction.
Summary
  • Independent variable on the horizontal axis, dependent on the vertical.
  • A line graph shows continuous change; a bar graph compares categories.
  • The pattern is the overall trend, and the conclusion is built on it.
  • Mean = sum ÷ number of values.
  • Percent change = change ÷ original value × 100.

The Microscope and Scientific Units

What you will learn
  • Calculate the total magnification of a microscope
  • Use magnification to find real size
  • Know the scientific units and convert between them

Magnification of a light microscope

In a compound light microscope the image passes through two lenses: the objective lens close to the specimen, and the eyepiece we look through. Their powers multiply, so the total magnification is the product of the two powers.

Example: a 10× eyepiece with a 40× objective gives a total magnification of 400×. Higher magnification shows finer detail but the field of view gets smaller.

Magnification ratio and real size

The magnification ratio compares the size of the image with the real size of the specimen. If the magnification is 400, the image is 400 times larger than the real object.

If we know the image size (measured on a drawing) and the magnification, we find the real size by dividing the image size by the magnification.

Scientific units

Scientists use the metric system because it is agreed worldwide and based on multiples of 10. Length: metre, centimetre, millimetre, micrometre. Mass: gram and kilogram. Volume: litre and millilitre. Temperature: degrees Celsius. Prefixes show the multiple: kilo means a thousand, centi a hundredth, milli a thousandth, and micro a millionth.

Cells are very small and are measured in micrometres, so we often convert between millimetres and micrometres.

Laws and rules

Total magnificationTotal=eyepiece×objectiveBoth powers are plain numbers (times of enlargement).
Real sizeReal size=magnificationimage size​Image size and real size are in the same unit of length.
Useful conversions1mm=1000μmAlso: 1cm=10mm, 1L=1000mL, 1kg=1000g.

Worked examples

A microscope has a 10× eyepiece and a 40× objective. What is the total magnification?
Show the solution
  1. Rule: total = eyepiece × objective.
  2. Substitute: 10×40.
  3. The product is 400.
Answer: 400 times.
A cell appears 2mm wide in a drawing made at magnification 400. What is its real width in micrometres?
Show the solution
  1. Real size = image size ÷ magnification.
  2. 2÷400=0.005mm.
  3. Convert: 1mm=1000μm, so multiply: 0.005×1000=5.
  4. Check: 5×400=2000μm=2mm.
Answer: 5μm.
A microscope has total magnification 600 and an eyepiece of power 15. What is the power of the objective lens?
Show the solution
  1. From the rule: total = eyepiece × objective.
  2. So objective = total ÷ eyepiece.
  3. 600÷15=40.
  4. Check: 15×40=600.
Answer: 40×.
Common mistakes
  • Adding the two lens powers instead of multiplying. Total magnification is the product.
  • Multiplying image size by the magnification to get real size. We divide.
  • Forgetting the mm to micrometre conversion (1000, not 100).
  • Using non-metric units in a scientific test. Scientists use the metric system.
Summary
  • Total magnification = eyepiece × objective.
  • Real size = image size ÷ magnification.
  • 1mm=1000μm.
  • Scientists use the metric system.

Characteristics of Life and Levels of Organization

What you will learn
  • List the characteristics of living things
  • Explain homeostasis and response to stimuli
  • Order the levels of organization from smallest to largest

What makes something alive?

All living things share these features: they are made of one or more cells, they are organised in a specific structure, they obtain and use energy (metabolism), they grow and develop, they reproduce, they respond to stimuli, they maintain homeostasis, they pass on traits through genetic material, and over generations they adapt to their environment.

Showing one feature is not enough: fire grows, but it does not reproduce and is not made of cells.

Response to stimuli

A stimulus is a change in the internal or external environment that the organism senses, such as light, heat, sound, touch or water. The response is the organism's reaction to it, such as a plant stem bending toward light, or a hand pulling away from a hot object.

Responses help the organism survive: they bring it toward food and away from danger.

Homeostasis

Homeostasis is an organism's ability to keep its internal conditions fairly constant even when the outside environment changes, such as human body temperature staying near 37∘C. In hot weather we sweat and cool down; in the cold we shiver and warm up.

Homeostasis works by correction: the body detects a departure from the right value and then brings it back.

Levels of organization

Life is arranged from smallest to largest: the cell, then the tissue (similar cells doing one job), then the organ (tissues working together), then the organ system (organs cooperating), then the organism. Beyond the organism: a population (individuals of one species in one place), then a community (populations of different species living together), then an ecosystem (the community with its non-living surroundings), then the biosphere.

Worked examples

A plant on a windowsill bends its stem toward the sunlight. Which characteristic of life is this?
Show the solution
  1. Identify the stimulus: sunlight, a change in the environment.
  2. Identify the reaction: the stem bends toward the light.
  3. An organism's reaction to a stimulus is response to stimuli.
Answer: Response to stimuli.
On a hot day a person's body stays near 37∘C and the person sweats. Which characteristic is this? What does sweat do?
Show the solution
  1. The outside temperature rose but the inside stayed almost the same.
  2. Keeping internal conditions steady is homeostasis.
  3. Sweat evaporates, cools the body and brings the temperature back to the right value.
Answer: Homeostasis; sweat is a cooling mechanism.
Order from smallest to largest: organism, cell, organ, tissue, organ system.
Show the solution
  1. The cell is the smallest living unit.
  2. Similar cells form a tissue, and tissues together form an organ.
  3. Cooperating organs form an organ system, and systems together form the organism.
Answer: Cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism.
Common mistakes
  • Taking growth alone as proof of life. Life needs the whole set of characteristics together.
  • Mixing up response and homeostasis. A response is a reaction to a stimulus; homeostasis keeps the inside steady.
  • Putting organ before tissue in the order. A tissue is smaller than an organ.
  • Mixing up community and ecosystem. An ecosystem also includes the non-living environment.
Summary
  • Living things are made of cells, use energy, grow, reproduce, respond and keep balance.
  • A response is a reaction to a stimulus.
  • Homeostasis keeps internal conditions fairly constant.
  • Cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism, population, community, ecosystem.
