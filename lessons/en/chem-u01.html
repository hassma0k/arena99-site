Atomic Structure, Isotopes and Ions

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Find the protons, neutrons and electrons in an atom or ion
  • Tell atomic number from mass number
  • Calculate average atomic mass from isotopes
  • Order the atomic models by the experiments that changed them

What an atom is made of

An atom has a tiny, dense nucleus at the centre, and electrons moving in the large empty space around it. The nucleus holds positive protons and neutral neutrons.

Only one thing decides which element an atom is: its number of protons, called the atomic number. The mass number is the protons plus the neutrons.

  • Proton: charge +1, mass about one atomic mass unit, found in the nucleus.
  • Neutron: neutral, mass close to the proton's, found in the nucleus.
  • Electron: charge −1, about 1840 times lighter than a proton, so nearly all the mass of an atom is in its nucleus.
  • In a neutral atom: electrons = protons.

Isotopes and average atomic mass

Isotopes are atoms of the same element: same atomic number, different numbers of neutrons, so different mass numbers. They behave the same chemically because they have the same number of electrons.

The atomic mass in the periodic table is a weighted average of the isotope masses, using how common each isotope is (its abundance). That is why it is not a whole number. Chlorine is 35.5 because its lighter isotope is more common than the heavier one.

Ions

An ion is an atom that has lost or gained electrons. The number of protons never changes, because that would make a different element.

An atom that loses electrons becomes a positive ion and is smaller than the atom, since the nucleus pulls fewer electrons more tightly. An atom that gains electrons becomes a negative ion and is larger, since the electrons repel each other more.

How the atomic models developed

Each model was built to explain what the previous one could not.

  • Dalton: the atom is a small solid sphere that cannot be divided.
  • Thomson: after the electron was found, the atom was a positive sphere with negative electrons spread through it.
  • Rutherford: in the gold foil experiment most alpha particles passed straight through, so the atom is mostly empty space; a few bounced back, so there is a small, dense, positive nucleus at the centre.
  • Bohr: electrons move in fixed energy levels around the nucleus.
  • Modern quantum model: the electron lives in a probability cloud called an orbital, and its path cannot be fixed exactly.

Laws and rules

Number of neutronsneutrons=mass number−atomic numberMass number = protons + neutrons; atomic number = number of protons.
Average atomic massatomic mass=(m1​×a1​)+(m2​×a2​)m is the isotope mass in u and a its abundance as a decimal, so 20% is 0.2. The abundances add to 1.
Electrons in an ionelectrons=atomic number−chargeThe charge is positive for a positive ion and negative for a negative ion, so MgX2+ has charge +2.

Worked examples

An atom has atomic number 11 and mass number 23. How many protons, neutrons and electrons does it have?
Show the solution
  1. Protons = atomic number = 11.
  2. The atom is neutral, so electrons = 11.
  3. Neutrons = 23−11=12.
Answer: 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 11 electrons.
How many electrons are in the MgX2+ ion (magnesium has atomic number 12)? How many in the SX2− ion (sulfur has atomic number 16)?
Show the solution
  1. A neutral magnesium atom has 12 electrons.
  2. The charge +2 means it lost two electrons: 12−2=10.
  3. A neutral sulfur atom has 16 electrons.
  4. The charge −2 means it gained two electrons: 16+2=18.
Answer: MgX2+ has 10 electrons and SX2− has 18 electrons.
Chlorine has two isotopes: mass 35 with abundance 75%, and mass 37 with abundance 25%. What is its average atomic mass?
Show the solution
  1. Turn the abundances into decimals: 0.75 and 0.25.
  2. First isotope: 35×0.75=26.25.
  3. Second isotope: 37×0.25=9.25.
  4. Add: 26.25+9.25=35.5.
Answer: The average atomic mass is 35.5, closer to 35 because that isotope is more abundant.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking an ion is a different element: an ion keeps its protons, so it is the same element; only its electrons changed.
  • Finding neutrons by subtracting the electrons from the mass number in an ion: always subtract the atomic number (protons).
  • Averaging two isotopes by adding the masses and dividing by two: the average is weighted by abundance, and the more common isotope pulls the answer towards it.
  • Believing isotopes differ chemically: they differ in mass only, and their electrons are the same.
Summary
  • Atomic number = number of protons = identity of the element.
  • Mass number = protons + neutrons.
  • Isotopes have the same protons and different neutrons.
  • Atomic mass is an abundance-weighted average, so it is not a whole number.
  • A positive ion is smaller than its atom; a negative ion is larger.

Light, Atomic Spectra and the Bohr Model

What you will learn
  • Link wavelength, frequency and photon energy
  • Explain why each element has its own line spectrum
  • See how an electron absorbs and releases energy between levels

Light is a wave, and its energy comes in packets

Light is an electromagnetic wave with a wavelength and a frequency. The shorter the wavelength, the higher the frequency, because their product is the fixed speed of light.

Light is also given out and taken in as small packets of energy called photons, and a quantum is the smallest amount of energy that can be exchanged. The energy of a photon is proportional to its frequency (and inversely proportional to its wavelength), so violet light carries more energy than red.

The atomic spectrum: an element's fingerprint

White light through a prism gives a continuous spectrum with every colour. A heated gas of one element gives out only certain colours, seen as separate bright lines called a line spectrum.

Each element has its own line spectrum that no other element repeats, which is why it is used to identify elements in stars and in samples.

Bohr's model and electron jumps

An electron can only be in certain energy levels and never rests between two of them. The lowest level is level 1, the ground state.

When an atom is given energy, an electron absorbs a photon whose energy equals exactly the gap between two levels and moves up (an excited state). When it falls back to a lower level it gives out a photon with energy equal to that gap, which shows up as a line in the spectrum.

  • A bigger energy drop gives a photon of higher frequency and shorter wavelength.
  • The levels get closer together farther from the nucleus, so the gap between levels 3 and 4 is smaller than between 1 and 2.
  • In hydrogen: jumps down to level 1 give ultraviolet light, jumps to level 2 give visible light, and jumps to level 3 give infrared.

The limits of Bohr's model

Bohr's model explained the spectrum of hydrogen but fails for larger atoms. The modern quantum model describes the electron by a probability orbital instead of a fixed orbit, and the idea of fixed energy levels is still correct in it.

Laws and rules

Wavelength and frequencyc=λfc=3×108m/s is the speed of light, λ the wavelength in m, f the frequency in Hz.
Photon energyE=hfh=6.6×10−34J⋅s is Planck's constant, f the frequency in Hz, E the energy in J. Energy is proportional to frequency.

Worked examples

Light has a frequency of 5×1014Hz. What is its wavelength in metres?
Show the solution
  1. Wavelength = speed of light divided by frequency.
  2. 5×10143×108​=0.6×10−6.
  3. In scientific notation: 6×10−7.
Answer: The wavelength is 6×10−7m, that is 600nm, which is visible light.
In a hydrogen atom an electron drops from level 3 to level 2 once, and from level 3 to level 1 another time. Which drop gives light of higher frequency?
Show the solution
  1. The photon given out has energy equal to the gap between the two levels.
  2. The gap from 3 to 1 is larger than the gap from 3 to 2 (level 1 lies lower in energy than level 2).
  3. Energy is proportional to frequency, so the larger energy means the higher frequency.
Answer: The drop from 3 to 1 gives the higher frequency and the shorter wavelength.
A hydrogen electron is excited from level 1 to level 3. How many different lines can appear in the spectrum as it returns to level 1?
Show the solution
  1. From level 3 it can fall straight to level 1: one line.
  2. Or it can fall to 2 and then to 1: two jumps, 3→2 and 2→1.
  3. The different jumps are 3→1, 3→2 and 2→1, each with its own energy.
Answer: Three different lines.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking an electron gives out energy when it jumps to a higher level: going up needs energy absorbed, and falling down is what releases it.
  • Mixing up wavelength and energy: a longer wavelength means lower frequency and lower energy, so red light has less energy than violet.
  • Thinking an electron can sit between levels or absorb any amount of energy: it absorbs exactly the gap between two levels.
  • Believing all elements have the same spectrum: each element has its own set of lines.
Summary
  • Wavelength times frequency equals the speed of light, so they are inversely related.
  • Photon energy is proportional to frequency.
  • A line spectrum is a fingerprint of each element.
  • An electron absorbs the energy gap to go up and gives it out as a photon when it drops.

Quantum Numbers and Electron Configuration

★ Key
What you will learn
  • State the allowed values of the four quantum numbers
  • Write the configuration of an atom or ion, and shorten it with a noble gas
  • Apply the Aufbau principle, the Pauli principle and Hund's rule
  • Find valence electrons and unpaired electrons

The four quantum numbers

Every electron in an atom has an address made of four numbers, and no two electrons share all four.

  • Principal quantum number: 1,2,3… It sets the energy level and the distance from the nucleus.
  • Secondary (azimuthal) quantum number: from 0 up to (principal −1). It sets the sublevel shape: 0 is the spherical s, 1 is the dumbbell p, 2 is d and 3 is f.
  • Magnetic quantum number: from (-secondary) to (+secondary). It sets the orientation of the orbital in space.
  • Spin quantum number: +21​ or −21​, the direction the electron spins.

Levels, sublevels and orbitals

An orbital is a region where an electron is likely to be, and it holds at most two electrons. A principal level has as many sublevels as its number: level 1 has only 1s, level 2 has 2s and 2p, and level 3 has 3s, 3p and 3d.

  • The s sublevel: one orbital, holds 2 electrons.
  • The p sublevel: three orbitals, holds 6 electrons.
  • The d sublevel: five orbitals, holds 10 electrons.
  • The f sublevel: seven orbitals, holds 14 electrons.

Rules for filling orbitals

Three rules work together.

Aufbau principle: an electron goes into the lowest-energy orbital available. The order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p. Note that 4s fills before 3d.

Pauli exclusion principle: an orbital holds at most two electrons, and they must spin in opposite directions.

Hund's rule: when orbitals have equal energy (such as the three 2p orbitals) one electron goes into each orbital first, all spinning the same way, and only then do electrons pair up.

Short form, exceptions and ions

The noble-gas short form replaces the part matching the previous noble gas by its symbol in brackets, so sodium 1s22s22p63s1 becomes [Ne]3s1.

Two famous exceptions: chromium is [Ar]4s13d5 and copper is [Ar]4s13d10, because a half-filled or full d sublevel is extra stable.

To make a positive ion, remove electrons from the highest principal level first (so 4s before 3d). To make a negative ion, add electrons into the next empty place in the filling order. Chloride ClX− is [Ne]3s23p6, the same as argon.

Valence electrons

Valence electrons are those in the outermost principal level (the highest number), and they take part in reactions. For main-group elements count the s and p electrons of the last level only, so sulfur 1s22s22p63s23p4 has 2+4=6 valence electrons.

Laws and rules

Orbitals in a sublevelorbitals=2ℓ+1ℓ is the secondary quantum number: s (ℓ=0) gives 1, p (ℓ=1) gives 3, d (ℓ=2) gives 5.
Maximum electrons in a principal levelmaximum=2n2n is the principal quantum number: level 1 holds 2, level 2 holds 8, level 3 holds 18, level 4 holds 32.

Worked examples

Sodium has atomic number 11. Write its full and short configuration and its number of valence electrons.
Show the solution
  1. A neutral atom has 11 electrons, filled in order.
  2. 1s2 (leaves 9), then 2s2 (leaves 7), then 2p6 (leaves 1), then 3s1.
  3. Full form: 1s22s22p63s1.
  4. The previous noble gas is neon (10 electrons), so the short form is [Ne]3s1.
  5. The last level is 3, and it holds one electron.
Answer: 1s22s22p63s1 or [Ne]3s1, with one valence electron.
Nitrogen has atomic number 7. How many unpaired electrons does its atom have?
Show the solution
  1. Configuration: 1s22s22p3 (2+2+3=7).
  2. 1s and 2s are full: their electrons are all paired.
  3. 2p has three orbitals and three electrons. By Hund's rule each orbital gets one electron before any pairing.
  4. So each of the three orbitals holds a single electron.
Answer: Three unpaired electrons.
Iron has atomic number 26. Write the short configuration of the FeX3+ ion.
Show the solution
  1. Short form of the iron atom: [Ar]4s23d6 (argon has 18 electrons, and 18+2+6=26).
  2. The FeX3+ ion has lost 3 electrons.
  3. Remove first from the highest level, 4s: two electrons, leaving [Ar]3d6.
  4. Remove the third from 3d: it becomes 3d5.
  5. Check: 18+5=23 electrons, which is 26−3.
Answer: [Ar]3d5, a half-filled d sublevel.
Common mistakes
  • Removing 3d electrons before 4s when forming an ion: 4s fills first, but it is also removed first, because it is in the higher level.
  • Pairing electrons in p before each orbital has one: this breaks Hund's rule.
  • Counting 3d electrons as valence electrons in main-group elements: valence means the last principal level only.
  • Accepting a secondary quantum number equal to or larger than the principal one: it is always smaller, so level 2 has no d sublevel.
Summary
  • Four quantum numbers: principal (energy), secondary (shape), magnetic (orientation), spin.
  • The sublevels s, p, d, f hold 2, 6, 10, 14 electrons.
  • Aufbau, then Pauli, then Hund; and 4s fills before 3d.
  • A positive ion loses electrons from the highest principal level first.
  • Valence electrons are the electrons of the last principal level.

Organising the Periodic Table and Locating Elements

What you will learn
  • Explain what the modern periodic table is arranged by
  • Find an element's period, group and block from its configuration
  • Describe the well-known groups and the noble gases

What the table is arranged by

The modern periodic table lists elements in increasing atomic number, that is, number of protons. The horizontal rows are periods (7 of them) and the vertical columns are groups (18 of them).

Elements in one group have the same number of valence electrons, so they behave alike chemically. Properties repeat in a pattern because the outer electron arrangement repeats.

Mendeleev first arranged the elements by atomic mass, and Moseley then corrected the order by using atomic number.

The four blocks

The table is split into blocks by the last sublevel that gets filled.

  • The s block: groups 1 and 2 (and helium).
  • The p block: groups 13 to 18.
  • The d block: groups 3 to 12, the transition metals.
  • The f block: the lanthanides and actinides, shown as two rows below the table.

Finding the position from the configuration

The period number is the highest principal level in the configuration.

The group number: in the s block it equals the number of valence electrons. In the p block it is 10 plus the number of valence electrons. In the d block it is the outer s electrons plus the d electrons just before them.

Metals, nonmetals and metalloids

Metals fill the left and middle of the table: they are shiny, conduct heat and electricity well, and tend to lose electrons. Nonmetals are at the top right: they conduct poorly and tend to gain electrons.

Metalloids (such as silicon and germanium) sit along the dividing line between them and have properties in between, which is why they are used as semiconductors. Hydrogen is a nonmetal even though it sits above group 1.

Important groups

Alkali metals (group 1, except hydrogen): one valence electron, soft and very reactive metals that form +1 ions, getting more reactive down the group. Alkaline earth metals (group 2): two valence electrons, forming +2 ions.

Halogens (group 17): seven valence electrons, reactive nonmetals that form −1 ions; fluorine is the most reactive and reactivity falls down the group.

Noble gases (group 18): a full outer level (eight electrons, two for helium), so they are stable, very unreactive, and exist as single atoms.

Laws and rules

Period numberperiod=highest principal levelExample: a configuration ending in 3p5 is in period 3.
Group number in the p blockgroup=10+valence electronsValence electrons are the s and p electrons of the last level. In the s block the group number equals the valence electrons directly.

Worked examples

An element has configuration 1s22s22p63s2. What are its period, group and block?
Show the solution
  1. The highest principal level is 3, so the period is 3.
  2. The last sublevel filled is 3s, so it is in the s block.
  3. It has 2 valence electrons, and in the s block the group number equals that: 2.
Answer: Period 3, group 2, s block (magnesium).
An element has atomic number 17. Give its period, group, block and type.
Show the solution
  1. Configuration: 1s22s22p63s23p5 (2+2+6+2+5=17).
  2. The highest level is 3, so the period is 3.
  3. Valence electrons: 2+5=7, and the last sublevel is 3p, so it is in the p block.
  4. Group = 10+7=17, the halogens.
Answer: Period 3, group 17, p block; a halogen nonmetal (chlorine).
An element has configuration [Ar]4s23d6. Which block is it in, and what are its period and group?
Show the solution
  1. The last sublevel being filled is 3d, so it is in the d block (a transition metal).
  2. The highest principal level is 4, so the period is 4.
  3. Group = 2+6=8 (the 4s electrons plus the 3d electrons).
Answer: d block, period 4, group 8 (iron).
Common mistakes
  • Taking the last level written as the block: the block comes from the last sublevel filled, so a configuration ending 4s23d6 is in the d block.
  • Adding up all the electrons to get the group: you need the valence electrons, and for the p block you add 10.
  • Thinking helium is not a noble gas because its outer level holds only two electrons: its first level is full, which is enough for stability.
  • Mixing up period and group: a period is a horizontal row set by the levels, a group is a vertical column set by the valence electrons.
Summary
  • The table is ordered by atomic number: horizontal periods and vertical groups.
  • Period number = highest principal level.
  • A group has one number of valence electrons, so its members behave alike.
  • Blocks s, p, d, f come from the last sublevel filled.
  • Noble gases are stable because their outer level is full.

Periodic Trends: Radius, Ionization Energy and Electronegativity

★ Key
What you will learn
  • State the direction each property changes across a period and down a group
  • Explain each trend using nuclear charge and number of levels
  • Read successive ionization energies to find the valence electrons

Atomic radius

Atomic radius is half the distance between the nuclei of two neighbouring atoms. It gets smaller across a period from left to right, because electrons are added to the same level while the nuclear charge grows and pulls them in harder.

It gets larger down a group, because each new atom adds a principal level, which puts the outer electrons farther from the nucleus and shields them with inner electrons.

Ionization energy

First ionization energy is the energy needed to remove the outermost electron from a neutral gaseous atom. The closer and more tightly held the electron, the more energy it takes.

It increases across a period (smaller radius, stronger pull) and decreases down a group (the electron is farther away and shielded). The highest value in each period belongs to the noble gas.

There are two dips in period 3, and they repeat in every period (boron is below beryllium, and oxygen is below nitrogen): aluminium is lower than magnesium because its outer electron is in 3p, which has higher energy and is shielded by 3s. Sulfur is lower than phosphorus because sulfur has two paired electrons in one 3p orbital that repel each other.

Successive ionization energies

The second electron is harder to remove than the first, because the pull gets stronger once the atom is an ion. When the valence electrons are used up and the next one comes from an inner level, closer to the nucleus, the energy jumps sharply. The number of electrons removed before the jump equals the number of valence electrons.

Electronegativity and metallic character

Electronegativity is how strongly an atom pulls bonding electrons towards itself. It increases across a period and decreases down a group, and the highest value is fluorine's (about 4). In an HCl molecule chlorine pulls the bonding electrons harder than hydrogen does.

Metallic character is how easily an atom loses electrons, so it is the opposite of ionization energy: it decreases across a period and increases down a group, and the most metallic elements are at the bottom left (caesium and francium).

Laws and rules

Successive ionization energiesvalence electrons=number of energies before the big jumpIonization energy is measured in kJ/mol. Look for the first big jump between two successive energies.

Worked examples

Rank the atoms sodium, magnesium and chlorine (all in period 3) from largest to smallest radius.
Show the solution
  1. All three are in the same period, so they have the same levels.
  2. From the left the order is sodium, then magnesium, then chlorine.
  3. Across a period the nuclear charge grows, so the radius gets smaller.
Answer: Sodium > magnesium > chlorine.
Rank sodium, magnesium and aluminium from highest to lowest first ionization energy.
Show the solution
  1. The general trend rises across the period, so the rising order is sodium, magnesium, aluminium.
  2. But aluminium is an exception: its outer electron is in 3p, higher in energy and shielded by 3s, so it is easier to remove than magnesium's 3s electron.
  3. So aluminium is lower than magnesium, but still higher than sodium, which has a single distant electron.
Answer: Magnesium > aluminium > sodium.
An element's successive ionization energies (in kJ/mol) are 738, 1451, 7733, 10543. How many valence electrons does it have, and which group is it in?
Show the solution
  1. Compare each energy with the one before: 738 to 1451 is only about double.
  2. 1451 to 7733 is more than five times: a big jump after the second electron.
  3. 7733 to 10543 is a small rise (less than one and a half times), so there is no new jump.
  4. So only two electrons come off before reaching an inner level: two valence electrons.
  5. Two valence electrons in the s block means group 2.
Answer: Two valence electrons; group 2 (magnesium).
Common mistakes
  • Thinking atoms get bigger across a period because they have more electrons: the level stays the same while the nuclear charge grows, so the atom shrinks.
  • Forgetting the aluminium and sulfur exceptions when ranking ionization energy.
  • Reading successive energies by picking the largest value: look for where the big jump happens, not for the highest number.
  • Calling the noble gases the most electronegative because they are stable: electronegativity measures the pull on bonding electrons, noble gases rarely form bonds, and the highest value is fluorine's.
Summary
  • Radius: smaller across a period, larger down a group.
  • Ionization energy and electronegativity: increase across a period and decrease down a group.
  • Metallic character is the reverse: decreases across and increases down.
  • Two dips: aluminium below magnesium, and sulfur below phosphorus.
  • The big jump in successive ionization energies shows the number of valence electrons.

Radioactivity and Nuclear Decay

What you will learn
  • Tell alpha, beta and gamma radiation apart
  • Balance a nuclear decay equation using atomic number and mass number
  • Find the amount left after a number of half-lives

Why do some nuclei decay?

Some nuclei are unstable, because their neutron-to-proton ratio is wrong or they are too heavy. These nuclei decay by themselves and give out radiation to reach a more stable nucleus. This is called radioactivity.

Types of radiation

The three types differ in what they are and how far they penetrate.

  • Alpha: a helium nucleus (2 protons and 2 neutrons), charge +2. It is heavy and slow, so paper or skin stops it, but it is the most harmful if it gets inside the body.
  • Beta: a fast electron from the nucleus, where a neutron turns into a proton. Charge −1; a thin sheet of aluminium stops it.
  • Gamma: a high-energy electromagnetic wave with no mass and no charge. It penetrates most, and only thick lead or concrete stops it.

Decay equations

In every nuclear equation the mass numbers add up to the same total on both sides, and so do the atomic numbers.

Alpha emission lowers the atomic number by 2 and the mass number by 4. Beta emission raises the atomic number by 1 and leaves the mass number unchanged. Gamma changes neither.

Half-life

The half-life is the time it takes for half of a radioactive sample to decay. After one half-life half remains, after two a quarter remains, and so on, whatever the starting amount. The half-life is fixed for each isotope and is not changed by heat or pressure.

Laws and rules

Mass left after half-livesm=m0​(21​)km0​ is the starting mass, m the mass left (same unit), and k the number of half-lives.
Number of half-livesk=t1/2​t​t is the total time and t1/2​ the half-life, in the same unit (seconds, days or years).

Worked examples

Radium X88226​X2882226​Ra decays by emitting an alpha particle. Write the equation and name the product nucleus.
Show the solution
  1. An alpha particle is X24​X2224​He.
  2. Mass number of the product: 226−4=222.
  3. Atomic number of the product: 88−2=86, which is radon.
Answer: X88226​X2882226​Ra​X86222​X2862222​Rn+X24​X2224​He
Carbon X614​X26214​C decays by beta emission. What nucleus is produced?
Show the solution
  1. A beta particle is an electron, X−10​X2−1​20​e (mass number 0, charge −1).
  2. The mass number stays 14, since the two masses on the right must add to 14.
  3. Atomic number: 6=Z+(−1), so Z=7, which is nitrogen.
Answer: X614​X26214​C​X714​X27214​N+X−10​X2−1​20​e
A radioactive sample has a mass of 80g and its isotope has a half-life of 5 days. How many grams remain after 15 days?
Show the solution
  1. Number of half-lives = 15÷5=3.
  2. After the first half-life: 80÷2=40g.
  3. After the second: 40÷2=20g.
  4. After the third: 20÷2=10g.
  5. Check with the law: 80×81​=10.
Answer: 10g remain.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking beta emission changes the mass number: beta only raises the atomic number by 1, and the mass number is unchanged.
  • Thinking the half-life is the time for the whole sample to decay: after two half-lives a quarter is still left.
  • Mixing up penetration and danger: alpha penetrates least but is very harmful inside the body, while gamma penetrates most.
  • Leaving the nuclear equation unbalanced: check that the mass numbers and the atomic numbers each add up the same on both sides.
Summary
  • Unstable nuclei decay and give out alpha, beta or gamma.
  • Alpha is a helium nucleus, beta is an electron, gamma is a massless wave.
  • Alpha lowers the atomic number by 2 and the mass number by 4; beta raises the atomic number by 1.
  • Each half-life cuts the amount in half.
