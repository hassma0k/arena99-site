The Heart, Vessels and Circulation

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Name the heart chambers and valves and what each does
  • Trace blood through the pulmonary and systemic circuits
  • Tell arteries, veins and capillaries apart
  • Calculate cardiac output at rest and during exercise

The heart: a pump with two sides

The heart is a muscle that pumps blood around two linked circuits. Its right side sends blood to the lungs and its left side sends blood to the whole body.

Each side has two chambers: the atrium receives blood and the ventricle pumps it out. Atria are thin-walled receiving chambers; ventricles are thick, muscular pumping chambers.

The left ventricle wall is thicker than the right, because the left pushes blood a long way around the body at high pressure while the right pushes blood only to the nearby lungs.

Valves and heart sounds

A valve lets blood move in one direction only. Between each atrium and ventricle there is an atrioventricular valve: tricuspid on the right and bicuspid on the left. Where blood leaves the ventricles there are two semilunar valves: pulmonary and aortic.

The first sound, "lub", comes from the atrioventricular valves closing as the ventricles start to contract. The second sound, "dub", comes from the semilunar valves closing after blood has left. The sounds are valves shutting, not blood flowing.

Valves stop backflow. If a valve is damaged, blood leaks backward and the heart works harder.

The path of blood through both circuits

Pulmonary circuit: oxygen-poor blood leaves the right ventricle through the pulmonary artery to the lungs, picks up oxygen and loses carbon dioxide, then returns through the pulmonary vein to the left atrium.

Systemic circuit: oxygen-rich blood leaves the left ventricle through the aorta to the body tissues, then returns oxygen-poor through the venae cavae to the right atrium.

Note the key exception: the pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood and the pulmonary vein carries oxygen-rich blood. Names follow direction, not blood type: arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins carry it toward the heart.

  • Right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs
  • Lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body

Arteries, veins and capillaries

Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure, so their walls are thick, elastic and muscular. Their elastic walls stretch with each surge from the heart and then recoil, and that is the pulse we feel at the wrist. We do not feel a pulse in veins, because blood reaches them at low pressure and flows smoothly.

Veins return blood to the heart at low pressure. Their walls are thinner and they contain valves that prevent backflow, especially in the legs where blood climbs against gravity helped by squeezing muscles. If these valves are damaged, blood pools in the legs.

Capillaries are the finest vessels. Their wall is one cell thick, and it is here that gases and nutrients are exchanged with cells. Blood flows slowly in them even though they are tiny, because the combined cross-section of millions of capillaries is much larger than that of the aorta. The slow flow gives time for exchange.

Cardiac output

Cardiac output is the volume of blood one ventricle pumps in one minute. It depends on two things: how many times the heart beats per minute and how much blood is pumped in each beat.

During exercise the muscles need more oxygen, so both the heart rate and the stroke volume rise, and cardiac output increases several times.

Laws and rules

Cardiac outputCardiac output=Heart rate×Stroke volumeCardiac output in mL or L per minute, heart rate in beats per minute, stroke volume in mL per beat. 1000 mL = 1 L.

Worked examples

At rest a person's heart beats 60 times per minute and the ventricle pumps 75 mL each beat. What is the cardiac output in litres per minute?
Show the solution
  1. Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume.
  2. Output = 60×75=4500 mL per minute.
  3. Convert: 4500÷1000=4.5 L.
Answer: 4.5 L per minute.
During exercise the heart rate rises to 150 beats per minute and the stroke volume to 100 mL. By how many litres did the output rise above the resting value of the previous example?
Show the solution
  1. Output in exercise = 150×100=15000 mL = 15 L.
  2. Output at rest = 4.5 L.
  3. Rise = 15−4.5=10.5 L.
  4. Check: 4.5×3=13.5 is less than 15, so the output more than tripled, which fits.
Answer: It rose by 10.5 L per minute.
Follow a drop of oxygen-poor blood from the leg to the aorta.
Show the solution
  1. From the leg it returns through the inferior vena cava to the right atrium.
  2. It passes the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle.
  3. The right ventricle pushes it through the pulmonary valve into the pulmonary artery and the lungs, where it gains oxygen.
  4. It returns by the pulmonary vein to the left atrium, then through the bicuspid valve to the left ventricle.
  5. The left ventricle pushes it through the aortic valve into the aorta.
Answer: Leg → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking every artery carries oxygen-rich blood and every vein oxygen-poor blood. In fact the pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood and the pulmonary vein oxygen-rich blood, because the name follows direction relative to the heart.
  • Confusing the heart sounds with blood flowing. The sounds come from valves closing.
  • Saying blood is fast in capillaries because they are tiny. It is slow because their total cross-section is huge, which gives time for exchange.
  • Forgetting to convert mL to L when calculating cardiac output.
Summary
  • The heart is a two-sided pump: right to the lungs, left to the body; atria receive and ventricles pump.
  • The left ventricle is thicker because it pumps to the whole body.
  • Valves keep blood moving one way, and their closing makes the heart sounds.
  • Arteries are thick and pulsing, veins have valves, capillaries are one cell thick and slow.
  • Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume, and it rises with exercise.

Breathing: Air In and Air Out

What you will learn
  • Describe the air path from nose to alveoli
  • Explain how air enters in inhalation and leaves in exhalation
  • Explain the effect of smoking, asthma and emphysema
  • Calculate minute ventilation and tell cellular respiration from breathing

The air path

Air passes from the nose through the pharynx and larynx into the trachea, which divides into two bronchi; each bronchus divides into fine bronchioles that end in alveoli.

The trachea wall has C-shaped cartilage rings that keep it open so it does not collapse during inhalation.

The lining has ciliated cells and mucus-secreting glands. Mucus traps dust and germs, and the cilia sweep like a brush, pushing the mucus upward toward the throat to be swallowed or coughed out.

The alveoli

Alveoli are tiny sacs numbering in the millions, and they are where gases are exchanged. Each alveolus has a wall one thin, moist cell thick, wrapped in a network of capillaries.

The huge number gives a huge surface area, the thin wall shortens the diffusion distance, moisture dissolves the gases, and the capillaries carry gas away so diffusion goes on continuously.

Inhalation: how does air get in?

In inhalation the diaphragm contracts, flattening and moving down, and the external intercostal muscles contract and lift the rib cage up and forward. The volume of the chest cavity increases.

When volume increases, pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric pressure, so air flows from the higher pressure (outside) to the lower pressure (lungs). We do not pull air in; atmospheric pressure pushes it in.

Quiet exhalation needs no effort: the diaphragm and rib muscles relax, the diaphragm rises, the elastic lungs recoil, the volume falls, the pressure rises and air leaves. The internal intercostal and abdominal muscles contract only in forced exhalation.

Control of breathing

The breathing centre in the medulla of the brain sends regular signals to the breathing muscles. The main trigger is a rise in carbon dioxide in the blood (which lowers blood pH), not a lack of oxygen.

During exercise carbon dioxide rises, so the centre increases the rate and depth of breaths.

Minute ventilation

Minute ventilation is the volume of air entering the lungs in one minute. It equals the number of breaths per minute multiplied by the volume of air in each breath.

What harms the respiratory system?

  • Smoking: it paralyses and destroys cilia, so mucus and germs collect in the airways, causing chronic cough and frequent infections.
  • Emphysema: the alveolar walls break down, reducing the surface area for exchange and the elasticity of the lung, so exhaling is hard and less oxygen reaches the blood.
  • Asthma attack: the muscles of the bronchioles tighten, the lining swells and makes more mucus, so the airways narrow and air is hard to push out. A bronchodilator relaxes these muscles.

Cellular respiration: why do we breathe at all?

Breathing brings in oxygen; cellular respiration is the process that uses oxygen inside every cell to release energy from glucose as ATP. Most of it happens in the mitochondria, the cell's power stations.

The products of cellular respiration are carbon dioxide and water, plus energy. The carbon dioxide diffuses from the cells into the blood, reaches the lungs and leaves in the exhaled air.

Laws and rules

Minute ventilationMinute ventilation=Breaths per minute×Volume per breathBreaths per minute, volume of one breath in mL, result in mL per minute.
Cellular respirationCX6​HX12​OX6​+6OX2​​6COX2​+6HX2​OGlucose and oxygen give carbon dioxide and water, and energy is released and stored in ATP.

Worked examples

A person takes 12 breaths per minute and each breath is 500 mL. What is the minute ventilation in litres?
Show the solution
  1. Minute ventilation = breaths per minute × volume per breath.
  2. 12×500=6000 mL.
  3. In litres: 6000÷1000=6.
Answer: 6 L per minute.
While running, the rate is 20 breaths per minute and each breath is 1000 mL. How many times larger is the ventilation than in the previous example?
Show the solution
  1. Ventilation while running = 20×1000=20000 mL = 20 L.
  2. Ventilation at rest = 6 L.
  3. Ratio = 20÷6≈3.3 times.
  4. Check: the rate rose about 1.7 times and the breath size doubled, and 1.7×2≈3.3.
Answer: About 3.3 times larger.
In inhalation the pressure in the lungs falls to 758 mmHg while atmospheric pressure is 760 mmHg. Which way does air move, and why?
Show the solution
  1. Air moves from higher pressure to lower pressure.
  2. Outside pressure 760 is higher than 758 in the lungs.
  3. So air moves from outside into the lungs.
  4. The root cause: the diaphragm and rib muscles contracted, the chest volume rose, so the pressure fell.
Answer: Air enters the lungs because their pressure is lower than atmospheric pressure.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the lungs pull air in by their own force. Lung tissue is elastic and cannot expand by itself; the diaphragm and the rib muscles change the volume, which changes the pressure.
  • Saying the diaphragm rises in inhalation. It contracts, flattens and moves down.
  • Mixing up breathing (gas exchange with air) and cellular respiration (making energy in mitochondria).
  • Believing low oxygen is the main trigger of breathing. The rise in carbon dioxide is the main trigger.
Summary
  • Air passes trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli; mucus and cilia clean it.
  • Inhalation: diaphragm and ribs contract, volume rises, pressure falls, air enters.
  • Quiet exhalation is passive, from the elastic recoil of the lungs.
  • Smoking paralyses cilia, emphysema destroys alveoli, asthma narrows bronchioles.
  • Cellular respiration in mitochondria releases energy from glucose using oxygen.

Blood and Gas Transport

What you will learn
  • List the parts of blood and what each does
  • Decide who can give and who can receive in blood groups
  • Explain how blood carries oxygen and carbon dioxide
  • Explain the effects of carbon monoxide, altitude and iron

Components of blood

Blood is a liquid tissue made of plasma (about 55%) and blood cells (about 45%). Plasma is a yellowish liquid, about 90% water, carrying dissolved nutrients, salts, hormones, wastes and proteins such as fibrinogen, which helps clotting.

Red blood cells are biconcave discs with no nucleus in adults, which leaves room for hemoglobin. They are made in bone marrow and live about 120 days. Their job is to carry oxygen.

White blood cells are larger, have a nucleus and are fewer. Their job is to defend the body by attacking germs.

Platelets are small cell fragments with no nucleus. They gather at a wound, plug it and start clotting, which forms the clot that stops bleeding.

Blood groups

A blood group is set by antigens (molecules) on the surface of red cells. In the ABO system: group A has antigen A, group B has antigen B, AB has both, and O has none. The plasma carries antibodies against the antigens you lack: group A has anti-B, group B has anti-A, O has both, and AB has none.

If blood with a foreign antigen is transfused, the antibodies attack it, the red cells clump, and serious problems follow.

So group O can give red cells to all groups (universal donor) because its cells have no antigens, and AB can receive from all (universal recipient) because its plasma has no antibodies. A blood group also carries an Rh factor, positive or negative.

Hemoglobin and oxygen transport

Hemoglobin is a protein in red cells that contains iron. Oxygen binds to the iron in the lungs forming oxyhemoglobin, and is released in the tissues.

Iron is needed to make hemoglobin, so too little iron in the diet causes anemia: less hemoglobin means less oxygen carried, and the person feels tired and weak and looks pale. Liver, meat and legumes are good iron sources.

In active tissues oxygen is low while carbon dioxide, acidity and temperature are high, which weakens the hold of hemoglobin on oxygen, so oxygen is released easily to the cells that need it most. In the lungs, where oxygen is high, hemoglobin picks it up.

Gas diffusion

Gases always diffuse from where their partial pressure is high to where it is low. In the alveolus oxygen pressure is high (about 100 mmHg) and in blood arriving from the body it is low (about 40), so oxygen diffuses into the blood. Carbon dioxide pressure is higher in the blood (about 45) than in the alveolus (about 40), so it diffuses into the alveolus.

In the tissues the directions reverse: oxygen goes from blood to cells and carbon dioxide from cells to blood.

Carbon dioxide transport and acidity

Blood carries carbon dioxide in three ways: about 70% as bicarbonate ions in the plasma, about 23% bound to hemoglobin, and about 7% dissolved in the plasma.

In the red cell, carbon dioxide combines with water with the help of an enzyme to form carbonic acid, which splits into a hydrogen ion and bicarbonate: COX2​+HX2​O​HX2​COX3​​HX++HCOX3​X−.

The more carbon dioxide, the more hydrogen ions and the lower the blood pH (more acidic). The medulla senses this and speeds up breathing.

Carbon monoxide and high altitude

Carbon monoxide is a colourless, odourless gas. It binds hemoglobin hundreds of times more strongly than oxygen and takes its place, blocking oxygen transport and causing suffocation without the person noticing.

At high altitude the air is still 21% oxygen but atmospheric pressure is lower, so the oxygen pressure falls. The body adapts over weeks: the kidney releases the hormone erythropoietin, which raises red cell production and hemoglobin, along with faster breathing.

Laws and rules

Carbon dioxide transport reactionCOX2​+HX2​O​HX2​COX3​​HX++HCOX3​X−Most carbon dioxide becomes bicarbonate in the plasma, and the released hydrogen ion lowers pH.

Worked examples

A person has 5 L of blood. How many litres are plasma and how many are cells?
Show the solution
  1. Plasma 55%: 5×0.55=2.75 L.
  2. Cells 45%: 5×0.45=2.25 L.
  3. Check: 2.75+2.25=5.
Answer: Plasma 2.75 L and cells 2.25 L.
A patient of group A needs a transfusion. Which groups can supply red cells?
Show the solution
  1. Group A plasma holds anti-B, so it rejects cells carrying antigen B.
  2. Exclude B and AB, which carry antigen B.
  3. A is accepted because it matches, and O is accepted because its cells carry no antigens.
Answer: From groups A and O.
Of every 100 carbon dioxide molecules in blood, how many travel as bicarbonate, bound to hemoglobin, and dissolved?
Show the solution
  1. Bicarbonate: about 70.
  2. Bound to hemoglobin: about 23.
  3. Dissolved: about 7.
  4. Check: 70+23+7=100.
Answer: About 70 as bicarbonate, 23 bound to hemoglobin, 7 dissolved.
Common mistakes
  • Saying red blood cells have a nucleus. Mature red cells have none, which leaves room for hemoglobin.
  • Mixing up white cells (defence) and platelets (clotting).
  • Thinking group O has antigens. Its cells have none, but its plasma has both antibodies.
  • Believing the percentage of oxygen falls at altitude. The percentage stays the same; its pressure falls.
Summary
  • Blood: plasma 55%, cells 45%; red cells carry oxygen, white cells defend, platelets clot.
  • Blood group depends on antigens on red cells; O is the universal donor and AB the universal recipient.
  • Iron is needed for hemoglobin, and lack of it causes anemia.
  • Gases diffuse from higher to lower pressure, and most carbon dioxide travels as bicarbonate.
  • More carbon dioxide lowers pH and speeds breathing; carbon monoxide takes oxygen's place.

Digestion from Mouth to Stomach

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell mechanical from chemical digestion
  • Say what saliva and the stomach digest and the products
  • Explain peristalsis, stomach acid and stomach mucus
  • Know why starch digestion stops in the stomach

Two kinds of digestion

Mechanical digestion breaks food into smaller pieces without changing its chemistry, for example chewing and the churning of the stomach. It increases the surface area for enzymes to act on.

Chemical digestion breaks large food molecules into small ones using enzymes and water. Starch, proteins and fats are large molecules that cannot be absorbed until they are broken down.

The mouth: the start of starch digestion

Saliva contains salivary amylase, which turns part of the starch into the sugar maltose (a double sugar). That is why bread chewed for a long time tastes sweet.

Chewing and the tongue form a ball of food (a bolus), which is swallowed into the pharynx and then the esophagus.

The esophagus and peristalsis

The esophagus is a muscular tube between the pharynx and the stomach, and it digests nothing. Its walls push the bolus along with successive waves of contraction and relaxation called peristalsis, so food reaches the stomach even if a person drinks upside down.

Peristalsis occurs along the whole digestive tract, pushing and mixing the food.

The stomach: acid, enzyme and mucus

The stomach squeezes the food with strong contractions (mechanical digestion) and mixes it with gastric juice to make a thick liquid.

Stomach cells secrete hydrochloric acid (HCl), which makes the contents very acidic (pH about 2). The acid kills most germs swallowed with food, activates pepsinogen into pepsin, and provides the acidity in which pepsin works.

Pepsin is an enzyme that digests protein into shorter pieces (peptides). Digestion is not finished here: the final products of protein digestion are amino acids, made in the intestine.

Other cells secrete thick mucus that coats the stomach wall and protects it from the acid and pepsin. If the mucus is thin or damaged, an ulcer can form.

Salivary amylase stops working in the stomach because it works in a nearly neutral medium, and the stomach acid changes the enzyme's shape so it stops working. So no starch is digested in the stomach.

Worked examples

Choose the enzyme: (a) acts in the mouth on starch, (b) acts in the stomach on protein. Give the product of each.
Show the solution
  1. (a) Starch in the mouth is digested by salivary amylase; the product is maltose.
  2. (b) Protein in the stomach is digested by pepsin; the products are shorter peptides.
Answer: Salivary amylase → maltose; pepsin → peptides.
A protein chain of 100 amino acids joined by peptide bonds. How many bonds must break to give free amino acids, and how many water molecules are used?
Show the solution
  1. A chain of 100 units has 100−1=99 bonds.
  2. Each bond is broken by adding one water molecule (hydrolysis).
  3. Water molecules = number of bonds = 99.
Answer: 99 bonds, using 99 water molecules.
A person swallows bread containing starch. Why does salivary amylase not keep digesting it in the stomach? What digests it afterwards?
Show the solution
  1. The stomach is very acidic because of HCl (pH about 2).
  2. Salivary amylase works in a nearly neutral medium, so acid disables it.
  3. Digestion continues in the small intestine, where the medium becomes neutral and pancreatic enzymes arrive.
Answer: Stomach acid disables the amylase; starch digestion finishes in the small intestine.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking stomach acid digests protein. The acid kills germs and activates pepsin; the enzyme does the digesting.
  • Saying the esophagus digests food. It only pushes food by peristalsis.
  • Mixing mechanical digestion (breaking up) with chemical digestion (enzymes).
  • Thinking mucus blocks digestion. It protects the stomach wall from being digested by its own juice.
Summary
  • Mechanical digestion breaks up food; chemical digestion splits molecules with enzymes.
  • Salivary amylase starts starch digestion to maltose in the mouth and is disabled in the stomach.
  • The esophagus pushes food by peristalsis and does not digest.
  • Stomach acid kills germs and activates pepsin, which turns protein into peptides.
  • Stomach mucus protects its wall; the final products of protein digestion are amino acids.

The Small Intestine, Liver and Absorption

What you will learn
  • Explain the roles of liver, gallbladder and pancreas in digestion
  • State the products of fat, protein and starch digestion
  • Explain villus design and the route of each nutrient after absorption
  • Know the role of the large intestine and vitamin D

Bile: the liver and gallbladder

The liver makes bile, which is stored and concentrated in the gallbladder and released into the duodenum when fatty food arrives.

Bile contains no enzymes and does not chemically digest fat. Its job is to emulsify fat: it breaks fat masses into very tiny droplets, increasing the surface area on which the enzyme lipase can act. It is alkaline, so it also neutralises some acid from the stomach.

If the gallbladder is removed, the liver still makes bile, but it is not stored, so large fatty meals are digested less efficiently.

Pancreatic juice

The pancreas releases a juice into the duodenum. It contains bicarbonate, which neutralises stomach acid so the medium becomes slightly alkaline, suitable for enzymes, and three main enzymes: pancreatic amylase finishes starch digestion, trypsin digests protein and peptides, and lipase digests fats.

Trypsin is a protein-digesting enzyme working in the intestine (slightly alkaline), unlike pepsin which works in the stomach (acidic).

Completing digestion and the products

The small intestine is where the digestion of carbohydrates, proteins and fats is completed, because bile, pancreatic juice and its own intestinal juice all pour into it. Final enzymes in its wall complete the digestion of double sugars and peptides into single units.

Each food has its own final product, and only what is small enough is absorbed:

  • Starch → glucose
  • Protein → amino acids
  • Fats → fatty acids + glycerol

Villi and absorption

The wall of the small intestine is covered with small projections called villi. They greatly increase surface area; each villus wall is one thin cell thick, and inside is a network of blood capillaries and a thin lymph vessel called a lacteal.

Glucose and amino acids are absorbed into the capillaries of the villus, and the blood then flows through the hepatic portal vein to the liver. The liver stores excess glucose as glycogen (muscles do too), and breaks it down when blood sugar falls.

Fatty acids and glycerol enter the cells, recombine into fats, then move into the lacteal (part of the lymph system) and reach the blood later. So fats do not go straight into the capillaries.

The dense capillaries in a villus keep the nutrient concentration in the blood low, so diffusion continues.

The large intestine and vitamin D

The large intestine hardly digests anything. It absorbs water and salts from what is left, forming feces. Helpful bacteria living there make some vitamins such as vitamin K.

Vitamin D dissolves in fat and is absorbed with it. It helps absorb calcium and phosphorus to build bone. A lack in children causes rickets: soft bones that bend under body weight. Sources are sunlight on skin, fish and fortified milk.

Worked examples

A fat mass shaped as a cube of side 2 cm is split by bile into 8 equal cubes of side 1 cm. How does the total surface area compare with the original?
Show the solution
  1. Original cube: 6×(2×2)=24 cm².
  2. Each small cube: 6×(1×1)=6 cm².
  3. Total for eight: 8×6=48 cm².
  4. Ratio: 48÷24=2.
Answer: The surface area doubled, so lipase acts on a larger surface.
Follow the route of glucose and of a fatty acid after absorption in the villus.
Show the solution
  1. Glucose enters the blood capillary, then the hepatic portal vein, then the liver where some is stored as glycogen.
  2. The fatty acid enters the villus cells and joins glycerol to make fat.
  3. The fat moves into the lacteal, then the lymph system, then the blood.
Answer: Glucose: capillary → portal vein → liver. Fat: lacteal → lymph → blood.
A patient's gallbladder is removed. Does fat digestion stop completely? Explain.
Show the solution
  1. The liver makes the bile; the gallbladder only stores it.
  2. After removal, bile reaches the intestine continuously rather than at mealtimes.
  3. The pancreas still releases lipase.
  4. But emulsification is weaker for large fatty meals.
Answer: It does not stop, but it is less efficient with fatty meals.
Common mistakes
  • Saying bile is an enzyme that digests fat. It has no enzymes; it emulsifies fat.
  • Thinking bile is made in the gallbladder. It is made in the liver and stored in the gallbladder.
  • Thinking every nutrient enters blood capillaries. Fats enter the lacteal.
  • Believing the large intestine absorbs most food. It absorbs water and salts; the small intestine absorbs nutrients.
Summary
  • Bile is made in the liver, stored in the gallbladder, and emulsifies fat without digesting it.
  • The pancreas releases bicarbonate, amylase, trypsin and lipase.
  • Products: glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids with glycerol.
  • Villi increase area: glucose and amino acids go to the blood, fats to the lacteal.
  • The large intestine absorbs water; vitamin D helps absorb calcium and its lack causes rickets.

Excretion: the Kidney and Filtration

★ Key
What you will learn
  • List the excretory organs and what each removes
  • Describe the parts of the nephron and the filtrate path
  • Explain glomerular filtration and the daily filtrate volume
  • Explain protein in urine and the idea of dialysis

Organs of excretion

Excretion is the removal of metabolic wastes produced by the body's own cells; it is not the expelling of undigested food.

The kidneys remove urea, excess salts and excess water. The lungs remove carbon dioxide and water vapour, so the lung is also an excretory organ. The skin releases sweat (water, salts and a little urea), mainly to cool the body. The liver makes urea and breaks down harmful substances.

Urea is made by liver cells from toxic ammonia produced when extra amino acids are broken down; the blood then carries it to the kidneys.

The kidney and the nephron

Blood reaches each kidney through the renal artery, a branch of the aorta that carries oxygen-rich blood loaded with urea, and leaves through the renal vein cleaner and with less urea. Each kidney holds about a million nephrons that clean the blood. The kidney also controls the amount of water and salts and the acidity of the blood.

A nephron has: a glomerulus (a ball of capillaries) inside Bowman's capsule, then the proximal tubule, the loop of Henle, the distal tubule, and the collecting duct.

Filtrate path: Bowman's capsule → proximal tubule → loop of Henle → distal tubule → collecting duct → renal pelvis. The urine then follows its path out of the kidney.

The urine pathway

Urine formed in the kidney flows down the ureter to the bladder, where it is stored, and leaves the body through the urethra.

  • Kidney → ureter → bladder → urethra

Glomerular filtration

Blood in the glomerulus is at high pressure because the vessel bringing blood in is wider than the vessel taking it out. This pressure squeezes fluid through the capillary wall into Bowman's capsule and forms the filtrate.

Water, glucose, amino acids, salts and urea pass through because they are small molecules. Blood cells and large proteins stay behind because they are too big.

So protein or blood cells in urine signal damage to the glomerulus, because the membrane no longer holds them back.

The kidneys make about 120 mL of filtrate a minute, which is about 180 L a day, yet only about 1.5 to 2 L of urine is produced, so about 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed.

Dialysis

When the kidneys fail, urea builds up in the blood. In dialysis the patient's blood flows beside a partially permeable membrane surrounded by a fluid containing no urea, so urea diffuses out of the blood into the fluid. The fluid has glucose and salts at normal levels so the blood does not lose them. The machine thus does the filtering job.

Laws and rules

Percent reabsorbedPercent=FiltrateFiltrate−Urine​×100Filtrate and urine in litres per day; the result is a percentage.

Worked examples

A person makes 180 L of filtrate a day and passes 1.8 L of urine. How many litres were reabsorbed?
Show the solution
  1. Reabsorbed = filtrate − urine.
  2. 180−1.8=178.2 L.
  3. Check: 178.2+1.8=180.
Answer: 178.2 L.
What percentage of the filtrate was reabsorbed in the previous example?
Show the solution
  1. Percent = reabsorbed ÷ filtrate × 100.
  2. 178.2÷180=0.99.
  3. 0.99×100=99%.
Answer: 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed.
A doctor finds protein in a patient's urine while glucose is normal. Which part of the nephron is damaged, and why?
Show the solution
  1. Proteins are large and normally do not pass from the glomerulus into the capsule.
  2. Their appearance in urine means the glomerulus (the filter membrane) now lets them through.
  3. Glucose normally passes and is reabsorbed, so normal glucose does not point to the problem.
Answer: The glomerulus (filtration) is damaged.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking excretion means passing feces. Excretion removes wastes made by cells; feces are undigested food.
  • Forgetting that the lung is an excretory organ for carbon dioxide.
  • Saying filtration is selective. Filtration works by size; selection happens in reabsorption.
  • Thinking filtrate equals urine. Filtrate is 180 L; urine is about 1.5 L.
Summary
  • Excretion removes metabolic wastes: urea (kidney), carbon dioxide (lung), sweat (skin).
  • Urea is made in the liver and removed by the kidney.
  • Nephron: glomerulus and capsule, proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule, collecting duct.
  • Filtration works by pressure and size, making about 180 L a day.
  • Urine path: kidney → ureter → bladder → urethra; dialysis removes urea through a partially permeable membrane.

The Kidney Tubules and Water Balance

What you will learn
  • Explain reabsorption in the proximal tubule
  • Describe the role of the loop of Henle and tubular secretion
  • Explain how ADH works
  • Explain why glucose is absent from normal urine and appears in diabetes

The proximal tubule: the biggest reabsorption

After filtration the filtrate holds useful substances we cannot afford to lose. The proximal tubule wall has cells rich in mitochondria and with a large surface, so it reabsorbs all the glucose and almost all the amino acids by active transport, most of the salts, and about 65% of the water.

That is why normal urine has no glucose: it is all returned to the blood in the proximal tubule.

The loop of Henle: building a salt gradient

The loop of Henle descends into the kidney medulla and climbs back. The ascending limb pumps salts into the surrounding tissue, so the medulla becomes very salty and the concentration of dissolved substances around the loop and collecting duct is high.

This salt gradient lets the collecting duct draw water out of the filtrate by osmosis, which is how the kidney can make urine more concentrated than blood.

Tubular secretion

Tubular secretion is the opposite of reabsorption: tubule cells move substances from the blood in the surrounding capillaries into the tubule, such as excess hydrogen and potassium ions and some drugs. The body thus removes substances the glomerulus did not filter fully, and helps control blood acidity.

Antidiuretic hormone (ADH)

It is made by the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary. It makes the collecting duct wall more permeable to water, so more water returns to the blood, and the urine becomes smaller in volume and more concentrated.

In dehydration or after drinking little, the blood becomes more concentrated and the body releases more ADH, giving little dark urine. After drinking a lot, the blood is diluted, ADH falls, the duct stays less permeable, and a lot of pale dilute urine is made.

Glucose in urine and diabetes

Proximal tubule cells have a limited capacity to reabsorb glucose. In a person with diabetes the blood glucose is very high, so the glucose in the filtrate exceeds the tubule's capacity, and the excess stays in the filtrate and leaves in the urine. The glucose also draws water with it, so the person urinates often and feels thirsty.

Worked examples

The daily filtrate is 180 L. The proximal tubule reabsorbs 65% of it. How many litres were reabsorbed and how many remain?
Show the solution
  1. Reabsorbed = 180×0.65=117 L.
  2. Remaining = 180−117=63 L.
  3. Check: 65%+35%=100% and 180×0.35=63.
Answer: 117 L reabsorbed and 63 L remain.
A person spends a hot day without drinking. What happens to the level of ADH and to the urine? Why?
Show the solution
  1. The body lost water, so the blood became more concentrated.
  2. ADH release increases.
  3. The collecting duct becomes more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed.
  4. Result: a small volume of dark concentrated urine.
Answer: ADH rises and the urine is less in volume and concentrated.
A urine test of a healthy person shows no glucose, but a patient's test shows glucose. Explain the difference.
Show the solution
  1. Glucose passes the filter, so it is in the filtrate in both cases.
  2. In the healthy person the proximal tubule reabsorbs all of it back to the blood.
  3. In the diabetic patient the blood glucose is high, so the filtrate's glucose exceeds the reabsorption capacity and the excess leaves.
Answer: In diabetes the glucose amount exceeds the capacity of reabsorption.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking glucose is not filtered at all. It is all filtered and then reabsorbed.
  • Mixing tubular secretion (blood to tubule) with reabsorption (tubule to blood).
  • Saying ADH increases urine. It decreases it and concentrates it.
  • Thinking dehydration lowers ADH. ADH rises in dehydration.
Summary
  • The proximal tubule reabsorbs all glucose and amino acids and about 65% of the water.
  • The loop of Henle builds a salt gradient in the medulla that allows concentrated urine.
  • Tubular secretion moves substances from blood into the tubule.
  • ADH makes the collecting duct more permeable, giving less, more concentrated urine.
  • Glucose in urine shows reabsorption capacity was exceeded, as in diabetes.
