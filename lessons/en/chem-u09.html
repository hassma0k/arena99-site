Oxidation numbers

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Know what an oxidation number tells you about an atom
  • Apply the assignment rules step by step
  • Find the oxidation number of an element inside a compound or ion

What is an oxidation number?

The oxidation number is the charge an atom would seem to have if all the bonding electrons went to the more electronegative atom. It is a bookkeeping tool for following electrons in a reaction.

It can be positive, negative or zero. If the number rises, the atom lost electrons; if it falls, the atom gained electrons.

Rules, in order

Start with the strongest rule and work down:

  • A free element has oxidation number 0, such as Fe, OX2​ and Na.
  • A simple ion has an oxidation number equal to its charge: NaX+ is +1 and ClX− is −1.
  • Oxygen is usually −2, except in peroxides such as HX2​OX2​, where it is −1.
  • Hydrogen is usually +1 when bonded to nonmetals, and −1 in metal hydrides.
  • Group 1 metals are always +1, group 2 metals +2, and fluorine always −1.
  • Halogens (chlorine, bromine, iodine) are −1 in binary compounds with metals, such as Cl in NaCl.

The sum equals the charge

In a neutral compound the oxidation numbers of all atoms add to zero. In a polyatomic ion they add to the charge of the ion.

Write x for the unknown, build an equation with the known numbers, and solve. Remember to multiply each number by the count of atoms.

Laws and rules

Sum rulesum of oxidation numbers=charge of the speciesThe charge is zero for a neutral compound and equals the ion charge for a polyatomic ion.

Worked examples

Find the oxidation number of carbon in COX2​ and of sulfur in HX2​SOX4​.
Show the solution
  1. In COX2​: oxygen is −2 and two atoms give −4.
  2. Let carbon be x. The compound is neutral: x+(−4)=0, so x=+4.
  3. In HX2​SOX4​: two hydrogens give +2, and four oxygens give −8.
  4. The total is zero: +2+x−8=0, so x=+6.
Answer: Carbon is +4 and sulfur is +6.
Find the oxidation number of manganese in the permanganate ion MnOX4​X−.
Show the solution
  1. Oxygen is −2; four atoms give −8.
  2. The ion charge is −1, so x+(−8)=−1.
  3. Solve: x=−1+8=+7.
  4. Check: +7−8=−1, the charge of the ion.
Answer: Manganese is +7.
Find the oxidation number of chromium in CrX2​OX7​X2−, then of oxygen in HX2​OX2​.
Show the solution
  1. Seven oxygens give −14 and the ion charge is −2.
  2. Two chromium atoms: 2x−14=−2, so 2x=12 and x=+6.
  3. In HX2​OX2​ two hydrogens give +2.
  4. The compound is neutral: +2+2y=0, so y=−1 for each oxygen (a peroxide).
Answer: Chromium is +6; oxygen in hydrogen peroxide is −1.
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting to multiply by the number of atoms, using oxygen as −2 instead of −8 in SOX4​.
  • Treating oxygen as always −2. In HX2​OX2​ it is −1, and this is a favourite trap.
  • Forgetting the ion charge, setting the sum to zero in MnOX4​X− instead of −1.
  • Thinking a pure element has a nonzero number. Pure iron and OX2​ are both zero.
Summary
  • A free element has oxidation number zero.
  • A simple ion has an oxidation number equal to its charge.
  • Oxygen is usually −2 (−1 in peroxides); hydrogen is usually +1.
  • The numbers add up to the charge of the species: set up the equation, solve, then check.

Redox and half-reactions

What you will learn
  • Tell oxidation from reduction using electrons and oxidation numbers
  • Identify the oxidizing and reducing agents
  • Write and balance a half-reaction with electrons

Oxidation and reduction together

Oxidation is loss of electrons, so the oxidation number rises. Reduction is gain of electrons, so the oxidation number falls.

Neither happens alone: every electron lost by one substance is taken by another. That is why the reaction is called redox.

Oxidizing and reducing agents

The oxidizing agent causes the oxidation of another substance, so it gains electrons and is itself reduced.

The reducing agent causes the reduction of another substance, so it loses electrons and is itself oxidized. Remember: what happens to the agent itself is the opposite of what it does to others.

Half-reactions

We split the reaction in two: an oxidation half with electrons among the products, and a reduction half with electrons among the reactants.

To balance a half-reaction, add electrons to the side that is short of negative charge until the total charge is equal on both sides.

Laws and rules

Direction reminderoxidation: number rises; reduction: number fallsThe number is the oxidation number; oxidation means losing electrons.

Worked examples

In Zn+CuX2+​ZnX2++Cu name the oxidizing and the reducing agent.
Show the solution
  1. Zinc goes from 0 to +2: the number rises, so it is oxidized.
  2. Copper ion goes from +2 to 0: the number falls, so it is reduced.
  3. The substance reduced (CuX2+) oxidized the zinc, so it is the oxidizing agent.
  4. The substance oxidized (Zn) is the reducing agent.
Answer: Oxidizing agent CuX2+; reducing agent Zn.
In FeX2​OX3​+3CO​2Fe+3COX2​ what is the reducing agent?
Show the solution
  1. Iron in FeX2​OX3​: 2x−6=0, so +3; in the product Fe it is 0. The number falls: reduced.
  2. Carbon in CO: x−2=0, so +2; in COX2​ it is +4. The number rises: oxidized.
  3. The substance that was oxidized is the reducing agent.
Answer: The reducing agent is CO; the oxidizing agent is FeX2​OX3​.
Balance the half-reactions FeX2+​FeX3+ and CuX2+​Cu and state the type of each.
Show the solution
  1. First: charge is +2 on the left and +3 on the right. Add one electron on the right: FeX2+​FeX3++eX−. Electron among products: oxidation.
  2. Second: left +2, right 0. Add two electrons on the left: CuX2++2eX−​Cu. Electrons among reactants: reduction.
  3. Check: first +2=+3−1; second +2−2=0.
Answer: The first is oxidation with one electron in the products; the second is reduction with two electrons in the reactants.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up the names: the oxidizing agent is the one that is reduced, and the reducing agent is the one that is oxidized.
  • Thinking reduction loses electrons because the word suggests less. Reduction lowers the oxidation number but gains electrons.
  • Putting electrons on the wrong side. Oxidation has electrons in the products, reduction in the reactants.
Summary
  • Oxidation is loss of electrons and a rise in oxidation number.
  • Reduction is gain of electrons and a fall in oxidation number.
  • The oxidizing agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidized.
  • Balance a half-reaction with electrons until the charge is equal on both sides.

Balancing redox equations

What you will learn
  • Understand why electrons lost must equal electrons gained
  • Balance an equation with the half-reaction method
  • Check the result for atoms and charge

The golden rule

In every redox reaction the electrons lost equal the electrons gained. Electrons never vanish or appear from nowhere.

So we multiply each half-reaction by a number that makes their electrons equal before adding them.

Steps

  • Write the oxidation half and the reduction half.
  • Balance each half with electrons until the charge matches.
  • Multiply each half so that both have the same number of electrons; the least common multiple is easiest.
  • Add the halves and cancel the electrons on both sides.
  • Check: every atom and the total charge are equal on both sides.

Acidic solution

When oxygen appears in a half-reaction, balance it by adding HX2​O to the side that needs it, then balance hydrogen by adding HX+, and finally balance the charge with electrons as usual.

Laws and rules

Balancing conditionelectrons lost=electrons gainedAfter multiplying, the electrons in the oxidation half equal those in the reduction half.

Worked examples

Balance Zn+AgX+​ZnX2++Ag.
Show the solution
  1. Oxidation: Zn​ZnX2++2eX−.
  2. Reduction: AgX++eX−​Ag.
  3. Multiply the reduction by 2 to get two electrons: 2AgX++2eX−​2Ag.
  4. Add: Zn+2AgX+​ZnX2++2Ag. Check: charge +2 on both sides.
Answer: Zn+2AgX+​ZnX2++2Ag
Balance Al+CuX2+​AlX3++Cu.
Show the solution
  1. Oxidation: Al​AlX3++3eX− (three electrons).
  2. Reduction: CuX2++2eX−​Cu (two electrons).
  3. The least common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6: multiply the first by 2 and the second by 3.
  4. Add: 2Al+3CuX2+​2AlX3++3Cu. Check: charge is +6 on both sides.
Answer: The copper ion coefficient is 3; the equation is 2Al+3CuX2+​2AlX3++3Cu.
Balance in acidic solution: MnOX4​X−+FeX2+​MnX2++FeX3+.
Show the solution
  1. Oxidation: FeX2+​FeX3++eX− (one electron).
  2. Reduction: balance oxygen by adding HX2​O: MnOX4​X−​MnX2++4HX2​O.
  3. Balance hydrogen by adding HX+: MnOX4​X−+8HX+​MnX2++4HX2​O.
  4. Balance charge: left −1+8=+7, right +2, so add 5 electrons on the left: MnOX4​X−+8HX++5eX−​MnX2++4HX2​O.
  5. Multiply the oxidation by 5, add and cancel the electrons: MnOX4​X−+8HX++5FeX2+​MnX2++4HX2​O+5FeX3+.
  6. Check: atoms match and the charge is +17 on both sides (−1+8+10 and +2+15).
Answer: MnOX4​X−+8HX++5FeX2+​MnX2++4HX2​O+5FeX3+
Common mistakes
  • Balancing atoms only and ignoring charge; Zn+AgX+​ZnX2++Ag looks balanced for atoms but the charge is +1 against +2.
  • Multiplying one half but not the other, so the electrons do not match.
  • Forgetting to cancel the electrons when adding, leaving them in the final equation.
  • In acidic solution: forgetting HX2​O and HX+, or adding them in the wrong order. The order is oxygen, then hydrogen, then charge.
Summary
  • Electrons lost equal electrons gained.
  • Balance each half-reaction, then multiply so the electrons match.
  • Add the halves and cancel the electrons.
  • Check atoms and charge together.
  • In acidic solution balance oxygen with water, then hydrogen with hydrogen ions, then charge with electrons.

Activity series and corrosion

What you will learn
  • Read the activity series and find the stronger reducing metal
  • Predict whether a displacement reaction happens
  • Explain rusting and how zinc protects iron

What is the activity series?

It ranks metals by how readily they lose electrons. A metal higher in the list is more active: it is oxidized more easily, so it is a stronger reducing agent, with a lower (more negative) reduction potential.

The usual order from the top: K, Na, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, then hydrogen H, then Cu, Ag, Au.

Predicting a reaction

A metal displaces the ion of a less active metal from solution. Iron reacts with CuSOX4​ because it is above copper; silver does not, because it is below.

A metal above hydrogen reacts with acids and releases hydrogen gas. The most active metals, such as potassium and sodium, react even with cold water.

Corrosion and protection

Rust is the oxidation of iron in the presence of oxygen and water, which turns iron into brittle oxides. It is a slow redox reaction.

We protect iron by coating it with a more active metal such as zinc. If the coating is scratched, the zinc is oxidized first because it is more active, and the iron stays intact. A coat of a less active metal such as tin protects only while it is unbroken; once scratched, the iron corrodes faster.

Worked examples

Does iron react with CuSOX4​ solution? Write the equation if so.
Show the solution
  1. Compare positions: iron is above copper in the series.
  2. The more active iron is oxidized and the copper ion is reduced.
  3. Write: Fe+CuX2+​FeX2++Cu.
  4. Charge is +2 on both sides and the atoms match.
Answer: Yes, it reacts and copper is deposited.
Does silver react with Cu(NOX3​)X2​ solution? Why?
Show the solution
  1. Silver is below copper in the series.
  2. A less active metal cannot give electrons to the ion of a more active metal.
Answer: No reaction, because silver is less active than copper.
A zinc-coated iron roof has a scratch in its coating. Why does the exposed iron not rust?
Show the solution
  1. Zinc is above iron in the series, so it is more active.
  2. The more active metal is oxidized first: Zn​ZnX2++2eX−.
  3. Electrons from the zinc flow to the iron and stop it from being oxidized.
  4. The zinc keeps protecting the iron until it is used up.
Answer: Zinc is oxidized before iron because it is more active, so it protects the iron even when scratched.
Common mistakes
  • Reversing the direction: thinking a less active metal displaces a more active one. The more active metal is oxidized and displaces the less active.
  • Explaining the protection by saying zinc is less active than iron. It is more active, so it is oxidized instead.
  • Forgetting that metals below hydrogen do not release hydrogen from dilute acids.
Summary
  • A metal higher in the series is more active and a stronger reducing agent.
  • A more active metal displaces the ion of a less active one.
  • Rusting is the oxidation of iron with oxygen and water.
  • Zinc coating protects iron because zinc is oxidized first, even when scratched.

Galvanic cells and cell potential

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Describe the parts of a galvanic cell and what each does
  • Identify anode, cathode and electron direction
  • Calculate standard cell potential and decide if a reaction is spontaneous

What does a galvanic cell do?

A galvanic cell turns chemical energy into electrical energy. It relies on a spontaneous redox reaction. We keep the two half-reactions in separate beakers and join them with a wire, so the electrons travel through the wire instead of passing directly.

It has two electrodes: the anode, where oxidation happens, and the cathode, where reduction happens. Remember: anode and oxidation both begin with a vowel; cathode and reduction both begin with a consonant. In a galvanic cell the anode is the negative electrode (electrons are released there) and the cathode is the positive one.

The salt bridge

As electrons flow, positive charge builds up in the anode beaker and negative charge in the cathode beaker, and the reaction would stop. The salt bridge moves ions between the beakers to balance the charges and keep the circuit closed: negative ions drift toward the anode and positive ions toward the cathode.

The bridge does not carry electrons; electrons flow only in the wire, while ions move in the solution.

Cell potential

Cell potential measures how strongly the cell pushes electrons, in volts. By agreement the standard hydrogen electrode has a potential of 0 V, and other potentials are measured against it. It comes from the two standard reduction potentials: the cathode is the electrode with the larger reduction potential, the anode the smaller.

A positive standard cell potential means the reaction is spontaneous. A negative one means it is not spontaneous in that direction.

Laws and rules

Standard cell potentialEcell​=Ecathode​−Eanode​All values are standard reduction potentials in volts. Do not multiply a potential by the equation coefficients.

Worked examples

The standard reduction potential of CuX2+/Cu is +0.34V and of ZnX2+/Zn is −0.76V. Find the zinc-copper cell potential and name the anode.
Show the solution
  1. The larger reduction potential is copper, +0.34, so copper is the cathode.
  2. The smaller is zinc, −0.76, so zinc is the anode.
  3. Ecell​=0.34−(−0.76)=0.34+0.76=1.10V.
  4. Overall reaction: Zn+CuX2+​ZnX2++Cu.
  5. The potential is positive, so the reaction is spontaneous.
Answer: 1.10V, and the anode is zinc.
The reduction potential of AgX+/Ag is +0.80V and of CuX2+/Cu is +0.34V. Find the silver-copper cell potential.
Show the solution
  1. Silver has the larger potential, so it is the cathode and copper is the anode.
  2. Ecell​=0.80−0.34=0.46V.
  3. Overall reaction: Cu+2AgX+​CuX2++2Ag.
  4. We do not double the potential even though the equation needs 2 silver ions.
Answer: 0.46V.
The reduction potential of FeX3+/FeX2+ is +0.77V and of IX2​/IX− is +0.54V. Does FeX3+ react with IX−?
Show the solution
  1. FeX3+ is reduced (larger potential, +0.77), so it is the cathode.
  2. IX− is oxidized to IX2​, so it is the anode, with +0.54.
  3. E=0.77−0.54=+0.23V.
  4. The potential is positive, so the reaction is spontaneous.
Answer: Yes: E=+0.23V, spontaneous.
Common mistakes
  • Swapping anode and cathode. The anode is where oxidation occurs; electrons leave it through the wire toward the cathode.
  • Thinking the salt bridge carries electrons. It carries ions to balance the charge.
  • Subtracting in the wrong order and getting a negative value. Always subtract the anode potential from the cathode potential.
  • Multiplying the potential by an equation coefficient. Potential does not change with the amount.
Summary
  • A galvanic cell turns chemical energy into electrical energy using a spontaneous reaction.
  • Oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode; electrons flow anode to cathode.
  • The salt bridge moves ions and balances the charge.
  • Ecell​=Ecathode​−Eanode​; positive means spontaneous.

Electrolysis and batteries

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell a galvanic cell from an electrolytic cell
  • Find the products at anode and cathode and the amount of metal deposited
  • Know the parts of a dry cell and a lead-acid battery

Electrolysis

Electrolysis uses electrical energy from an outside source to force a nonspontaneous reaction. The cell is called an electrolytic cell, the opposite of a galvanic cell.

Oxidation is still at the anode and reduction at the cathode, but here the anode is positive and the cathode negative, the reverse of a galvanic cell. Positive ions move to the cathode and negative ions to the anode.

Examples of products

  • Molten NaCl: at the cathode NaX++eX−​Na and sodium is deposited; at the anode 2ClX−​ClX2​+2eX− and chlorine bubbles off.
  • Acidified water: OX2​ forms at the anode and HX2​ at the cathode, with twice as much hydrogen by volume as oxygen.
  • Electroplating: the object to be plated is joined to the negative terminal so it is the cathode, and metal ions deposit on it.
  • Concentrated NaCl solution: ClX2​ forms at the anode and HX2​ at the cathode, leaving NaOH in solution, because water is reduced instead of NaX+.

Amount of metal deposited

Compare moles of electrons with the half-reaction. In CuX2++2eX−​Cu two moles of electrons give one mole of copper; in AgX++eX−​Ag one mole of electrons gives one mole of silver.

Batteries

A dry cell is a galvanic cell: the zinc can is the anode and the graphite rod is the cathode, surrounded by a paste of MnOX2​ and NHX4​Cl. It is a primary cell: used up and not recharged, unlike secondary (rechargeable) cells.

In a car lead-acid battery the anode is Pb and the cathode is PbOX2​, in HX2​SOX4​ solution. It acts as a galvanic cell when it runs and gives current, forming PbSOX4​: Pb+PbOX2​+2HX2​SOX4​​2PbSOX4​+2HX2​O. When charged it acts as an electrolytic cell, taking current from an outside source and reversing the reaction.

Laws and rules

Moles of metal depositednmetal​=electrons per metal ionnelectrons​​Electrons per ion equals the ion charge: 2 for CuX2+ and 1 for AgX+.

Worked examples

A current carrying 4 mol of electrons passes through CuSOX4​ solution. How many moles of copper deposit at the cathode?
Show the solution
  1. Half-reaction: CuX2++2eX−​Cu.
  2. Each mole of copper needs two moles of electrons.
  3. Moles of copper =4÷2=2 mol.
Answer: 2 mol of copper.
Give the products of electrolysis of molten NaCl and the moles of each when 2 mol of electrons pass.
Show the solution
  1. Cathode (reduction): NaX++eX−​Na, so each mole of electrons gives a mole of sodium; 2 mol of electrons deposit 2 mol of sodium.
  2. Anode (oxidation): 2ClX−​ClX2​+2eX−, so each mole of ClX2​ releases two moles of electrons.
  3. For 2 mol of electrons: 2÷2=1 mol of ClX2​.
Answer: 2 mol sodium at the cathode and 1 mol chlorine at the anode.
A spoon is to be silver-plated. How is it connected, and how many moles of silver deposit when 3 mol of electrons pass?
Show the solution
  1. Metal is deposited by reduction, and reduction happens at the cathode.
  2. So the spoon is joined to the negative terminal and becomes the cathode; a silver bar is the anode.
  3. Half-reaction: AgX++eX−​Ag, one electron per silver atom.
  4. Moles of silver =3÷1=3 mol.
Answer: The spoon is the cathode at the negative terminal, and 3 mol of silver deposit.
Common mistakes
  • Saying the anode is always positive. It is positive in an electrolytic cell and negative in a galvanic cell, but in both it is where oxidation happens.
  • Joining the object to be plated to the positive terminal. It must be the cathode (negative) because the metal is reduced on it.
  • Dividing moles of electrons by the wrong number; always go back to the half-reaction.
  • Believing a car battery stays galvanic while charging. During charging it works as an electrolytic cell.
Summary
  • An electrolytic cell needs an outside energy source to force a nonspontaneous reaction.
  • In an electrolytic cell the anode is positive (oxidation) and the cathode is negative (reduction).
  • Moles of metal = moles of electrons divided by the ion charge.
  • The dry cell is galvanic with a zinc anode and graphite cathode; the lead-acid battery is galvanic on discharge and electrolytic on charge.
