Types of chemical reactions

What you will learn
  • Classify a reaction by counting the substances before and after the arrow
  • Tell synthesis, decomposition, single and double replacement, neutralization and combustion apart
  • Use the activity series to decide whether a single replacement happens
  • Predict the products of common reactions

Classify by counting substances

Every equation tells a story: substances react and substances form. The quickest way to find the type is to count the substances before and after the arrow.

In synthesis two or more substances join to make one. In decomposition one substance breaks into two or more, usually with heat or electricity.

  • Synthesis: 2Mg+OX2​​2MgO
  • Decomposition: 2HX2​O​2HX2​+OX2​
  • Heating calcium carbonate (thermal decomposition): CaCOX3​​CaO+COX2​

Replacement: one element pushes out another

In a single replacement a free element takes the place of an element in a compound, and that element comes out free: Zn+CuClX2​​ZnClX2​+Cu.

It does not always happen. The activity series ranks metals from most to least reactive: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, hydrogen, copper, silver. A metal replaces only the metals below it in the series. Metals above hydrogen replace it in dilute acids and release hydrogen gas, but copper and silver do not. Halogens have a similar order: fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine; the more reactive one pushes out those below it from the solution of their salts.

Double replacement and neutralization

In a double replacement two compounds swap their ions: AgNOX3​+NaCl​AgCl+NaNOX3​. It usually happens when a solid, a gas or water forms.

Neutralization is a special case: the acid gives HX+ and the base gives OHX−, which join to make water, and the two remaining ions make a salt. So acid + base gives salt + water.

Combustion

Combustion is a reaction with oxygen that releases heat and light. Complete combustion of a carbon-hydrogen compound gives carbon dioxide and water: CHX4​+2OX2​​COX2​+2HX2​O.

Oxygen among the reactants is the sign. A reaction can fit two labels: burning magnesium is both combustion and synthesis.

Laws and rules

SynthesisA+B​ABTwo or more substances give one substance. The letters stand for elements or groups.
DecompositionAB​A+BOne substance gives two or more substances.
Single replacementA+BC​AC+BThe more reactive element (first) pushes the less reactive element out of its compound.
Double replacementAB+CD​AD+CBThe two compounds swap their positive and negative ions.
NeutralizationHCl+NaOH​NaCl+HX2​OAcid + base gives salt + water.
Complete combustionCHX4​+2OX2​​COX2​+2HX2​OCarbon-hydrogen compound + oxygen gives carbon dioxide + water.

Worked examples

Classify each reaction: (a) 2Na+ClX2​​2NaCl (b) 2HgO​2Hg+OX2​ (c) Zn+2HCl​ZnClX2​+HX2​.
Show the solution
  1. Count the substances before and after the arrow in each equation.
  2. (a) Two substances give one: synthesis.
  3. (b) One substance gives two: decomposition.
  4. (c) Zinc took the place of hydrogen in the acid and hydrogen came out free: single replacement.
Answer: (a) synthesis, (b) decomposition, (c) single replacement.
Does magnesium react with zinc sulfate solution? Does copper react with iron sulfate solution?
Show the solution
  1. Write the activity order: magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, hydrogen, copper.
  2. Magnesium is above zinc, so it pushes it out: Mg+ZnSOX4​​MgSOX4​+Zn.
  3. Copper is below iron, so it cannot push iron out of its compound.
Answer: The first happens (single replacement); the second does not.
What are the products of sulfuric acid and sodium hydroxide? Write the balanced equation and name the type.
Show the solution
  1. The acid gives HX+ and the base gives OHX−, which join to make water.
  2. The remaining ions NaX+ and SOX4​X2− make the salt NaX2​SOX4​.
  3. Balance: HX2​SOX4​+2NaOH​NaX2​SOX4​+2HX2​O.
  4. Check: sodium 2 = 2, sulfur 1 = 1, oxygen 6 = 6, hydrogen 4 = 4.
Answer: Sodium sulfate (a salt) and water; the type is neutralization.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking any element replaces any other. Correct: only a more reactive element pushes out a less reactive one, so check the activity series before writing products.
  • Mixing up synthesis and decomposition because they are opposites. Correct: find the side with one substance: after the arrow is synthesis, before it is decomposition.
  • Writing only water as the product of neutralization. Correct: there are always two products, the salt and the water.
  • Calling combustion a decomposition because the fuel "disappears". Correct: combustion is a reaction with oxygen and the fuel turns into oxides.
Summary
  • Synthesis: several substances become one. Decomposition: one becomes several.
  • A single replacement needs an element more reactive than the one pushed out.
  • Double replacement swaps ions; neutralization is acid + base giving salt + water.
  • Combustion is a reaction with oxygen; carbon and hydrogen compounds give carbon dioxide and water.

Balancing equations and conservation of mass

What you will learn
  • Count atoms using coefficients, subscripts and brackets
  • Explain the law of conservation of mass
  • Balance equations, including combustion
  • See that coefficients give moles, not masses

Counting atoms

The small lowered number after a symbol belongs to that element alone. The big number in front of a formula (the coefficient) multiplies everything in it. A number after a bracket multiplies everything inside.

Example: 3Ca(OH)X2​ has 3 calcium atoms, 3×2=6 oxygen atoms and 6 hydrogen atoms.

Conservation of mass

In a chemical reaction matter is neither destroyed nor created; atoms are only rearranged. So the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products, and each element has the same number of atoms on both sides.

Balancing turns this law into an equation. If wood seems to lose mass when burnt in an open dish, it is because the gases escaped into the air, not because matter vanished.

Steps of balancing

In hydrocarbon combustion balance carbon first (it becomes COX2​), then hydrogen (it becomes HX2​O), and oxygen last because it appears in both products. If oxygen needs a fractional coefficient, multiply the whole equation by 2: 2CX2​HX6​+7OX2​​4COX2​+6HX2​O.

  • Write the correct formulas and never change them: change only coefficients, because changing a subscript changes the substance itself.
  • Count the atoms of each element on both sides.
  • Start with the element found in the fewest substances and leave oxygen and hydrogen for last.
  • Recount to check, and make sure the coefficients are the smallest whole numbers.

What coefficients mean

A coefficient counts particles or moles, not mass. In 2HX2​+OX2​​2HX2​O, 2 mol of hydrogen react with 1 mol of oxygen to make 2 mol of water. The masses differ because each substance has a different molar mass.

Laws and rules

Atoms in a formulaatoms=coefficient×subscriptIf the element is inside a bracket, also multiply by the number after the bracket.
Conservation of massmass of reactants=mass of productsIn grams, or any one mass unit for both sides.

Worked examples

Which equation is balanced: (a) Mg+OX2​​MgO (b) 2Mg+OX2​​2MgO?
Show the solution
  1. Count oxygen in (a): two atoms on the left and one on the right, so it is not balanced.
  2. In (b): magnesium 2 = 2, oxygen 2 = 2 (from 2×1).
  3. Both sides match for every element.
Answer: (b) is balanced.
Balance: Fe+OX2​​FeX2​OX3​.
Show the solution
  1. FeX2​OX3​ has 2 iron and 3 oxygen atoms, and OX2​ has 2 oxygen atoms.
  2. Match oxygen at the lowest common multiple of 2 and 3, which is 6: take 3 molecules of OX2​ (6 atoms) and 2 of FeX2​OX3​ (6 atoms).
  3. Iron in the product is now 2×2=4, so put 4 in front of Fe.
  4. Equation: 4Fe+3OX2​​2FeX2​OX3​. Check: iron 4 = 4, oxygen 6 = 6.
Answer: 4Fe+3OX2​​2FeX2​OX3​
Balance the combustion of propane and add up the masses to check conservation of mass: CX3​HX8​+OX2​​COX2​+HX2​O. Atomic masses: C=12, H=1, O=16.
Show the solution
  1. Carbon first: 3 atoms in CX3​HX8​ give 3 COX2​.
  2. Hydrogen: 8 atoms give 4 HX2​O.
  3. Oxygen: the products hold 3×2+4×1=10 atoms, so we need 5 OX2​.
  4. Equation: CX3​HX8​+5OX2​​3COX2​+4HX2​O.
  5. Mass check: reactants 44+5×32=204 g, products 3×44+4×18=132+72=204 g.
Answer: CX3​HX8​+5OX2​​3COX2​+4HX2​O, and mass is conserved (204 g on both sides).
Common mistakes
  • Changing a subscript to balance, writing HX2​OX2​ instead of HX2​O. Correct: the subscript fixes the identity of the substance; only the coefficient changes.
  • Believing coefficients are masses. Correct: they are moles or particles, and we convert to mass with the molar mass.
  • Forgetting that the number after a bracket multiplies every atom inside, and miscounting oxygen in Ca(OH)X2​.
  • Leaving a fractional coefficient in a combustion equation. Correct: multiply the whole equation by 2 to get whole numbers.
Summary
  • Change coefficients only, never subscripts.
  • Balanced means equal atoms of every element on both sides.
  • Mass is conserved: reactants = products.
  • In combustion balance carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen last.
  • Coefficients give the ratio of moles, not of masses.

The mole, molar mass and Avogadro's number

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Understand the mole and Avogadro's number
  • Find molar mass from a formula
  • Convert between mass, moles, particles and volume
  • Compare samples by moles, not by mass

What is a mole?

Atoms and molecules are far too small to count one by one. Chemists count them in moles, the way we count eggs in dozens: one mole holds 6.02×1023 particles, and this is Avogadro's number.

The particles are atoms, molecules or ions depending on the substance. A mole of water holds that many water molecules, and a mole of iron holds that many iron atoms.

Molar mass

Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance in grams, and it equals the sum of the atomic masses in the formula. For water HX2​O: 2×1+16=18 g/mol.

When adding, multiply each element's mass by its number of atoms in the formula; a number after a bracket multiplies everything inside.

The mole bridge

The mole is always the go-between: never jump from mass straight to particles. Divide by the molar mass to reach moles, then multiply by Avogadro's number to reach particles.

For gases at standard conditions (0 °C and 1 atm), one mole of any gas fills 22.4 L. To count atoms, multiply the number of molecules by the atoms in one molecule.

Compare with moles

The heavier sample does not always have more particles. 10 g of CHX4​ (molar mass 16) is 0.625 mol, and 10 g of HX2​O (18) is about 0.56 mol. Methane has more particles at equal mass because its molar mass is smaller.

Laws and rules

Molesn=Mm​n moles (mol), m mass (g), M molar mass (g/mol).
Mass from molesm=n×MResult in grams.
Number of particlesN=n×6.02×1023N is the number of particles; 6.02×1023 per mole is Avogadro's number.
Gas volume at standard conditionsV=n×22.4LVolume in litres, standard conditions only.

Worked examples

How many moles and how many molecules are in 36 g of water HX2​O? (H=1, O=16)
Show the solution
  1. Molar mass of water: 2×1+16=18 g/mol.
  2. Moles: 1836​=2 mol.
  3. Molecules: 2×6.02×1023=12.04×1023.
Answer: 2 mol and 12.04×1023 molecules (that is 1.204×1024).
What is the mass of 0.5 mol of calcium carbonate CaCOX3​? (Ca=40, C=12, O=16)
Show the solution
  1. Molar mass: 40+12+3×16=100 g/mol.
  2. Mass = moles × molar mass = 0.5×100=50 g.
Answer: 50 g
A sample of carbon dioxide COX2​ has a mass of 22 g. Find the moles, the number of oxygen atoms, and the gas volume at standard conditions. (C=12, O=16)
Show the solution
  1. Molar mass: 12+2×16=44 g/mol.
  2. Moles: 4422​=0.5 mol.
  3. Molecules: 0.5×6.02×1023=3.01×1023.
  4. Each molecule has two oxygen atoms: 2×3.01×1023=6.02×1023 atoms.
  5. Volume: 0.5×22.4=11.2 L.
Answer: 0.5 mol, 6.02×1023 oxygen atoms, and 11.2 L.
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting to multiply atoms inside a bracket, or those after a symbol, when finding molar mass. Correct: count the atoms first, then multiply by the atomic mass.
  • Multiplying mass by Avogadro's number directly. Correct: mass to moles (divide), then moles to particles (multiply).
  • Treating the number of atoms as the number of molecules. Correct: multiply molecules by the atoms in one molecule.
  • Using 22.4 L for liquids and solids. Correct: that value is for gases at standard conditions only.
Summary
  • One mole holds 6.02×1023 particles.
  • Molar mass = sum of atomic masses in the formula (g/mol).
  • Mass to moles: divide by molar mass; the reverse: multiply.
  • One mole of gas at standard conditions = 22.4 L.
  • Compare samples by moles, not by mass.

Percent composition by mass

What you will learn
  • Find the percent by mass of each element in a compound
  • Find the mass of an element in a sample of known mass
  • Check your answer by making the total 100%

What does percent by mass mean?

It is the element's share of the compound's mass out of every 100 g. We work from one mole of the compound: the element's mass in a mole divided by the compound's molar mass, times 100.

Because a compound has a fixed formula, the percentage is the same in any sample, big or small.

Steps

  • Find the molar mass of the compound.
  • Find the element's mass in a mole: atomic mass × atoms in the formula.
  • Divide the second by the first and multiply by 100.
  • The percentages of all elements add to 100%, a ready-made check.

Mass of an element in a sample

Once you know the percentage, multiply it (as a fraction or decimal) by the sample mass to get the element's mass.

It also works backwards: divide the element's mass by its fraction to get the sample mass.

Laws and rules

Percent by mass%=molar mass of compoundmass of element in one mole​×100Masses in grams; the result is a percentage.
Mass of element in a samplemass of element=fraction×mass of sampleThe fraction is a decimal (60% means 0.6); mass in grams.

Worked examples

Find the percent by mass of each element in CaCOX3​. (Ca=40, C=12, O=16)
Show the solution
  1. Molar mass: 40+12+48=100 g/mol.
  2. Calcium: 10040​×100=40%.
  3. Carbon: 10012​×100=12%, and oxygen (3×16=48): 48%.
  4. Check: 40+12+48=100.
Answer: Calcium 40%, carbon 12%, oxygen 48%.
How many grams of iron are in 80 g of iron oxide FeX2​OX3​? (Fe=56, O=16)
Show the solution
  1. Molar mass: 2×56+3×16=112+48=160 g/mol.
  2. Iron fraction: 160112​=0.7, that is 70%.
  3. Mass of iron: 0.7×80=56 g.
Answer: 56 g
What mass of magnesium oxide MgO contains 36 g of magnesium? (Mg=24, O=16)
Show the solution
  1. Molar mass: 24+16=40 g/mol.
  2. Magnesium fraction: 4024​=0.6.
  3. Sample mass = element mass ÷ fraction = 0.636​=60 g.
  4. Check: 0.6×60=36.
Answer: 60 g
Common mistakes
  • Dividing by another element's mass instead of the molar mass of the whole compound. Correct: the denominator is always the compound.
  • Forgetting to multiply the element's mass by its number of atoms (such as 2 iron in FeX2​OX3​).
  • Adding the percentages, not reaching 100%, and ignoring it. Correct: the parts must total 100%; use it to catch errors.
  • Multiplying by 60 instead of 0.6 for the mass of the element. Correct: turn the percentage into a decimal first.
Summary
  • Percent = element mass in a mole ÷ molar mass × 100.
  • The percentages of a compound add to 100%.
  • Mass of element in a sample = fraction × sample mass.
  • The percentage is the same for any amount of the compound.

Empirical and molecular formulas

What you will learn
  • Tell the empirical formula from the molecular formula
  • Find the empirical formula from masses or percentages
  • Get the molecular formula from the molar mass

Two formulas for one compound

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms in a compound. CHX2​O means one carbon for every two hydrogens and one oxygen.

The molecular formula shows the real number of atoms in a molecule and is a whole-number multiple of the empirical one: glucose CX6​HX12​OX6​ has the empirical formula CHX2​O. The two can be the same, as in water HX2​O.

Finding the empirical formula

We convert to moles because a formula counts atoms, not grams: a carbon atom is heavier than a hydrogen atom, so masses cannot be compared directly. Dividing by the smallest number makes the scarcest element equal to 1 and reveals the simple ratio.

  • Use masses as they are. For percentages, assume a 100 g sample so the percentages become grams.
  • Convert each element's mass to moles by dividing by its atomic mass.
  • Divide every mole value by the smallest one.
  • If the results are not whole numbers, multiply them all by a small number: 2 if you see 1.5, 3 if you see 1.33 or 1.67, 4 if you see 1.25 or 1.75.

From empirical to molecular

Find the molar mass of the empirical formula, then divide the compound's real molar mass by it to get a whole number n. Then multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by n.

Laws and rules

Moles from massn=Mm​m mass in grams, M atomic mass in g/mol, n moles.
Multiplierx=molar mass of empirical formulamolar mass of molecule​x is a whole number; molecular formula = empirical formula × x.

Worked examples

A compound contains 7 g of nitrogen and 16 g of oxygen. What is its empirical formula? (N=14, O=16)
Show the solution
  1. Nitrogen: 147​=0.5 mol. Oxygen: 1616​=1 mol.
  2. Divide by the smallest, 0.5: nitrogen 1, oxygen 2.
  3. The ratio is 1:2, so the formula is NOX2​.
Answer: NOX2​
A compound is 40% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen. Find its empirical formula, then its molecular formula if its molar mass is 180 g/mol. (C=12, H=1, O=16)
Show the solution
  1. Take 100 g: 40 g carbon, 6.7 g hydrogen, 53.3 g oxygen.
  2. Moles: carbon 1240​≈3.33, hydrogen 16.7​=6.7, oxygen 1653.3​≈3.33.
  3. Divide by 3.33: carbon 1, hydrogen 2, oxygen 1. Empirical: CHX2​O.
  4. Empirical mass: 12+2+16=30. So x=30180​=6.
  5. Multiply each subscript by 6: CX6​HX12​OX6​.
Answer: Empirical CHX2​O, molecular CX6​HX12​OX6​.
A sample contains 12 g of carbon and 3 g of hydrogen, and the molar mass is 30 g/mol. What is the molecular formula? (C=12, H=1)
Show the solution
  1. Carbon: 1212​=1 mol. Hydrogen: 13​=3 mol.
  2. Ratio 1:3, so the empirical formula is CHX3​ with mass 12+3=15.
  3. The multiplier is x=1530​=2.
  4. Molecular formula: CX2​HX6​.
Answer: CX2​HX6​
Common mistakes
  • Rounding 1.5 to 2 before multiplying. Correct: 1.5 means a ratio of 3 to 2, so multiply everything by 2.
  • Dividing the masses by each other without converting to moles. Correct: a formula ratio is a ratio of atoms, i.e. moles, not of masses.
  • Assuming the empirical formula is always the molecular one. Correct: the molecular formula is a multiple, found from the molar mass.
  • Forgetting to assume a 100 g sample when given percentages. Correct: that turns percentages into grams so you can continue directly.
Summary
  • Empirical: the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms.
  • Steps: grams → moles → divide by the smallest → whole numbers.
  • Percentages: assume 100 g.
  • Molecular = empirical × x, where x = molar mass of molecule ÷ empirical mass.

Stoichiometry: moles, mass and volume

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Read the mole ratio from a balanced equation
  • Solve mole-mole, mass-mole and mass-mass problems
  • Find volumes of gases that react or form

Coefficients are ratios

In a balanced equation the coefficients give the ratio of moles that react. In NX2​+3HX2​​2NHX3​, one mole of nitrogen reacts with 3 mol of hydrogen to give 2 mol of ammonia. This is the mole ratio.

Mole-mole example: how many moles of NHX3​ come from 1.5 mol of HX2​? 1.5×32​=1 mol. Coefficients do not give masses, so convert masses to moles first.

The solution map

Whatever the question, follow the same steps: balance the equation, convert the given quantity to moles, multiply by the mole ratio, then convert the wanted moles to mass or volume as asked.

A mole-mole problem starts at the second step only. A mass-mass problem uses the whole map.

Gas volumes

At the same temperature and pressure, gas volumes are proportional to their moles, so the volume ratio equals the mole ratio. In 2HX2​+OX2​​2HX2​O (all gases), 10 L of hydrogen reacts with 5 L of oxygen and makes 10 L of steam, with no need to convert to moles.

If you are given a mass and asked for a volume, go through the mole: mass → moles → volume (multiply by 22.4 L per mole at standard conditions).

Laws and rules

Mole rationwanted​=ngiven​×coefficient of givencoefficient of wanted​Coefficients come from the balanced equation.
Molesn=Mm​m mass in grams, M molar mass in g/mol.
Gas volumeV=n×22.4LLitres, at standard conditions.

Worked examples

In NX2​+3HX2​​2NHX3​: (a) how many moles of ammonia form from 6 g of hydrogen? (H=1) (b) how many litres of ammonia form from 30 L of nitrogen, if all gases are at the same conditions?
Show the solution
  1. (a) The molar mass of HX2​ is 2 g/mol, so moles =26​=3 mol.
  2. The mole ratio NHX3​ to HX2​ is 2:3, so moles of NHX3​: 3×32​=2 mol.
  3. (b) For gases the volume ratio equals the mole ratio, and NHX3​ to NX2​ is 2:1.
  4. Volume: 30×2=60 L.
Answer: (a) 2 mol, (b) 60 L.
In 2Mg+OX2​​2MgO, 12 g of magnesium burns. What mass of magnesium oxide forms? (Mg=24, O=16)
Show the solution
  1. Moles of magnesium: 2412​=0.5 mol.
  2. The ratio MgO to Mg is 2:2, one mole for one mole: 0.5 mol MgO.
  3. Molar mass of MgO: 24+16=40.
  4. Mass: 0.5×40=20 g.
  5. Check with conservation of mass: oxygen used 0.25×32=8 g, and 12+8=20 g.
Answer: 20 g
50 g of calcium carbonate is heated and decomposes: CaCOX3​​CaO+COX2​. What volume of carbon dioxide forms at standard conditions? (Ca=40, C=12, O=16)
Show the solution
  1. Molar mass of CaCOX3​: 40+12+48=100 g/mol.
  2. Moles: 10050​=0.5 mol.
  3. The ratio is 1:1, so COX2​ is 0.5 mol.
  4. Volume: 0.5×22.4=11.2 L.
Answer: 11.2 L
Common mistakes
  • Using the equation's coefficients as if they were masses. Correct: coefficients are moles; use molar mass to convert to mass.
  • Working from an unbalanced equation, which gives the wrong mole ratio. Correct: always balance first.
  • Inverting the mole ratio. Correct: the wanted substance goes on top and the given substance on the bottom.
  • Applying 22.4 L to a gas not at standard conditions, or to a non-gas.
Summary
  • Balanced coefficients = mole ratio (and volume ratio for gases).
  • Map: mass → moles → (mole ratio) → moles → mass or volume.
  • Mole-mole: ratio only. Mass-mass: the whole map.
  • One mole of gas at standard conditions = 22.4 L.

Limiting and excess reactants, percent yield

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Identify the limiting and the excess reactant
  • Find the theoretical yield from the limiting reactant
  • Find how much of the excess reactant is left
  • Calculate percent yield

Limiting and excess

We rarely mix reactants in exactly the equation's ratio. The reactant that runs out first is the limiting reactant, and it sets how much product forms. The reactant with some left over when the reaction ends is the excess reactant.

Analogy: a sandwich needs two slices of bread and one slice of cheese. With 10 slices of bread and 3 of cheese you make only 3 sandwiches, so cheese is limiting and 4 slices of bread are left over.

How to find the limiting reactant

  • Convert each reactant's mass to moles.
  • Divide each by its coefficient in the balanced equation.
  • The reactant with the smallest result is limiting.
  • Compute the product from the limiting reactant only. For the excess: left over = available − used.

Percent yield

The theoretical yield is the most product the calculation allows, found from the limiting reactant. The actual yield is what you really collect in the lab, usually less because of lost material or side reactions.

Percent yield measures the efficiency of the reaction and cannot exceed 100% in sound work.

Laws and rules

Limiting reactant testcoefficientmoles​Compute for each reactant; the smallest result is the limiting reactant.
Excess left overleft=available−usedThe amount used comes from the mole ratio with the limiting reactant.
Percent yield% yield=theoretical yieldactual yield​×100Both yields in the same unit (g or mol).

Worked examples

130 g of zinc reacts with 32 g of sulfur: Zn+S​ZnS. Find the limiting reactant, the mass of excess left, and the mass of ZnS formed. (Zn=65, S=32)
Show the solution
  1. Zinc: 65130​=2 mol. Sulfur: 3232​=1 mol.
  2. Both coefficients are 1, so the smaller amount, sulfur, is limiting.
  3. One mole of sulfur uses one mole of zinc, leaving 2−1=1 mol of zinc, that is 65 g.
  4. Product from the limiting reactant: 1 mol of ZnS has a mass of 65+32=97 g.
Answer: Sulfur is limiting, 65 g of zinc is left, and 97 g of ZnS forms.
100 g of calcium carbonate decomposes: CaCOX3​​CaO+COX2​. If 42 g of CaO actually forms, what is the percent yield? (Ca=40, C=12, O=16)
Show the solution
  1. Moles of CaCOX3​: 100100​=1 mol.
  2. The ratio is 1:1, so the theoretical yield is 1 mol of CaO, mass 40+16=56 g.
  3. Yield: 5642​×100=75%.
Answer: 75%
48 g of magnesium reacts with 16 g of oxygen: 2Mg+OX2​​2MgO. Find the limiting reactant, the theoretical yield, and the percent yield if 32 g of MgO actually forms. (Mg=24, O=16)
Show the solution
  1. Magnesium: 2448​=2 mol. Oxygen OX2​: 3216​=0.5 mol.
  2. Divide by the coefficient: magnesium 22​=1, oxygen 10.5​=0.5. Oxygen is smaller, so it is limiting.
  3. Theoretical yield: 0.5×2=1 mol of MgO, that is 1×40=40 g.
  4. Yield: 4032​×100=80%.
  5. Excess: 1 mol of the 2 mol of magnesium was used, so 1 mol, 24 g, is left.
Answer: Oxygen is limiting, the theoretical yield is 40 g, and the percent yield is 80%.
Common mistakes
  • Assuming the reactant with the smaller mass is limiting. Correct: compare moles divided by coefficients, not masses.
  • Calculating the product from the excess reactant. Correct: the limiting reactant always sets the theoretical yield.
  • Inverting percent yield by dividing theoretical by actual. Correct: actual ÷ theoretical × 100.
  • Skipping the division by coefficients before comparing, and going wrong when the ratio is not 1:1.
Summary
  • The limiting reactant runs out first and sets the product.
  • Test: moles ÷ coefficient; the smallest is limiting.
  • Excess left = available − used.
  • Percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100, never above 100%.
