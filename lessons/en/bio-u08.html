Levels of Organization, Habitat, Adaptation and Behavior

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Order the levels of ecological organization from individual to biosphere
  • Tell habitat from niche
  • Distinguish types of adaptation and innate from learned behavior

Levels of organization

Ecology studies how living things relate to each other and to their surroundings. We start small and go up: individual, population, community, ecosystem, biome, biosphere.

The question that fixes the level is always: how many species are here, and are non-living factors mentioned?

  • Individual: one organism.
  • Population: individuals of one species in one place at one time.
  • Community: populations of several species living together.
  • Ecosystem: the community plus its non-living factors (water, soil, light, temperature).
  • Biome: a large region set by climate that holds many ecosystems.
  • Biosphere: every place where life exists on Earth, the sum of all ecosystems.

Biotic and abiotic factors

Biotic factors are living things and their products: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria. Abiotic factors are the non-living influences such as temperature, sunlight, water, soil, wind and salinity.

Habitat and niche

A habitat is where an organism lives: its "address". A niche is its whole role: what it eats, where it breeds, what eats it and how it affects others: its "job".

Two species may share a habitat, but they cannot long keep exactly the same niche (the competitive exclusion principle, in the lesson on interactions).

Adaptation

An adaptation is an inherited trait that improves an organism's chance to survive and reproduce in its environment. It builds up over many generations by natural selection, not within one lifetime.

Types: structural (body form, such as the camel's hump, which stores fat, or the thorns of desert plants), behavioral (such as migration), and physiological (an internal function, such as the camel's concentrated urine). Defensive adaptations include camouflage (body color matching the surroundings) and mimicry (a harmless species resembling a harmful one, so predators avoid it).

Animal behavior

Behavior is an animal's response to a stimulus. Innate behavior is inherited: it appears in all members of the species without learning or training, such as a spider spinning its web or a young mammal suckling. Learned behavior comes from experience and differs from one individual to another.

Kinds of learning: imprinting (a young animal bonding with the first thing it sees during a critical period, like a gosling), conditioning (linking a stimulus with another stimulus or a reward), trial and error (repeating attempts until the successful act appears), habituation (ceasing to respond to a repeated harmless stimulus), and insight (solving a new problem by reasoning).

Examples of behavior: seasonal migration (birds travelling long distances to escape cold or find food), hibernation and estivation (body activity, temperature and pulse drop in a harsh season, like a bear in winter), territorial behavior (defending an area against rivals, like a bird singing to keep others away), courtship displays that come before mating, and living in groups. Animals communicate with sounds, scents and movements, like the bee's dance that tells hive-mates where the nectar is.

Worked examples

A lake holds fish, frogs and algae together with water, rocks and light. What level of organization is this?
Show the solution
  1. Count the species: fish, frogs and algae, so more than one.
  2. Are non-living factors mentioned? Yes: water, rocks and light.
  3. A community together with its non-living factors is an ecosystem.
Answer: An ecosystem.
A desert mouse lives in burrows and comes out at night to eat seeds. State its habitat and its niche.
Show the solution
  1. The habitat is the place: the desert and its burrows.
  2. The niche is the role: it eats seeds, is active at night, and is prey for snakes and owls.
Answer: Habitat: the desert. Niche: a nocturnal seed eater that is prey for predators.
Classify each as innate or learned: (a) birds migrating in autumn, (b) a trained dog that sits when told "sit", (c) a duckling that follows the first moving object it sees after hatching.
Show the solution
  1. Migration happens without learning in all members of the species, so it is innate.
  2. The dog linked the word with a reward during training, so it learned it (conditioning).
  3. The duckling bonded with the first thing it saw during a critical period; this learning is called imprinting, so it is learned.
Answer: (a) Innate. (b) Learned (conditioning). (c) Learned (imprinting).
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up population and community: a population is one species, a community several.
  • Thinking an ecosystem is only living things; it is living things plus non-living factors.
  • Treating habitat and niche as one idea; habitat is a place, niche is a role.
  • Believing an animal adapts during its life and passes that to its young; adaptation builds up over generations.
Summary
  • Order: individual, population, community, ecosystem, biome, biosphere.
  • One species = population, several species = community.
  • Habitat is the place and niche is the role.
  • Adaptation is an inherited trait that aids survival; behavior can be innate or learned.

Energy Flow: Chains, Webs and Pyramids

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Identify producers, consumers and decomposers
  • Read food chains and food webs
  • Calculate energy passed between levels with the 10% rule

Feeding types

Energy enters an ecosystem from the sun. Producers (autotrophs) such as plants and algae make their food by photosynthesis. Consumers (heterotrophs) get energy by eating other organisms.

Consumers include herbivores (primary consumers), carnivores (secondary or higher consumers), and omnivores that eat both plants and animals, such as a chicken or a human.

Decomposers

Decomposers such as bacteria and fungi break down dead remains and waste and return the materials to the soil, where producers can use them again. Without them dead matter would pile up and the cycling of materials would stop.

Difference: a decomposer breaks the dead matter down and returns the materials, while a scavenger only eats parts of dead animals.

Food chains and food webs

A food chain is a single path of feeding. The arrow shows the direction energy moves, from the eaten to the eater. Example: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake. Each link is a trophic level.

Most animals eat more than one thing, so chains cross each other and form a food web. A web is more stable: if one species disappears, consumers can switch to other food.

Energy transfer and the 10% rule

At each step from one level to the next only about 10% of the energy reaches the next level. The rest is used in respiration and movement and lost as heat, or stays in parts that are not eaten.

This is why energy falls as we go up, and why food chains are usually short (3 to 5 links).

Ecological pyramids

A pyramid draws each feeding level as a layer. An energy pyramid is always pointed at the top, because energy is lost at every transfer, so no level can hold more than it received from the level below.

Pyramids of numbers and of biomass are usually pyramid-shaped too, with the producers at the base, but can be inverted: one tree carrying thousands of insects in a numbers pyramid, or a small mass of fast-growing, quickly eaten phytoplankton feeding animals of larger mass in a sea biomass pyramid. An energy pyramid is never inverted.

Laws and rules

10% ruleEnergy passed on=Energy available×101​Energy in kilojoules; "available" is the energy of the lower level, "passed on" is what reaches the next level.

Worked examples

In the chain grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk, name the secondary and the tertiary consumer.
Show the solution
  1. Grass is a producer, not a consumer.
  2. The grasshopper eats grass, so it is the primary consumer.
  3. The frog eats the grasshopper, so it is the secondary consumer.
  4. The snake eats the frog, so it is the tertiary consumer.
Answer: Secondary: the frog. Tertiary: the snake.
Grass stores 5000 kJ. How much reaches the rabbit and then the fox in grass → rabbit → fox?
Show the solution
  1. Grass to rabbit: 5000×101​=500 kJ.
  2. Rabbit to fox: 500×101​=50 kJ.
  3. Check: two steps, so 5000÷100=50.
Answer: Rabbit 500 kJ, fox 50 kJ.
A tertiary consumer receives 20 kJ. How much energy did the producers hold?
Show the solution
  1. From producers to the tertiary consumer there are three transfers.
  2. Work backwards: multiply by 10 each step.
  3. Secondary: 20×10=200. Primary: 200×10=2000. Producers: 2000×10=20000.
  4. Check: 20000×10001​=20.
Answer: 20000 kJ.
Common mistakes
  • Drawing the arrow from eater to eaten; the arrow follows the energy, toward the eater.
  • Thinking most of a level's energy passes to the one above; only about 10% does, and the rest is lost as heat or stays in uneaten parts.
  • Counting a producer as a primary consumer; the primary consumer is the herbivore.
  • Forgetting the decomposers' key job: returning materials to the soil.
Summary
  • Producers make food, consumers eat it, decomposers break down the dead.
  • The arrow points the way energy flows.
  • A food web is crossed chains and is more stable.
  • Only about 10% of energy passes between levels.
  • An energy pyramid is never inverted.

Cycles of Matter: Water, Carbon and Nitrogen

What you will learn
  • Explain why matter cycles while energy passes through once
  • Describe the water cycle and the carbon cycle
  • Explain the role of bacteria in the nitrogen cycle

Energy passes through, matter cycles

Energy arrives from the sun and leaves as heat; it never returns. Matter such as water, carbon and nitrogen stays roughly constant on Earth, so it moves among organisms, air, soil and water and is used again. These are called biogeochemical cycles.

The water cycle

The sun evaporates water from seas and rivers, and plants release it by transpiration. The vapor condenses into clouds, falls as rain or snow, then runs over the surface or soaks into the ground and returns to the sea. Part of the water stays locked for a long time in ice and groundwater.

  • Evaporation and transpiration
  • Condensation
  • Precipitation
  • Runoff and infiltration

The carbon cycle

Producers take carbon dioxide COX2​ from the air and turn it into sugars by photosynthesis. Consumers eat plants, and carbon passes to them.

Carbon returns to the air through the respiration of all organisms (plants included), the decay of the dead by decomposers, and the burning of fuels. Fossil fuels are carbon stored for millions of years from the remains of buried organisms, so burning them returns in a few years what took millions of years to build up. Some of the gas also dissolves in the seas, where marine organisms build shells from it. Cutting down forests raises COX2​ twice over: it removes plants that absorbed it, and burning or decaying the wood releases the carbon stored in it.

The nitrogen cycle

Air is 78% nitrogen, but plants cannot use it as a gas. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (some live in the root nodules of legumes) convert it into ammonia, and lightning turns a little of it into nitrates.

Nitrifying bacteria then change ammonia to nitrates (the main sequence is NX2​​NHX3​​NOX3​X−: fixation, then nitrification), which plants absorb to build proteins, and the nitrogen reaches animals by eating. Decomposers turn the nitrogen in dead remains and waste back into ammonia, and denitrifying bacteria return it to the air as gas.

The phosphorus cycle (for comparison)

Phosphorus is an element organisms need to build genetic molecules and energy carriers. Rain and erosion release it from rocks into soil and water, plants absorb it, it passes to animals, and decomposers return it to the soil.

The difference from carbon and nitrogen: phosphorus has no gas phase in the air, so its cycle runs through rock, soil and water, and more slowly.

Worked examples

Put in order: precipitation, evaporation, condensation, runoff.
Show the solution
  1. The sun heats the water first: evaporation.
  2. The vapor cools in the air: condensation.
  3. Clouds release the water: precipitation.
  4. The water flows back to the sea: runoff.
Answer: Evaporation → condensation → precipitation → runoff.
Explain why burning coal and cutting forests raise the share of COX2​ in the air.
Show the solution
  1. Coal is a fossil fuel from ancient remains, and its carbon has been stored for millions of years.
  2. Burning turns this carbon into COX2​ that enters the air in a short time.
  3. Trees absorb COX2​ by photosynthesis, so cutting them reduces absorption, and burning or decaying their wood releases their carbon.
  4. More goes into the air and less is taken out, so the share rises.
Answer: More is released and less absorbed, so COX2​ in the air rises.
Why does soil become richer after growing clover (a legume)?
Show the solution
  1. Legume roots have nodules that house nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
  2. These bacteria turn nitrogen gas from the air into ammonia and then nitrates the plant can use.
  3. So the usable nitrogen in the soil increases.
Answer: Because the bacteria in root nodules fix atmospheric nitrogen into the soil.
Common mistakes
  • Saying plants take nitrogen gas directly from air; bacteria must fix it first.
  • Forgetting that plants also respire and release carbon.
  • Thinking energy cycles like matter; energy is lost as heat.
  • Mixing evaporation with transpiration; transpiration is water lost from plant leaves.
Summary
  • Energy flows one way; matter cycles.
  • Water: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, runoff.
  • Carbon enters by photosynthesis and leaves by respiration, decay and burning.
  • Nitrogen needs bacteria for fixation and nitrification, and denitrifying bacteria return it to the air as gas.
  • Phosphorus has no gas phase in the air, so its cycle runs through rock, soil and water.

Interactions: Competition, Predation and Symbiosis

What you will learn
  • Tell apart competition, predation, parasitism, mutualism and commensalism
  • Understand competition and competitive exclusion
  • Know biological control and keystone species

Types of interaction

Organisms in a community affect each other by helping, harming or leaving each other unaffected. Identify the relationship with two questions: who benefits, and who is harmed? We write + for benefit, − for harm and 0 for no effect.

  • Predation (+,−): the predator benefits and the prey dies (wolf and gazelle).
  • Parasitism (+,−): the parasite benefits and the host is harmed, usually not killed (a tick sucking a camel's blood).
  • Mutualism (+,+): both benefit (bee and flower, or a lichen: fungus and alga).
  • Commensalism (+,0): one benefits and the other is unaffected (a bird nesting in a tree, or an orchid growing on a tree trunk to reach light without harming it).
  • Competition (−,−): both are harmed because the resource is limited.

Competition

Competition happens when two organisms need a limited resource such as food, water, light or shelter. It can be within one species or between two species. Competition within one species is usually fiercer, because the individuals' needs are identical, like two male deer fighting over the same female.

The competitive exclusion principle: two species cannot keep the same niche in the same habitat; one wins and the other declines, moves away, or specializes on different food.

Effects of predation

A predator limits the number of its prey, and the supply of prey limits the number of predators, so their numbers rise and fall together: more hares bring more lynxes, then fewer hares bring fewer lynxes.

Prey have adaptations that protect them, such as camouflage (body color matching the surroundings), warning colors, and mimicry (a harmless species resembling a poisonous one).

Biological control

It means using a living organism to reduce a pest instead of chemical pesticides, for example releasing ladybugs to eat aphids, or wasps that parasitize a pest's larvae. It pollutes less, but a new organism must be tested so it does not become a pest itself.

Keystone species

A keystone species has an effect on its community far larger than its numbers suggest. Removing it changes the whole community. Example: sea otters eat sea urchins; if otters vanish, urchins multiply and eat the kelp forests, and the animals living there disappear.

Worked examples

Name the relationship: (a) a tick sucking a dog's blood, (b) a bee taking nectar and carrying pollen, (c) an orchid growing on a tree to reach light without harming it.
Show the solution
  1. (a) The tick benefits and the dog is harmed but usually not killed: parasitism (+,−).
  2. (b) The bee gets food and the flower is pollinated, so both benefit: mutualism (+,+).
  3. (c) The orchid benefits and the tree is unaffected: commensalism (+,0).
Answer: (a) Parasitism. (b) Mutualism. (c) Commensalism.
Two bird species eat the same insects in the same tree, and each gets less food. What is the relationship, and what may happen?
Show the solution
  1. Two species need a limited resource and both lose: competition.
  2. By competitive exclusion one wins, or each specializes on a different part of the tree.
Answer: Competition; one species may decline, or they share resources with different niches.
Sea otters vanish from a coast, urchins multiply and the kelp forests shrink. Explain.
Show the solution
  1. Otters prey on urchins and keep their numbers low.
  2. Without otters, urchins multiply and eat the kelp.
  3. One species affecting the whole community is a keystone species.
Answer: The otter is a keystone species; its loss upset the whole community.
Common mistakes
  • Calling every relationship between two organisms "competition"; predation and mutualism are different.
  • Mixing parasitism with predation: a parasite usually does not kill its host quickly.
  • Thinking the keystone species is the most numerous; it is the most influential.
  • Mixing mutualism (both benefit) with commensalism (one benefits, the other unharmed).
Summary
  • Name the relationship by asking who benefits and who is harmed: mutualism (+,+), commensalism (+,0), parasitism (+,−), competition (−,−).
  • Competition for limited resources harms both sides.
  • Two species cannot keep the same niche in one habitat.
  • Biological control uses a living organism against a pest.
  • A keystone species matters more than its numbers.

Populations and Their Growth

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Calculate density and population change
  • Distinguish exponential from logistic growth
  • Classify limiting factors and understand human population growth

Population features

A population has a size (number of individuals), a density (individuals per unit area) and a distribution (even, random or clumped).

Population size rises with births and immigration and falls with deaths and emigration. If births exceed deaths (without migration), growth is positive.

We usually cannot count every individual, so we count them in small sample quadrats and scale up: estimated number =number in the sample×sample areafield area​.

Growth patterns

In exponential growth (a J-shaped curve) the population multiplies very fast when resources are plentiful and nothing holds it back; this cannot go on for long.

In logistic growth (an S-shaped curve) numbers rise fast, then slow down and level off near the carrying capacity, the largest number the environment can support over time (also called the environment's carrying capacity).

Limiting factors

Limiting factors keep a population from growing without limit. Density-dependent factors act more strongly as numbers rise (competition for food, disease, predation, waste build-up). Density-independent factors act the same whatever the numbers (drought, fire, flood, frost).

Human population

The human population grew slowly for thousands of years, then rose sharply after the Industrial Revolution thanks to better farming, medicine and hygiene that lowered death rates. Growth puts pressure on water, land and energy.

Age structure is shown in a population pyramid: a wide base means many young people and fast growth (a country where half the people are under 15 is set to grow quickly, because they will become parents).

Laws and rules

Population densityDensity=AreaNumber of individuals​Number of individuals as a count, area in square kilometers or square meters; the result is individuals per unit area.
Change in population sizeNew size=Size+(Births+Immigrants)−(Deaths+Emigrants)All terms are counts of individuals over the same time period.
Population growth rateGrowth rate=SizeBirths−Deaths​×100Births, deaths and size as counts of individuals over the same period, without migration; the result is a percentage.

Worked examples

200 gazelles live in a reserve of 4 square kilometers. What is the density? In a field of 200 square meters a researcher counts 3 plants in each 1 square meter sample quadrat; about how many plants are in the field?
Show the solution
  1. Density = number of individuals ÷ area.
  2. 4200​=50.
  3. The field equals 200 sample quadrats, each with 3 plants.
  4. Estimated number: 3×200=600.
Answer: Density 50 gazelles per square kilometer; about 600 plants in the field.
A population of 500 has 60 births, 10 immigrants, 40 deaths and 20 emigrants. What is its new size? What is its natural growth rate (from births and deaths only)?
Show the solution
  1. Gains: 60+10=70.
  2. Losses: 40+20=60.
  3. New size: 500+70−60=510.
  4. Natural growth rate: 50060−40​×100=50020​×100=4.
Answer: New size 510 individuals; natural growth rate 4%.
Classify as density-dependent or independent: (a) disease spreading in a crowded farm, (b) severe drought, (c) food shortage as numbers grow.
Show the solution
  1. (a) Disease spreads faster with crowding: dependent.
  2. (b) Drought hits all regardless of numbers: independent.
  3. (c) Competition for food grows with numbers: dependent.
Answer: (a) Dependent. (b) Independent. (c) Dependent.
Common mistakes
  • Dividing area by number to find density; the correct form is number ÷ area.
  • Forgetting migration when calculating the change in size, or including it in the natural growth rate, which uses births and deaths only.
  • Treating fire and drought as density-dependent; they are independent of density.
  • Thinking a population grows forever; the carrying capacity sets a limit.
Summary
  • Density = number ÷ area.
  • Births and immigrants add; deaths and emigrants subtract; the natural growth rate uses births and deaths only.
  • Exponential growth keeps rising; logistic growth levels at carrying capacity.
  • Disease and competition are density-dependent; drought and fire are not.

Biomes, Aquatic Ecosystems and Succession

What you will learn
  • Describe the main land biomes by climate and plants
  • Tell fresh water from salt water systems
  • Distinguish primary from secondary succession and know the climax

Land biomes

A biome is a large area defined by climate (temperature and rainfall), giving it characteristic plants and animals.

  • Tropical rainforest: hot, very wet, the highest diversity.
  • Savanna: grass with scattered trees and a dry season.
  • Desert: less than 25 cm of rain a year; plants and animals adapted to dryness (such as a cactus that stores water in its stem).
  • Temperate forest: four seasons and deciduous trees.
  • Temperate grassland: plenty of grass and few trees, with hot summers and cold winters.
  • Taiga (conifer forest): long cold winters and cone-bearing trees.
  • Tundra: extremely cold with permanently frozen soil below the surface (permafrost).

Aquatic ecosystems

Water covers about three quarters of the Earth. The deciding factor is salinity. Fresh water includes lakes, rivers and wetlands such as marshes. Salt water includes seas and oceans, with the intertidal zone, the coastal zone and the open ocean.

Estuaries, where a river meets the sea, have medium salinity and act as nurseries for many fish because of their rich food. The sunlit surface layer of water holds phytoplankton, which builds most of the sea's food; below it lies a dark layer that light does not reach, so no photosynthesis happens there. Coral reefs live only in warm, shallow, clear water, because the algae that live with the coral need light.

Ecological succession

Succession is an orderly change in a community over time until it reaches a mature, stable community called the climax community.

Primary succession starts on bare rock with no soil: pioneer organisms such as lichens break the rock and make soil, then come mosses and grasses, shrubs and trees. It takes hundreds of years.

Secondary succession begins after a disturbance (fire, logging, flood) when soil remains, so it is much faster. Grasses and annual plants grow first, then shrubs and trees.

Worked examples

A region gets 15 cm of rain a year and has organisms that store water. What biome is it?
Show the solution
  1. Rainfall is below 25 cm, which is very low.
  2. That is the condition for a desert.
Answer: Desert.
A field burns and new grasses sprout a year later. What kind of succession is this?
Show the solution
  1. Is the soil still there? Yes, fire does not remove soil.
  2. Succession that starts with soil present is secondary.
Answer: Secondary succession.
Order the stages of primary succession on bare rock: trees, lichens, grasses, shrubs.
Show the solution
  1. Pioneers come first: lichens.
  2. Then grasses once a thin soil forms.
  3. Then shrubs, and finally trees as the climax community.
Answer: Lichens → grasses → shrubs → trees.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking deserts have no life; they hold well-adapted plants and animals.
  • Mixing primary and secondary: primary has no soil, secondary starts with soil.
  • Calling an estuary fresh water; its salinity is medium.
  • Thinking the climax community never changes; it is only relatively stable.
Summary
  • Climate decides the biome.
  • Salinity separates fresh water from salt water.
  • Primary succession starts on bare rock; secondary follows a disturbance with soil kept.
  • Pioneer organisms like lichens start primary succession.

Biodiversity and Its Protection

What you will learn
  • Define biodiversity, its three levels and its value
  • List its threats and explain why an invasive species does harm
  • Know ways to protect species and habitats

Biodiversity

Biodiversity is the variety of life on Earth and has three levels: genetic diversity (gene differences within one species, such as the many varieties of date palm), species diversity (the number of different species in an area), and ecosystem diversity (forests, deserts and lakes in one country).

Its value: food, medicine, clean air and a stable ecosystem during disasters. The more diversity, the more stability, because some species withstand drought or disease when others weaken, so the whole system does not collapse.

Threats to biodiversity

Main threats: habitat destruction and fragmentation (the biggest), overexploitation (taking more than the population can replace), invasive species, pollution, and climate change. An invasive species has entered a new area where it has no natural enemies, so it multiplies without limit and outcompetes the native species.

Threatened species that are not protected go extinct, and extinction cannot be undone: what disappears does not come back.

Conserving biodiversity

Protection comes from nature reserves that keep habitats, laws against overhunting, captive breeding followed by release (such as returning the Arabian oryx to the wild), seed banks, and moderate use of resources.

Worked examples

Classify as genetic, species or ecosystem diversity: (a) many varieties of date palm in one country, (b) 300 fish species in a lake, (c) forests, deserts and wetlands in one country.
Show the solution
  1. (a) Variation within one species (the palm) is a difference in genes: genetic diversity.
  2. (b) The number of different species in an area: species diversity.
  3. (c) A variety of ecosystem types: ecosystem diversity.
Answer: (a) Genetic. (b) Species. (c) Ecosystem.
One forest has 40 tree species and another has 3. Which is more stable against a disease that attacks one species, and why?
Show the solution
  1. The disease hits only one species.
  2. In the diverse forest most species stay healthy.
  3. In the forest with few species, the infected one may be most of the forest.
Answer: The 40-species forest; diversity increases stability.
An invasive plant spreads through an oasis and the native plants decline. Explain why, and what happens to biodiversity.
Show the solution
  1. The invasive species has entered a new area where it has no natural enemies.
  2. So it multiplies without limit and outcompetes native plants for water and light.
  3. Native species decline or vanish from the oasis.
  4. The number of species falls, so biodiversity and ecosystem stability fall.
Answer: It has no enemies, so it multiplies and displaces natives; biodiversity falls.
Common mistakes
  • Limiting biodiversity to the number of species; it also includes genetic and ecosystem diversity.
  • Thinking that protecting species is enough without protecting their habitats; habitat destruction is the biggest threat.
  • Thinking that extinct species can return; extinction is final.
  • Treating every new species in an area as beneficial; an invasive species displaces natives.
Summary
  • Biodiversity: genetic, species, ecosystem.
  • The more diversity, the more stable the ecosystem.
  • The biggest threat is habitat destruction and fragmentation, then overexploitation and invasive species.
  • Extinction is final and cannot be undone.
  • Species are protected by reserves, laws and captive breeding.

Pollution and Global Warming

What you will learn
  • Link each pollutant to its effect: acid rain, eutrophication, ozone thinning
  • Explain biomagnification and why top predators suffer most
  • Understand global warming and why greenhouse gases are rising

Pollution

Pollution is adding harmful materials to air, water or soil. Soil is polluted by pesticides and waste, and water by oil and wastes.

Air pollution

Burning fuel releases SOX2​ and NOX2​, which dissolve in atmospheric water and fall as acid rain that harms plants, waters and buildings.

The ozone layer shields living things from ultraviolet rays, but chlorofluorocarbons, used in some coolants and sprays, destroy it, so more ultraviolet light reaches the Earth.

Eutrophication

Excess fertilizer and waste rich in nitrogen and phosphorus in water cause eutrophication: algae multiply, then die and decay, and decay uses up the dissolved oxygen so fish die.

Biomagnification

Persistent toxins (such as pesticides and mercury) do not break down or leave the body easily, so they become more concentrated at each feeding level, and top predators are harmed the most.

Global warming

Greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide COX2​ and methane trap part of Earth's heat near the surface. This is natural and useful; without it Earth would freeze.

But more of these gases from burning fuel and cutting forests warms the Earth, melts ice, raises the sea level and changes rainfall.

Worked examples

Match each pollutant to its effect: (a) SOX2​ and NOX2​ from burning fuel, (b) chlorofluorocarbons, (c) fertilizer leaking into a lake, (d) extra COX2​ and methane in the air.
Show the solution
  1. (a) Gases that dissolve in atmospheric water and form acid rain.
  2. (b) They destroy the ozone layer that blocks ultraviolet rays.
  3. (c) Nitrogen and phosphorus cause eutrophication.
  4. (d) Greenhouse gases trap heat, so global warming happens.
Answer: (a) Acid rain. (b) Ozone thinning. (c) Eutrophication. (d) Global warming.
A pesticide is at 1 unit in the water and becomes 10 times more concentrated at each level: plankton, small fish, big fish, bird. What is its concentration in the bird?
Show the solution
  1. Plankton: 1×10=10.
  2. Small fish: 10×10=100.
  3. Big fish: 100×10=1000.
  4. Bird: 1000×10=10000.
  5. Check: four steps of a factor of 10 give 104=10000.
Answer: 10000 units; this is biomagnification.
After fertilizer leaked into a lake, the fish died within weeks. Put the events in order.
Show the solution
  1. Fertilizer is rich in nitrogen and phosphorus, so algae multiplied.
  2. The algae died and bacteria decomposed them.
  3. Decomposition used up the water's oxygen.
  4. With low oxygen the fish suffocated.
Answer: Eutrophication → algal bloom → death and decay → oxygen loss → fish die.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking all greenhouse gases are harmful; without them Earth would freeze, the excess is the problem.
  • Mixing up global warming with ozone thinning; the first comes from gases that trap heat, the second from compounds that destroy the ozone that blocks ultraviolet rays.
  • Thinking eutrophication is good because algae increase; the result is oxygen loss.
  • Thinking a pesticide gets diluted up the chain; it concentrates.
Summary
  • Burning fuel causes acid rain, and chlorofluorocarbons destroy ozone.
  • Fertilizer causes eutrophication, so oxygen falls and fish die.
  • Persistent toxins magnify: more concentrated at each level, with top predators hurt most.
  • More greenhouse gases warm the Earth.
