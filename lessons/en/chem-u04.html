Kinetic Theory and Temperature

What you will learn
  • Describe the three states by particle motion
  • Link temperature to average kinetic energy
  • Convert between Celsius and Kelvin

What does kinetic theory say?

All matter is made of tiny particles in constant motion. In a gas the particles are far apart and move freely, and their collisions with the walls of the container are what we call gas pressure.

In a liquid the particles are close but slide past each other; in a solid they vibrate in place without leaving it.

  • Particles are very far apart compared with their size, so their own volume is negligible.
  • There is no attraction between them in an ideal gas.
  • Collisions are elastic: no energy is lost.
  • Motion is random, in every direction.

Kinetic energy and temperature

The kinetic energy of a particle depends on its mass and speed. Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles: the hotter the sample, the faster they move.

At the same temperature every gas has the same average kinetic energy. Lighter particles are faster, because the energy is half the mass times the speed squared.

Average kinetic energy is directly proportional to the kelvin temperature: double the kelvin and the average doubles.

The Kelvin scale

Zero kelvin is absolute zero: the coldest possible temperature, where particle motion nearly stops. There are no negative kelvin temperatures, so gas laws always use kelvin.

A step of one degree is the same size on both scales, but the starting points differ by 273.

Laws and rules

Kinetic energyKE=21​mv2m is the mass in kg and v the speed in m/s; the energy is in joules.
Converting to kelvinT=t+273t is the Celsius temperature in ∘C; T is the kelvin temperature in K.

Worked examples

Convert 25∘C to kelvin, then 400K to Celsius.
Show the solution
  1. Celsius to kelvin: add 273. 25+273=298K.
  2. Kelvin to Celsius: subtract 273. 400−273=127∘C.
Answer: 298K and 127∘C.
HX2​ (molar mass 2g/mol) and OX2​ (molar mass 32g/mol) are at the same temperature. Compare their average kinetic energies and speeds.
Show the solution
  1. Same temperature, so the average kinetic energy is equal for both.
  2. 21​mv2 is equal, so the smaller the mass, the larger the speed.
  3. The mass ratio is 232​=16, so the ratio of squared speeds is 16 and the ratio of speeds is 4.
Answer: Equal energies; HX2​ moves 4 times faster than OX2​.
A gas is heated from 27∘C to 327∘C. By what factor does its average kinetic energy change?
Show the solution
  1. Convert to kelvin: 27+273=300 and 327+273=600.
  2. Average energy is proportional to kelvin temperature, and 300600​=2.
  3. The ratio of the two Celsius values means nothing here.
Answer: The average kinetic energy doubles.
Common mistakes
  • Using Celsius in ratios: zero Celsius is not zero energy, so ratios need kelvin.
  • Thinking a heavy gas has more kinetic energy at the same temperature: the energies are equal and the lighter gas is faster.
  • Forgetting that temperature gives only the average: a gas always has particles faster and slower than average.
Summary
  • A gas is fast, widely spaced particles whose wall collisions make pressure.
  • Temperature measures average kinetic energy.
  • At one temperature, energies are equal and lighter particles are faster.
  • T=t+273 and there is no negative kelvin.

Forces Between Molecules

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Separate bonds inside molecules from forces between molecules
  • Decide when London forces and hydrogen bonds act
  • Rank substances by boiling point from attraction strength

Inside a molecule and between molecules

A covalent bond is strong and joins atoms inside one molecule. Forces between molecules are much weaker; they hold molecules close together in liquids and solids.

When water boils, molecules separate from their neighbours; the H to O bonds inside each molecule stay intact.

London dispersion forces

In every molecule, polar or nonpolar, the electrons keep moving. For an instant they bunch up on one side, giving a temporary partial charge, which pushes the neighbour into a matching shift, so the two attract.

London forces are the only force between nonpolar particles, such as noble gases. They grow with the number of electrons and the size of the molecule, which is why noble gas boiling points rise from helium to xenon.

Dipole-dipole forces

A polar molecule such as HCl has a partially positive end and a partially negative end. The positive end of one molecule is attracted to the negative end of a neighbour.

These forces are stronger than London forces when the molecules are similar in size, so polar substances usually boil higher than nonpolar ones of similar size.

Hydrogen bonding

It occurs when H is bonded to N, O or F. That H carries a large partial positive charge and is attracted to a lone pair on an N, O or F of a nearby molecule.

It is the strongest force between molecules, though still weaker than a covalent bond. It is why water boils at 100∘C despite its small molecules, while CHX4​ has none because its H is bonded to carbon.

That is why HX2​O boils at 100∘C, while the heavier HX2​S boils at about 60∘C below zero.

Boiling and forces

To boil, we pull molecules apart, which takes energy to overcome the forces between them. Stronger forces need more heat, so the boiling point is higher.

For molecules of similar size: London forces only, then dipole-dipole forces, then hydrogen bonding, in order of increasing strength.

Worked examples

Which substances form hydrogen bonds between their molecules: CHX4​, NHX3​ or HCl?
Show the solution
  1. Condition: H bonded directly to N, O or F.
  2. CHX4​: H is bonded to C, so no. HCl: H is bonded to Cl, not one of the three, so no.
  3. NHX3​: H is bonded to N, so yes.
Answer: Only NHX3​.
Arrange He, Ar and Xe from lowest to highest boiling point.
Show the solution
  1. All three are nonpolar noble gases, so only London forces act.
  2. London forces increase with the number of electrons and atom size.
  3. Electron counts: He has 2, Ar has 18, Xe has 54.
Answer: He, then Ar, then Xe.
Arrange CHX4​, HCl and HX2​O from lowest to highest boiling point, given that their molecules are similar in size.
Show the solution
  1. CHX4​ is nonpolar: London forces only.
  2. HCl is polar but forms no hydrogen bonds because H is bonded to Cl: London and dipole-dipole forces.
  3. HX2​O has H bonded to O: hydrogen bonding, the strongest of the three.
  4. Stronger forces mean a higher boiling point.
Answer: CHX4​, then HCl, then HX2​O.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking boiling breaks covalent bonds: only the forces between molecules break, and steam is still water.
  • Thinking any molecule containing H forms hydrogen bonds: H must be bonded to N, O or F.
  • Thinking London forces belong only to nonpolar molecules: they exist in all matter, but they are the only force in nonpolar ones.
  • Comparing only the type of force and ignoring size: London forces in a very large molecule can exceed dipole-dipole forces in a small one, so we compare types for molecules of similar size.
Summary
  • Covalent bonds are inside molecules; forces between molecules are weaker.
  • London forces exist in all matter and grow with molecular size.
  • Dipole-dipole forces act between polar molecules and beat London forces at similar size.
  • Hydrogen bonding needs H bonded to N, O or F.
  • Stronger forces mean a higher boiling point.

Changes of State, Energy and Vapor Pressure

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Name the changes of state and say whether each absorbs or releases energy
  • Explain why temperature stays constant during melting and boiling
  • Link vapor pressure to attraction strength and boiling point

Six changes of state

Breaking attraction between particles takes energy, and forming it releases energy. So changes of state either absorb heat or release it.

  • Melting: solid to liquid, absorbs energy.
  • Freezing: liquid to solid, releases energy.
  • Evaporation or boiling: liquid to gas, absorbs energy.
  • Condensation: gas to liquid, releases energy.
  • Sublimation: solid straight to gas, absorbs energy, like dry ice.
  • Deposition: gas straight to solid, releases energy.

Evaporation and boiling

Evaporation happens at the surface at any temperature: the fastest particles escape, so the average energy of those left falls and the liquid cools. This is why sweat cools you.

Boiling happens throughout the liquid when its vapor pressure equals the outside pressure.

Because boiling starts when vapor pressure equals the outside pressure, lowering the outside pressure, as on a mountain top, lowers the boiling point. The normal boiling point is measured at 1atm.

Vapor pressure

In a closed container the liquid evaporates and its vapor condenses until the two rates match and the pressure levels off. That pressure is the vapor pressure at that temperature.

Vapor pressure rises with temperature. Stronger forces between molecules let fewer escape, so vapor pressure is lower and the boiling point is higher: the two always move in opposite directions.

The heating curve

When a sample is heated, the temperature rises as kinetic energy rises, then stays constant during melting and boiling even though heating continues.

The added energy is breaking attractions, which raises potential energy and not kinetic energy, so the temperature does not rise.

Laws and rules

Energy of a change of stateq=n×ΔHn is the number of moles; ΔH is the molar heat of fusion or vaporization in kJ/mol; q is in kJ.

Worked examples

Name each change and say whether it absorbs or releases energy: (a) dry ice turns to gas. (b) Water vapor forms droplets on a cold glass.
Show the solution
  1. (a) A solid turns straight into a gas: sublimation, which absorbs energy because the attractions are broken.
  2. (b) A gas turns into a liquid: condensation, which releases energy because attractions form.
Answer: (a) Sublimation, absorbs. (b) Condensation, releases.
A substance has molar heat of fusion 6kJ/mol and molar heat of vaporization 44kJ/mol. How much energy melts 3mol? How much is released when 2mol of its vapor condenses?
Show the solution
  1. Melting: 3×6=18kJ absorbed.
  2. Condensation is the reverse of vaporization, so it releases the same energy: 2×44=88kJ released.
Answer: 18kJ absorbed and 88kJ released.
Liquid A boils at 35∘C and liquid B at 78∘C. Which has the higher vapor pressure at 25∘C, and why?
Show the solution
  1. The lower boiling point means weaker forces between molecules in A.
  2. Weaker forces let more molecules escape into the gas.
  3. More molecules in the gas means a higher vapor pressure.
Answer: A has the higher vapor pressure, because its forces between molecules are weaker.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the temperature stays constant because heating stopped: heating continues, but the energy breaks attractions instead of speeding particles.
  • Mixing up condensation and evaporation: condensation releases energy, evaporation absorbs it.
  • Thinking higher vapor pressure means a higher boiling point: the relation is opposite.
Summary
  • Melting, evaporation and sublimation absorb energy; freezing, condensation and deposition release it.
  • During a change of state the temperature is constant because energy changes potential energy.
  • A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the outside pressure.
  • Stronger forces mean lower vapor pressure and a higher boiling point.

Boyle's and Charles's Laws

What you will learn
  • Explain how pressure and volume are related at constant temperature
  • Explain how volume and temperature are related at constant pressure
  • Solve problems after converting temperature to kelvin

Variables of a gas

A fixed amount of gas is described by three variables: pressure P, volume V and temperature in kelvin T. We change one, hold another fixed, and see how the third responds.

Boyle's law: pressure and volume

At constant temperature and amount of gas, shrinking the volume makes particles hit the walls more often each second, so the pressure rises. The relation is inverse: the product of pressure and volume is constant.

Double the volume and the pressure halves; cut the volume to a third and the pressure triples.

Charles's law: volume and temperature

At constant pressure and amount of gas, heating makes particles faster and their impacts harder, so the gas expands to keep the pressure the same. The relation is direct: volume divided by kelvin temperature is constant.

You must use kelvin, because volume doubles when the kelvin temperature doubles, not the Celsius one.

Units of pressure and volume

Pressure has several units, related by: 1atm equals 760mmHg, which equals 101.3kPa.

In Boyle's and Charles's laws you may use any pressure unit and any volume unit as long as the same unit appears on both sides. Temperature, however, is always in kelvin.

Laws and rules

Boyle's lawP1​V1​=P2​V2​P is pressure (atm), V is volume (L). Temperature and amount of gas are constant.
Charles's lawT1​V1​​=T2​V2​​V is volume (L), T is temperature in kelvin. Pressure and amount of gas are constant.

Worked examples

A gas of volume 6L at 1atm is compressed to 2L at constant temperature. What is its new pressure?
Show the solution
  1. Boyle: P1​V1​=P2​V2​, so 1×6=P2​×2.
  2. P2​=26​=3.
  3. Check: the volume fell to a third, so the pressure tripled.
Answer: 3atm.
A gas of volume 4L is at 2atm. At constant temperature, what is the volume when the pressure drops to 0.5atm?
Show the solution
  1. P1​V1​=P2​V2​: 2×4=0.5×V2​.
  2. V2​=0.58​=16L.
  3. Check: the pressure became a quarter, so the volume became four times larger.
Answer: 16L.
A gas of volume 3L at 27∘C is heated to 127∘C at constant pressure. What is its new volume?
Show the solution
  1. Convert to kelvin: T1​=27+273=300 and T2​=127+273=400.
  2. Charles: 3003​=400V2​​.
  3. V2​=3003×400​=4L.
  4. Check: the temperature rose, so the volume increases.
Answer: 4L.
Common mistakes
  • Using Celsius in Charles's law: going from 27 to 54∘C does not double the volume, because kelvin only goes from 300 to 327.
  • Mixing the two relations: Boyle's is inverse so we multiply; Charles's is direct so we divide.
  • Forgetting the constants: Boyle needs constant temperature, Charles needs constant pressure.
  • Mixing units of pressure or volume between the two states: convert the second state to the unit of the first before substituting.
Summary
  • Boyle: pressure and volume are inverse, and pressure times volume is constant.
  • Charles: volume and kelvin are direct, and volume divided by kelvin is constant.
  • Gas laws always use kelvin.
  • Check that the direction of your answer makes sense.

Gay-Lussac's Law and the Combined Gas Law

What you will learn
  • Explain how pressure and temperature are related at constant volume
  • Merge the three laws into one
  • Solve problems where more than one variable changes

Gay-Lussac's law: pressure and temperature

In a sealed rigid container the volume is fixed. Heating makes particles faster, so they hit the walls harder and more often, and the pressure rises. The relation is direct: pressure divided by kelvin temperature is constant.

That is why sealed spray cans must not be heated: the pressure climbs until they burst.

The combined gas law

When pressure, volume and temperature all change, the three laws merge into one relation: pressure times volume divided by kelvin temperature is constant.

Each earlier law is a special case: hold temperature fixed and it is Boyle's, hold pressure fixed and it is Charles's, hold volume fixed and it is Gay-Lussac's.

Steps to solve

  • Convert every temperature to kelvin.
  • Write the law and keep each state complete on one side of the equation.
  • Isolate the unknown, then check the direction makes sense.

Laws and rules

Gay-Lussac's lawT1​P1​​=T2​P2​​P is pressure (atm), T is temperature in kelvin. Volume and amount of gas are constant.
Combined gas lawT1​P1​V1​​=T2​P2​V2​​V is volume (L). The amount of gas is constant and temperature is in kelvin.

Worked examples

A gas in a rigid container is at 4atm and 400K. It is cooled to 200K. What is its pressure?
Show the solution
  1. Volume is constant, so use Gay-Lussac: 4004​=200P2​​.
  2. P2​=4004×200​=2.
  3. Check: kelvin halved, so the pressure halved.
Answer: 2atm.
A sealed container holds gas at 2atm and 27∘C. It is placed in a fire and reaches 327∘C. What is the pressure if the volume is fixed?
Show the solution
  1. Convert: 27+273=300 and 327+273=600K.
  2. 3002​=600P2​​, so P2​=3002×600​=4.
  3. Check: kelvin doubled so pressure doubled. Using Celsius would give a wrong answer.
Answer: 4atm.
A gas of volume 6L is at 2atm and 300K. It changes to 4atm and 200K. What is its new volume?
Show the solution
  1. Use the combined law: 3002×6​=2004×V2​​.
  2. The first side: 30012​=0.04.
  3. 0.04=2004V2​​, so 4V2​=0.04×200=8.
  4. V2​=2L. Check: pressure doubled and kelvin fell, so the volume shrinks.
Answer: 2L.
Common mistakes
  • Using Celsius instead of kelvin: the result is always wrong.
  • Misplacing numbers in the combined law: keep each state complete on one side.
  • Thinking pressure falls when a sealed container is heated: the relation is direct.
Summary
  • Gay-Lussac: pressure is directly proportional to kelvin at constant volume.
  • Combined: pressure times volume divided by kelvin is constant for a fixed amount of gas.
  • The three laws are special cases of the combined law.
  • Convert to kelvin first and check the direction last.

Avogadro, Molar Volume and the Ideal Gas Law

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Link the volume of a gas to its number of molecules
  • Use the molar volume 22.4L at standard conditions
  • Solve ideal gas law problems

Avogadro's law

Equal volumes of different gases at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number of molecules. So volume is directly proportional to the number of moles.

The reason is that gas particles are so far apart that their own size does not matter; only their number does.

One mole of any gas contains 6.02×1023 molecules, which is Avogadro's number.

Molar volume

Standard conditions are 0∘C (273K) and 1atm. There, one mole of any ideal gas occupies 22.4L.

So we convert between moles and volume by multiplying or dividing by 22.4, but only at standard conditions.

The ideal gas law

It joins all the variables in one equation: pressure times volume equals moles times the gas constant times kelvin temperature.

The gas constant is about 0.082L⋅atm/(mol⋅K) when you use atm, litres and kelvin. It is about 8.31L⋅kPa/(mol⋅K) when you use kPa. If a problem gives a value, use that one.

The ideal gas is a simplified model. Real gases come closest to it at low pressure and high temperature, and depart from it at high pressure and low temperature, because molecular volume and attraction between molecules then start to matter.

Laws and rules

Avogadro's lawn1​V1​​=n2​V2​​V is volume (L), n is moles. Temperature and pressure are constant.
Molar volumeV=n×22.4V in litres at standard conditions, n in moles.
Ideal gas lawPV=nRTP in atm, V in L, n in mol, R=0.082L⋅atm/(mol⋅K), T in kelvin.

Worked examples

What is the volume of 0.5mol of a gas at standard conditions? How many moles are in 44.8L of it?
Show the solution
  1. Volume =0.5×22.4=11.2L.
  2. Moles =22.444.8​=2mol.
Answer: 11.2L and 2mol.
3mol of a gas occupy 12L at a given temperature and pressure. What volume do 5mol occupy under the same conditions?
Show the solution
  1. Avogadro: 312​=5V2​​.
  2. 312​=4L per mole, so V2​=4×5=20.
Answer: 20L.
Take R=0.08L⋅atm/(mol⋅K). What is the pressure of 2mol of gas in a 12L container at 300K?
Show the solution
  1. From PV=nRT: P=VnRT​.
  2. Numerator: 2×0.08×300=48.
  3. P=1248​=4. Check: 4×12=48.
Answer: 4atm.
Common mistakes
  • Using 22.4L away from standard conditions: at other temperatures or pressures you need the ideal gas law.
  • Using Celsius in nRT: kelvin only.
  • Thinking a heavier gas takes more volume: the number of molecules matters, and the molar volume is the same for all gases.
  • Mismatched units for the gas constant: 0.082 needs litres, atm and kelvin; if pressure is in kPa, use 8.31 or convert it.
Summary
  • Equal volumes of gases under the same conditions hold the same number of molecules.
  • One mole =22.4L at standard conditions only.
  • The ideal gas law uses kelvin, litres and atm.
  • The gas constant is about 0.082.

Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures

What you will learn
  • Define partial pressure
  • Add partial pressures to get the total
  • Calculate partial pressure from mole fraction

A mixture of gases

The particles of each gas in a mixture move independently of the others because there is no attraction between them. So each gas presses on the walls as if it were alone in the container.

The pressure one gas of the mixture creates is its partial pressure.

Dalton's law

The total pressure of a gas mixture equals the sum of the partial pressures of its gases.

A gas's partial pressure depends on its moles: the mole fraction times the total pressure. A gas making up half the moles makes half the pressure.

The mole fraction of a gas is its moles divided by the total moles, and the mole fractions in a mixture add up to 1.

Gas collected over water

When a gas is collected over water it is mixed with water vapor. The total pressure is the dry gas pressure plus the vapor pressure of water, so we subtract the water vapor pressure to find the gas alone.

Laws and rules

Dalton's lawPtotal​=PA​+PB​+PC​P is pressure in atm. Each term is the partial pressure of one gas.
Partial pressurePA​=ntotal​nA​​×Ptotal​n is moles, and the fraction is the mole fraction of gas A.

Worked examples

A mixture of three gases has partial pressures 1.5, 2 and 0.5atm. What is the total pressure?
Show the solution
  1. Add, by Dalton: 1.5+2+0.5.
  2. 1.5+2=3.5, then 3.5+0.5=4.
Answer: 4atm.
A mixture holds 2mol of nitrogen and 3mol of oxygen at a total pressure of 5atm. What is the partial pressure of each gas?
Show the solution
  1. Total moles =2+3=5.
  2. Nitrogen: 52​×5=2atm.
  3. Oxygen: 53​×5=3atm. Check: 2+3=5.
Answer: Nitrogen 2atm, oxygen 3atm.
A gas is collected over water at a total pressure of 1.0atm. Water vapor pressure at that temperature is 0.04atm. What is the pressure of the gas alone?
Show the solution
  1. Total = gas + water vapor, so gas =1.0−0.04.
  2. 1.00−0.04=0.96.
Answer: 0.96atm.
Common mistakes
  • Subtracting partial pressures from each other instead of adding them to find the total.
  • Forgetting water vapor when a gas is collected over water.
  • Thinking a heavier gas makes a larger partial pressure: moles matter, not mass.
Summary
  • Total pressure = sum of partial pressures.
  • Partial pressure = mole fraction times total pressure.
  • Gas collected over water: subtract the water vapor pressure.
  • Number of moles matters, not the kind of gas.
