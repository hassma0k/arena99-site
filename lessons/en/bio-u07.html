1

Plant tissues, root and leaf

What you will learn
  • Tell dermal, vascular, ground and meristem tissue apart.
  • Explain how the root is built for absorption.
  • Describe the layers of a leaf and why stomata are mostly underneath.
  • Explain how a trunk gets thicker by secondary growth.

Plant tissues

A tissue is a group of similar cells working together on one job. A plant has three tissue systems: dermal tissue (the epidermis) covers and protects, vascular tissue (xylem and phloem) transports, and ground tissue makes and stores food and gives support.

Alongside them is meristem tissue: cells that divide again and again at the tips of roots and stems and make new cells. It is the source of all growth; the other tissues come from its cells.

Root structure

The root anchors the plant and absorbs water and minerals. Root hairs are tiny extensions of epidermal cells; they greatly increase the absorbing surface, so the root takes in more water and minerals.

At the tip a root cap protects the growing point as it pushes through soil. Behind it is the meristem zone that lengthens the root. In the centre is the vascular cylinder containing xylem and phloem. Around it lie the cortex (ground tissue that stores food) and then the endodermis, which controls what enters the cylinder.

Leaf structure

A leaf is covered by an upper and a lower epidermis with a waxy layer that cuts water loss. Between them is the middle tissue: tall palisade cells packed with chloroplasts under the upper epidermis (they make most of the food), and below them spongy cells with air spaces that let gases move.

Veins are bundles of xylem and phloem. Stomata are usually more numerous in the lower epidermis because that side is cooler and less exposed to the sun, so less water is lost by transpiration while carbon dioxide can still enter.

Secondary growth

Primary growth lengthens the plant by meristems at the tips. Secondary growth makes it thicker, and happens in trees and shrubs. It is driven by the vascular cambium, a meristem layer between the xylem and phloem.

The cambium makes secondary xylem inward and secondary phloem outward, so the trunk widens year after year. Each growing season adds a ring of wood, so the rings roughly tell the tree's age. A ring is wide in a wet year and narrow in a dry one.

Outside, the cork cambium makes protective cork. Everything outside the vascular cambium (phloem and cork) is called bark.

Laws and rules

What the vascular cambium doesvascular cambium→secondary xylem (inward)+secondary phloem (outward)The vascular cambium is a meristem between xylem and phloem; it adds wood toward the centre and phloem outward, so the trunk thickens.
Where growth comes fromapical meristem→more lengthMeristems at root and shoot tips add length; the vascular cambium adds thickness.

Worked examples

Choose the part responsible for each: (a) a root getting longer, (b) a tree trunk getting thicker, (c) more water absorbed.
Show the solution
  1. Length comes from cell division in the apical meristem at the root tip.
  2. Thickness comes from secondary growth, driven by the vascular cambium.
  3. Absorption is increased by root hairs because they enlarge the surface area.
Answer: (a) apical meristem, (b) vascular cambium, (c) root hairs.
An epidermal root cell without a hair has a surface area of 4 units. If its root hair adds 6 units, how many times as large does its absorbing area become?
Show the solution
  1. Area with the hair: 4+6=10 units.
  2. Divide new by old: 410​.
  3. 410​=2.5.
Answer: The absorbing area becomes 2.5 times as large, which is why root hairs matter.
A tree trunk is cut and shows 9 complete rings of wood. About how old is the tree? Which rings are newest: those near the centre or those near the phloem?
Show the solution
  1. Each growing season adds one ring, so the number of rings equals the years: 9 years.
  2. The cambium lies near the outside between xylem and phloem and adds new wood on its inner side.
  3. So the oldest wood is at the centre and the newest is next to the cambium.
Answer: About 9 years old; the newest ring is the one farthest from the centre (next to the cambium).
Common mistakes
  • Thinking root hairs are small roots that carry water; they are extensions of epidermal cells that increase the absorbing surface.
  • Mixing up the apical meristem (length) with the vascular cambium (thickness).
  • Thinking stomata are mostly on the top because it gets the light; the cooler lower side loses less water.
  • Mixing up ground tissue (makes and stores food) with the transport tissues (xylem and phloem).
Summary
  • Meristem is tissue whose cells keep dividing; it is the source of growth.
  • Root hairs increase the absorbing surface.
  • Stomata are mostly on the lower leaf surface to reduce water loss.
  • The vascular cambium makes secondary xylem and phloem, thickening the trunk.

Water and food transport, transpiration and stomata

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell the job of xylem from the job of phloem.
  • Explain how water climbs to the top of a tall tree.
  • List what increases transpiration.
  • Explain how guard cells open and close a stoma.

Xylem and phloem

Xylem is a set of hollow tubes made of dead cells. It carries water and minerals from the roots to the stem and leaves, in one direction: upward. Its strengthened walls also support the plant.

Phloem is made of living cells (sieve tubes and their companion cells). It carries the sugars made in leaves to the parts that need or store them, such as roots and fruits. That is why removing a full ring of phloem around a stem cuts food off from the roots and they die.

Sugar moves in phloem from a source (the leaf that makes it) to a sink (a root or fruit that uses or stores it), so it can travel up or down depending on where it is needed.

How does water rise?

The main force is transpiration pull together with the cohesion of water. As water evaporates from the leaf it pulls the water behind it in the xylem; water molecules stick to each other, so the column stays unbroken and is pulled upward, like liquid drawn up a straw.

Root pressure pushes water up a little from below, but it is not enough to lift water to the top of a tall tree. The pull from above does the work.

Water enters the root by osmosis from the soil into the root-hair cells, because their solute concentration is higher. Besides cohesion between water molecules, water also sticks to the xylem walls (adhesion), which helps hold the column up.

Transpiration

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from a plant, mostly through the stomata of leaves. It increases with high temperature, dry air, wind and strong light, and decreases with high humidity. It has two uses: it pulls water and minerals upward, and it cools the leaf.

If transpiration is faster than the roots can absorb, the plant wilts for a while even when the soil is moist. This often happens at midday in the heat.

Stomata and guard cells

A stoma is a tiny opening in the leaf epidermis where carbon dioxide, needed for photosynthesis, enters and water vapour leaves. Each stoma lies between two guard cells that control it; they are the only epidermal cells with chloroplasts.

When potassium ions enter the guard cells, water follows by osmosis; the cells fill, swell and bend apart, so the stoma opens (usually in daylight). When they lose water they shrink and the stoma closes. In drought the hormone abscisic acid is released and closes the stomata to save water.

Laws and rules

Direction of transport in xylemXylem: roots→leavesCarries water and minerals upward only, by transpiration pull and water cohesion.
Direction of transport in phloemPhloem: leaves→other plant partsCarries the sugars made in leaves to where food is used or stored.
What raises transpirationhigher heat+dryness+wind→more transpirationThese speed up water vapour leaving the leaf; high humidity slows it.
Opening a stomaguard cells fill with water→swell→stoma openLosing water makes them shrink and the stoma closes.

Worked examples

A full ring of bark and phloem is removed around a tree stem, but the xylem is left. What happens to the roots and the leaves?
Show the solution
  1. Phloem carries sugar from the leaves to the roots, and it has been cut, so sugar piles up above the ring and cannot cross it.
  2. No food reaches the roots, so over time they starve and die.
  3. The xylem is intact, so water still reaches the leaves and they stay alive for a while before the dead roots affect them.
Answer: The roots die because sugar no longer reaches them; the leaves stay alive for a time because the xylem is still working.
In one square centimetre of a leaf there are 240 stomata on the lower surface and 30 on the upper. How many times as many stomata does the lower surface have as the upper? Why does this help the plant?
Show the solution
  1. Divide lower by upper: 30240​.
  2. 30240​=8.
  3. The lower side is cooler and less exposed to the sun, so stomata there let COX2​ in with the least water loss.
Answer: The lower surface has 8 times as many stomata as the upper; being there reduces water lost by transpiration.
A plant wilts at midday on a hot day although its soil is moist, then recovers in the evening. Explain this. What does the plant do in a long drought?
Show the solution
  1. At midday the heat is strong, so transpiration is very fast.
  2. Transpiration outruns what the roots can absorb, so the plant wilts for a while despite moist soil.
  3. In the evening the leaf cools and transpiration falls, so absorption catches up and the plant recovers.
  4. In a long drought the plant releases abscisic acid, the guard cells shrink and the stomata close.
Answer: It wilts because transpiration is faster than absorption at midday; in drought it closes its stomata with abscisic acid to save water.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up xylem (water and minerals upward) with phloem (sugar from leaves to the rest of the plant).
  • Thinking the root pushes water to the top of a tree; the main force is transpiration pull and the cohesion of water molecules.
  • Thinking moist soil always prevents wilting; a plant can wilt if transpiration is faster than absorption.
  • Thinking a stoma opens when the guard cells lose water; it opens when they fill up and swell.
Summary
  • Xylem carries water and minerals up; phloem carries sugar made in leaves.
  • Water rises by transpiration pull and the cohesion of water.
  • Heat, dryness and wind increase transpiration.
  • Guard cells full of water open the stoma; losing water closes it.

Plant hormones and tropisms

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Match each plant hormone to its main job.
  • Explain bending toward light by the distribution of auxin.
  • Explain apical dominance and why cutting the tip lets side buds grow.
  • Decide whether a tropism is positive or negative for roots and stems.

Plant hormones

A plant hormone is a chemical made by plant cells in small amounts. It travels to another place and regulates growth and responses to the environment. Five matter most.

  • Auxin: lengthens cells, makes stems bend toward light, and produces apical dominance.
  • Gibberellin: lengthens the stem, and ends seed dormancy so seeds germinate.
  • Cytokinin: stimulates cell division, delays leaf ageing (yellowing and withering), and stimulates side buds.
  • Ethylene: a gas hormone that speeds fruit ripening and makes leaves fall.
  • Abscisic acid: the stress hormone; closes stomata in drought and keeps seeds and buds dormant.

Apical dominance

The terminal bud at the top of the stem makes auxin that moves downward and stops the side buds from growing, so the plant grows tall with a single stem.

If the tip is cut off, the auxin that held back the side buds is gone, so they grow and the plant becomes bushy. This is why farmers trim the tops of hedges. Cytokinin works the opposite way: it stimulates side-bud growth.

Tropisms

A tropism is growth of a plant part toward or away from a stimulus. Growth toward the stimulus is a positive tropism; growth away from it is a negative tropism.

  • Phototropism (light): the stem is positive and grows toward light.
  • Gravitropism (gravity): the root is positive and grows down; the stem is negative and grows up.
  • Hydrotropism (water): the root is positive and grows toward water.
  • Thigmotropism (touch): a tendril grows coiled around the support it touches.

How does auxin cause bending?

When light comes from one side, auxin collects on the shaded side of the stem, so those cells lengthen more than the cells on the lit side. The longer side pushes the tip over, and the stem bends toward the light.

The same idea explains gravitropism. In a horizontal stem auxin collects on the lower side and lengthens its cells, so the stem curves up. In a root the same high concentration holds growth back, so the upper side grows more and the root curves down.

Laws and rules

Phototropismmore auxin on shaded side→longer cells→bend toward lightThe lit side has less auxin, so it lengthens less.
Apical dominancecut terminal bud→no auxin→side buds growIt is the terminal bud's auxin that holds back the side buds.
Naming a tropismgrowth toward stimulus=positive tropismGrowth away from the stimulus is a negative tropism. The root is positive to gravity; the stem is negative to it.

Worked examples

A seed germinates in the dark, lying on its side. Which way does its root grow and which way its stem? Name each response and say whether it is positive or negative.
Show the solution
  1. Gravity points downward in both cases.
  2. The root grows down, toward gravity: positive gravitropism.
  3. The stem grows up, away from gravity: negative gravitropism.
Answer: The root grows down (positive gravitropism); the stem grows up (negative gravitropism).
Light falls on a plant stem from the left. On which side does auxin collect, and which way does the stem bend?
Show the solution
  1. The side opposite the light, the right, is the shaded side.
  2. Auxin collects on the shaded side, the right.
  3. The right-hand cells lengthen more, pushing the tip to the left, toward the light.
Answer: Auxin collects on the shaded right side, and the stem bends left, toward the light.
Three identical plants: plant A is left alone; the farmer cuts the terminal bud of plant B; he cuts the terminal bud of plant C and puts auxin on the cut. What happens to the side buds of each?
Show the solution
  1. In A the terminal bud makes auxin that holds back the side buds, so they stay dormant.
  2. In B the source of auxin is gone, so the side buds grow.
  3. In C the added auxin takes the place of the terminal bud's auxin, so the side buds stay held back.
Answer: The side buds grow only in plant B; they do not grow in A or C.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking auxin collects on the lit side; it collects on the shaded side.
  • Mixing up gibberellin (wakes dormant seeds and lengthens stems) and cytokinin (divides cells and delays ageing).
  • Reading "positive" and "negative" as good and bad; positive is toward the stimulus, negative is away.
  • Forgetting that roots and stems respond to gravity in opposite ways.
Summary
  • Auxin lengthens cells and produces apical dominance and bending toward light.
  • Gibberellin ends seed dormancy; cytokinin divides cells and delays ageing.
  • Ethylene ripens fruit; abscisic acid closes stomata in drought.
  • The root is positive to gravity and the stem is negative to it.

Plant groups and alternation of generations

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Compare nonvascular, seedless vascular and seed plants.
  • Follow the life cycle and the chromosome sets in each phase.
  • Say which phase dominates in mosses, ferns and seed plants.
  • Tell gymnosperms from angiosperms.

Three main groups

Nonvascular plants (mosses) have no xylem or phloem, so they cannot move water far. That is why they are short and usually live in damp places, taking up water directly through their surface. Fertilization needs a film of water because their sperm have flagella and swim to the egg.

Seedless vascular plants (ferns and horsetails) have xylem and phloem, so they carry water upward and grow taller. But they reproduce by spores, not seeds, formed on the underside of their leaves, and their fertilization also needs water.

Seed plants are vascular and make seeds. There are two kinds: gymnosperms such as pine carry their seeds uncovered on the scales of cones, with no flower or fruit; angiosperms (flowering plants) form their seeds inside a fruit. In seed plants the male gametes travel inside pollen grains, so fertilization does not need a film of water.

Alternation of generations

A plant's life cycle has two phases that take turns. The sporophyte (2n) makes spores by meiosis, so spores carry n chromosomes. A spore grows into the gametophyte (n), which makes gametes by mitosis.

Two gametes (n and n) fuse in fertilization to form a zygote (2n), which grows into a new sporophyte, and the cycle repeats.

Which phase dominates?

The dominant phase is the larger, longer-lived one: the plant you see.

  • Mosses: the gametophyte (n) is the dominant green plant; the small sporophyte lives on it.
  • Ferns: the sporophyte (2n) dominates; it is the large leafy plant, while the gametophyte is small, thin and lives independently.
  • Seed plants: the sporophyte (2n) dominates; the gametophyte is tiny, inside the flower or cone.

Laws and rules

Making sporessporophyte (2n)→spores (n)By meiosis; spores grow into the gametophyte (n).
Making gametesgametophyte (n)→gametes (n)By mitosis, so the chromosome set does not change.
Fertilizationgamete (n)+gamete (n)→zygote (2n)The zygote grows into the sporophyte.

Worked examples

The gametophyte cells of a moss have 8 chromosomes (n=8). How many chromosomes are in the zygote? In the spores?
Show the solution
  1. Gametes have n=8.
  2. Fertilization joins two gametes: 8+8=16, so the zygote is 2n=16.
  3. Spores are made by meiosis from the 2n sporophyte, so they carry n=8.
Answer: The zygote has 16 chromosomes and the spores have 8.
A plant has large leafy fronds with spore patches on their undersides and makes no seeds. Name its group, its dominant phase, and say why it needs water for fertilization.
Show the solution
  1. A vascular plant (xylem and phloem) that reproduces by spores without seeds is a fern.
  2. The large leafy plant is the dominant 2n sporophyte.
  3. Its sperm have flagella and swim through a film of water to the egg.
Answer: A seedless vascular plant (fern); the dominant phase is the sporophyte (2n); it needs water so sperm can swim to the egg.
The body cells of a fern sporophyte have 50 chromosomes. How many chromosomes are in a spore? A gamete? The zygote? Where do pine seeds form?
Show the solution
  1. Sporophyte 2n=50, so n=25.
  2. A spore is made by meiosis: 25 chromosomes; a gamete is made by mitosis from the gametophyte: 25 as well.
  3. The zygote joins two gametes: 25+25=50.
  4. Pine is a gymnosperm, so its seeds lie uncovered on the scales of cones.
Answer: Spore 25, gamete 25, zygote 50 chromosomes; pine seeds form on cone scales.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking mosses are vascular; they have no xylem or phloem, which is why they stay short and near water.
  • Thinking the sporophyte always dominates; in mosses the gametophyte (n) dominates.
  • Thinking ferns make seeds; they reproduce by spores.
  • Thinking gametes are made by meiosis; spores are made by meiosis, gametes by mitosis.
Summary
  • Mosses are short and nonvascular, ferns are vascular without seeds, pine and flowering plants are seed plants.
  • Mosses and ferns need water for fertilization.
  • The 2n sporophyte makes n spores; the n gametophyte makes n gametes.
  • Dominant: gametophyte in mosses; sporophyte in ferns and seed plants.

Monocots and dicots

What you will learn
  • Know what a cotyledon is and how many each group has.
  • Compare leaves, stems, flowers and roots in the two groups.
  • Classify a plant as a monocot or dicot from one or more features.

What is a cotyledon?

A cotyledon is an embryo leaf inside the seed. In dicots such as the broad bean the two cotyledons store the food; in monocots such as wheat the food stays in the endosperm and the cotyledon absorbs it and passes it to the embryo. Flowering plants split into two groups by the number of cotyledons: monocots (one) and dicots (two).

Monocots

Examples include wheat, maize, rice, onion and the date palm. Their features:

  • One cotyledon in the seed.
  • Leaf veins are parallel.
  • Vascular bundles are scattered through the ground tissue of the stem.
  • Flower parts (petals and so on) come in multiples of 3.
  • Fibrous roots: many thin roots of similar size.
  • Usually no vascular cambium, so no secondary thickening.
  • Pollen grains with one pore.

Dicots

Examples include broad bean, kidney bean, orange, rose and tomato. Their features:

  • Two cotyledons in the seed.
  • Leaf veins form a network.
  • Vascular bundles are arranged in a ring in the stem.
  • Flower parts come in multiples of 4 or 5.
  • Taproot: one large main root with smaller roots branching from it.
  • A vascular cambium between xylem and phloem, so woody dicots grow thicker by secondary growth.
  • Pollen grains with three pores.

Laws and rules

Monocotone cotyledon→parallel veins+scattered bundlesFlower parts in multiples of 3; fibrous roots.
Dicottwo cotyledons→net veins+ring of bundlesFlower parts in multiples of 4 or 5; taproot.

Worked examples

A plant's leaf has parallel veins. Which group is it in? What would its stem look like?
Show the solution
  1. Parallel veins are a monocot feature.
  2. In monocots the vascular bundles in the stem are scattered.
Answer: A monocot, with scattered vascular bundles in its stem.
A stem cross section shows its vascular bundles in a neat ring. What group is it? What do you expect of its root and its seed's cotyledons?
Show the solution
  1. Bundles in a ring is a dicot feature.
  2. Dicots have a taproot.
  3. And their seeds have two cotyledons.
Answer: A dicot, with a taproot and a seed with two cotyledons.
One plant has flowers with 6 petals and fibrous roots; another has flowers with 5 petals and one big main root. Classify each.
Show the solution
  1. 6 is a multiple of 3 and the roots are fibrous, so the first is a monocot.
  2. 5 petals and a big main root (taproot) point to a dicot.
  3. Check: the first matches two monocot features and the second matches two dicot features.
Answer: The first is a monocot; the second is a dicot.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up parallel veins (monocot) and net veins (dicot).
  • Mixing up bundle arrangement: scattered for monocots, a ring for dicots.
  • Thinking the number in the name is the petal count; it is the number of cotyledons in the seed.
  • Judging by one feature alone; check two or more features before classifying.
Summary
  • Monocots: one cotyledon, parallel veins, scattered bundles, fibrous roots, flower parts in 3s.
  • Dicots: two cotyledons, net veins, ring of bundles, taproot, flower parts in 4s or 5s.

Flower, double fertilization, fruit and germination

What you will learn
  • Name the flower parts and what each does.
  • Tell pollination from fertilization.
  • Work out chromosome numbers in the zygote and endosperm.
  • Know what the ovary and ovule become, and what seeds need to germinate.

Flower parts

The flower is the sexual reproductive organ of flowering plants. Sepals protect the bud, and coloured petals attract pollinating insects. The stamen is the male part: a filament carrying an anther that makes pollen grains.

The carpel is the female part: a sticky stigma that receives pollen, a style joining stigma to ovary, and an ovary holding one or more ovules. Flowers are adapted to their pollinator: insect-pollinated flowers are colourful with nectar, wind-pollinated flowers are small with no showy petals and make a lot of light pollen.

Pollination and fertilization

Pollination is the transfer of pollen from the anther to the stigma, by wind, insects or birds. Fertilization is the fusion of male and female gametes. They are two different events.

A pollen grain germinates on the stigma and grows a pollen tube down the style toward the ovary, carrying two sperm nuclei (n each) to the ovule.

Double fertilization

In angiosperms two fusions happen. One sperm nucleus fuses with the egg to make the zygote (2n), which grows into the embryo. The other sperm nucleus fuses with the two polar nuclei to make the endosperm (3n), which feeds the embryo.

Seed, fruit and germination

After fertilization the ovule becomes a seed (embryo, food store and coat), and the ovary becomes a fruit that protects the seeds and helps spread them. The embryo has a radicle (which becomes the root), a plumule (which becomes the shoot and leaves) and one or two cotyledons.

A healthy seed needs three things to germinate: water, which it absorbs and swells and wakes up; oxygen for respiration; and a suitable temperature. The radicle emerges first. Gibberellin ends the dormancy of many seeds and stimulates germination.

Laws and rules

Making the zygotesperm nucleus (n)+egg (n)→zygote (2n)The zygote grows into the embryo.
Making the endospermsperm nucleus (n)+two polar nuclei (n+n)→endosperm (3n)It stores food and feeds the embryo.
After fertilizationovule→seed,ovary→fruitThe fruit protects the seeds and helps spread them.

Worked examples

Leaf cells of a flowering plant have 20 chromosomes. How many are in the egg? The zygote? An endosperm nucleus?
Show the solution
  1. A leaf cell is a body cell: 2n=20, so n=10.
  2. The egg is a gamete: n=10 chromosomes.
  3. The zygote is egg plus sperm nucleus: 10+10=20 (2n).
  4. The endosperm is 3n=3×10=30.
Answer: Egg 10, zygote 20, endosperm 30 chromosomes.
A flower's ovary holds 5 ovules and all are fertilized. How many seeds and how many fruits form? How many nuclear fusions happened in all?
Show the solution
  1. Each fertilized ovule becomes a seed, so 5 seeds form.
  2. One ovary becomes one fruit.
  3. Each ovule has two fusions (egg with a sperm nucleus, then the polar nuclei with a sperm nucleus): 5×2=10.
Answer: 5 seeds and 1 fruit; 10 nuclear fusions.
Three sets of identical healthy seeds: (a) in moist, warm, aerated soil, (b) in dry warm soil, (c) submerged in water in a cold place. Which germinate, and why?
Show the solution
  1. (a) has water, oxygen and warmth: all three conditions are met.
  2. (b) has no water, so the seed does not swell or wake up.
  3. (c) is submerged so little oxygen reaches it, and the cold slows germination.
Answer: Only (a) germinates, because it alone meets the need for water, oxygen and warmth.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up pollination (pollen reaching the stigma) and fertilization (gametes fusing).
  • Forgetting that endosperm is 3n, not 2n: one sperm nucleus n plus two polar nuclei.
  • Thinking the seed becomes the fruit; the ovary becomes the fruit and the ovule becomes the seed.
  • Thinking germination needs light or rich soil; the basic needs are water, oxygen and warmth.
Summary
  • The anther makes pollen; the ovary holds the ovules.
  • Pollination is pollen transfer; fertilization is gamete fusion.
  • Double fertilization gives a 2n zygote and a 3n endosperm.
  • The ovule becomes a seed and the ovary a fruit; germination needs water, oxygen and warmth.
