Genetics Terms and the Law of Segregation

What you will learn
  • Tell apart gene, allele, genotype and phenotype.
  • Identify dominant and recessive alleles and predict the appearance.
  • Explain how the two alleles separate when gametes form.
  • Know what the first generation means.

Gene and allele

A gene is a stretch of DNA that carries the instructions for a trait, such as flower colour or stem height. One gene can come in different versions called alleles. For pea seed colour, there is an allele for yellow and an allele for green.

An individual inherits two alleles for each trait: one from the mother and one from the father. We write them with one letter: a capital for the dominant allele and a small letter for the recessive one, such as A and a.

Genotype and phenotype

The genotype is the set of alleles an individual carries, such as Aa. The phenotype is the trait we actually see, such as a red flower.

If the two alleles are the same, the individual is pure (homozygous), like AA or aa. If they differ, it is a hybrid (heterozygous), like Aa.

Dominant and recessive

A dominant allele shows its effect even when only one copy is present. A recessive allele hides beside a dominant one and shows only when two copies are present.

  • AA: pure dominant, dominant appearance.
  • Aa: hybrid, dominant appearance (the recessive allele is hidden but still there).
  • aa: pure recessive, the only genotype that shows the recessive appearance.

The law of segregation and the first generation

Gametes are sex cells: the egg in the female and the sperm in the male. Mendel's law of segregation says the two alleles of a trait separate when gametes form, so each gamete carries only one allele. An Aa individual gives half its gametes A and half a.

At fertilization a sperm and an egg meet, so the new individual has two alleles again. The offspring of two different pure lines are called the first generation; when they mate with each other they produce the second generation.

Worked examples

In peas, tall stem (T) is dominant over short (t). A plant is Tt. What does it look like and what gametes does it make?
Show the solution
  1. Allele T is dominant, so it shows: the plant is tall.
  2. The alleles separate when gametes form, so each gamete carries one allele: T or t.
  3. Each kind of gamete is equally likely, half and half.
Answer: It is tall, and its gametes are T and t in equal numbers.
A pure yellow-seeded pea (YY) is crossed with a pure green-seeded pea (yy); yellow is dominant. What are the genotype and phenotype of the first generation?
Show the solution
  1. The father always gives Y and the mother always gives y, because each is pure.
  2. All offspring are Yy.
  3. They carry a dominant allele, so they are yellow.
Answer: The whole first generation is Yy with yellow seeds.
First-generation plants Yy are crossed with each other and give 400 plants. How many green-seeded plants do you expect?
Show the solution
  1. Yy×Yy gives YY, Yy, Yy, yy: 4 equally likely outcomes.
  2. Green shows only in yy, one outcome out of 4: 41​.
  3. Expected number =400×41​=100.
Answer: About 100 green-seeded plants.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing genotype and phenotype: Aa is a genotype; the phenotype is what we see, here the dominant one.
  • Thinking the recessive allele is lost in a hybrid. It is still there, and shows in offspring that inherit it from both parents.
  • Thinking a gamete carries two alleles for a trait. A gamete carries one; the individual carries two.
Summary
  • An allele is a version of a gene, and an individual inherits two alleles per trait.
  • The genotype is the alleles; the phenotype is the visible trait.
  • A dominant allele shows with one copy; a recessive one needs two.
  • Alleles separate when gametes form, and the offspring of two pure lines are the first generation.

Punnett Squares, Ratios and Probability

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Draw a Punnett square and read its results.
  • Turn a ratio into a probability and an expected number.
  • Work out conditional probability from the possible boxes.
  • Use the test cross and work out the parents' genotypes.

The Punnett square

A Punnett square is a table: write the gametes of one parent on top and the gametes of the other down the side, then fill each box by combining the two alleles. Each box is one outcome, and all boxes are equally likely.

  • Write each parent's genotype.
  • Write each parent's gametes (one allele per gamete).
  • Fill the boxes by combining the top allele with the side allele.
  • Count genotypes and then phenotypes: that is the ratio.

A cross between two hybrids

In Aa×Aa each parent gives A or a, so the boxes are AA, Aa, Aa, aa. The genotype ratio is 1:2:1.

If A shows complete dominance, the dominant appearance fills three boxes and the recessive one box, so the phenotype ratio is 3:1.

From ratio to probability and number

We turn a ratio into a probability by dividing the wanted boxes by all boxes. From Aa×Aa the recessive appearance has probability 41​ and the dominant one 43​.

To find the expected number of offspring, multiply the probability by the total. Real results come closer to the expected ones as the numbers grow.

To find the chance of two independent events together, multiply their probabilities. Every birth starts fresh: for parents Aa×Aa, the chance that both the first and second children are affected is 41​×41​=161​, and an affected child born earlier does not change the chance for the next child (41​).

For conditional probability, remove the boxes the information rules out, then count using only the boxes that remain.

Test cross and parent genotypes

How do we know whether an individual with the dominant appearance is AA or Aa? We cross it with a recessive aa. If all offspring show the dominant appearance, the individual is pure AA. If even one recessive offspring appears, the individual is Aa and the ratio is 1:1.

We can also work backwards: if two parents with the dominant appearance have a recessive child aa, each gave an a, so both parents are Aa.

Laws and rules

Results of Aa×Aa1:2:1 genotype and 3:1 phenotypeComplete dominance; the recessive appearance is in a quarter of the offspring.
Test crossAa×aa gives 1:1The unknown individual is Aa if recessive offspring appear, and AA if none do.
ProbabilityProbability=all boxeswanted boxes​Boxes from a Punnett square.
Expected numberExpected number=Probability×total offspringTotal offspring is a whole number with no unit.
Product ruleBoth events=Probability of first×Probability of secondFor independent events only, such as two births in a row.

Worked examples

In a flower, purple (P) is dominant over white (p). Two plants Pp×Pp are crossed. What is the probability that a flower is white?
Show the solution
  1. Each parent's gametes are P or p.
  2. The boxes are PP, Pp, Pp, pp.
  3. White shows only in pp: one box out of 4.
Answer: 41​
A tall plant (T dominant) of unknown genotype is crossed with a short plant tt and gives 52 tall and 48 short offspring. What is the tall plant's genotype?
Show the solution
  1. If it were TT, it would always give T, so all offspring would be tall Tt.
  2. Short offspring tt exist, so each took a t from the tall parent.
  3. So the tall plant carries t: it is Tt. Check: Tt×tt gives 1:1, and the counts are close to that.
Answer: Tt
Two parents with normal appearance have a child with albinism, a recessive trait. (a) What are the parents' genotypes? (b) What is the probability the next child is affected? (c) If the next child is unaffected, what is the probability that the child is a carrier?
Show the solution
  1. The affected child aa took an a from each parent, and both look dominant, so each parent is Aa.
  2. Aa×Aa gives AA, Aa, Aa, aa, so the affected chance is 41​.
  3. Given the child is unaffected, remove the aa box: 3 boxes remain, AA, Aa, Aa.
  4. A carrier Aa fills two of the three: 32​.
Answer: (a) Aa and Aa (b) 41​ (c) 32​
Common mistakes
  • Working out conditional probability from all 4 boxes after learning the child is unaffected. Remove the ruled-out box and divide by what is left: 32​, not 42​.
  • Thinking a 3:1 ratio means every four children contain exactly one recessive child. It is a probability, and each child is a fresh chance.
  • Forgetting that two parents with the dominant appearance can both be Aa. A recessive child is the proof.
Summary
  • A Punnett square shows every possible meeting of gametes, all equally likely.
  • Aa×Aa gives 1:2:1 by genotype and 3:1 by phenotype.
  • Probability = wanted boxes ÷ all boxes; expected number = probability × total.
  • A test cross with a recessive individual reveals an unknown dominant genotype.

Dihybrid Crosses and Independent Assortment

What you will learn
  • Write the gametes of an individual that is hybrid for two traits.
  • Get the 9:3:3:1 phenotype ratio and work out its probabilities.
  • Know the condition for independent assortment and tell it from linkage.

What is a dihybrid cross?

It is a cross that follows two traits together, such as seed colour and seed shape. An AaBb individual is hybrid for both: the first pair is Aa and the second Bb.

The law of independent assortment

It says the alleles of one gene move into gametes independently of the alleles of another gene, provided the two genes are on different chromosomes.

So an AaBb individual makes four kinds of gametes in equal numbers, each 41​: AB, Ab, aB and ab. Take one allele from each pair and try every combination.

Results of AaBb×AaBb

The Punnett square has 16 boxes. The phenotype ratio is 9:3:3:1: nine dominant in both traits, three dominant in the first and recessive in the second, three the other way round, and one recessive in both.

A quicker way is to solve each trait on its own and multiply. For one trait, dominant is 43​ and recessive 41​, so dominant in both is 43​×43​=169​.

Linked genes

When two genes lie close together on the same chromosome, they usually travel together as a unit. They do not follow independent assortment, and the ratios differ from 9:3:3:1.

For example, if A and B lie on one chromosome and a and b on its partner, the hybrid mostly makes only two gametes, AB and ab. Two such hybrids then give a 3:1 phenotype ratio instead of 9:3:3:1 when linkage is complete.

Linkage is not always complete. Crossing over lets matching chromosomes swap pieces, so a few new combinations still appear.

Laws and rules

Number of gamete types2nn: number of hybrid gene pairs; AaBb has two, so 22=4 gametes.
Phenotype ratio of AaBb×AaBb9:3:3:1Dominant in both, dominant in the first only, dominant in the second only, recessive in both.

Worked examples

What gametes does an AaBB individual make?
Show the solution
  1. The first pair is hybrid Aa: choose A or a.
  2. The second pair is pure BB: only B can be chosen.
  3. Combinations: AB and aB. Number of types 21=2.
Answer: AB and aB
In peas, round (R) is dominant over wrinkled (r), and yellow (Y) over green (y). RrYy×RrYy gives 160 seeds. How many round green seeds do you expect?
Show the solution
  1. Round: 43​. Green yy: 41​.
  2. Round green =43​×41​=163​.
  3. Number =160×163​=10×3=30.
Answer: 30 seeds
What is the probability of an aabb offspring from AaBb×Aabb?
Show the solution
  1. Solve each trait alone. Aa×Aa gives aa with probability 41​.
  2. Bb×bb gives bb with probability 21​, since the second parent gives only b.
  3. The traits are independent, so multiply: 41​×21​=81​.
  4. Check: gametes 4×2=8 boxes, and aabb is exactly one of them.
Answer: 81​
Common mistakes
  • Writing the gametes of AaBb with both alleles of one pair together, like AA or Bb. A gamete takes one allele from each pair.
  • Believing 9:3:3:1 always holds. It holds only with independent assortment, complete dominance in both traits, and a cross of two hybrids.
  • Drawing a 16-box square when multiplying two simple probabilities will do. Solving each trait alone is faster and safer.
Summary
  • An AaBb individual makes 4 equal gametes.
  • AaBb×AaBb gives 9:3:3:1 by phenotype in 16 boxes.
  • Solve each trait alone, then multiply the probabilities.
  • Genes close together on one chromosome are linked and do not assort independently.

Incomplete Dominance, Codominance, Epistasis and Polygenic Traits

What you will learn
  • Tell incomplete dominance from codominance.
  • Work out the results of crossing two hybrids in incomplete dominance.
  • Explain epistasis and the modified 9:3:4 ratio.
  • Know that polygenic traits vary in a graded way.

Incomplete dominance

Here neither allele is fully dominant, so the hybrid looks in between its parents. In snapdragons, RR is red, rr is white and Rr is pink.

RR×rr gives a first generation that is all pink Rr. Crossing two pinks, Rr×Rr, gives 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. Here the phenotype ratio equals the genotype ratio 1:2:1. Crossing a pink with a white, Rr×rr, gives 1 pink : 1 white.

Codominance

In codominance both alleles show their full effect together in the hybrid, with no blending. Roan cattle have red hairs and white hairs side by side, and blood group AB shows both antigens.

The difference: incomplete dominance is a blend, one in-between appearance; codominance shows both traits at once, each complete.

Epistasis

In epistasis one gene hides the effect of another gene at a different position. In Labrador dogs, gene B sets the colour: black (B) is dominant over brown (b). A second gene (E) controls whether pigment is deposited: with genotype ee no pigment is deposited, so the dog is yellow whatever its B genotype.

From BbEe×BbEe the ratio is 9 black : 3 brown : 4 yellow. It is the 9:3:3:1 ratio with 3 and 1 merged, because ee hides both B and b.

Polygenic traits

Several genes control one trait, each adding a small effect, as in height and skin colour. The trait appears in continuous degrees rather than two forms, and the population forms a bell shape: most individuals in the middle, few at the extremes.

If each gene has two alleles of equal effect, the number of phenotype classes =2× number of genes +1. Two genes give 5 classes, and crossing two hybrids AaBb×AaBb gives 1:4:6:4:1 according to the number of effective alleles (0 to 4).

The environment (food and sunlight, for example) also affects these traits.

Laws and rules

Incomplete dominanceRr×Rr gives 1:2:1Red : pink : white, so phenotype ratio = genotype ratio.
Epistasis in LabradorsBbEe×BbEe gives 9:3:4Black : brown : yellow.
Polygenic inheritanceClasses=2×number of genes+1Two genes of equal effect give 5 phenotype classes, and the middle class is the most common.

Worked examples

Pink plants (Rr) are crossed together and give 80 plants. How many pink plants do you expect?
Show the solution
  1. Rr×Rr gives RR, Rr, Rr, rr, that is 1:2:1.
  2. Pink Rr fills two boxes out of 4: 21​.
  3. Number =80×21​=40.
Answer: 40 plants
In cattle, red is RR, white is WW and the hybrid RW is roan (red and white hairs together). What are the results of crossing red RR with roan RW?
Show the solution
  1. The red parent always gives R. The roan parent gives R or W equally.
  2. The boxes are RR and RW, each 21​.
  3. RR is red and RW is roan, since both alleles show together (codominance).
Answer: Half the offspring are red and half are roan.
In Labradors, B (black) is dominant over b (brown), and ee gives yellow. BbEe×BbEe gives 160 puppies. How many are yellow? How many are brown?
Show the solution
  1. Yellow is ee with any B genotype: three boxes with B and ee, and one bbee box, total 4 out of 16.
  2. Brown is bb with at least one E: 3 boxes out of 16.
  3. Yellow =160×164​=40. Brown =160×163​=30.
  4. Check: black is 9 boxes =90, and 90+30+40=160.
Answer: 40 yellow and 30 brown
Common mistakes
  • Mixing incomplete dominance with codominance: incomplete gives a new in-between colour; codominance shows both colours together.
  • Using the 3:1 ratio in incomplete dominance. The correct ratio is 1:2:1 because the hybrid has its own appearance.
  • Assuming epistasis keeps the ratio 9:3:3:1. Merging two appearances makes it 9:3:4 in our example.
  • Thinking two genes for a polygenic trait give only two or three classes. The effective alleles add up, so two genes give five classes.
Summary
  • Incomplete dominance: the hybrid is in between, and Rr×Rr gives 1:2:1.
  • Codominance: both alleles show together, each complete.
  • Epistasis: one gene hides another, changing the ratio to 9:3:4.
  • Polygenic: several genes for one trait give continuous degrees.

ABO Blood Groups and Multiple Alleles

★ Key
What you will learn
  • List the blood-group alleles and the genotypes of each group.
  • Explain the roles of codominance and recessiveness in blood groups.
  • Solve parent and child problems in the ABO system.

Three alleles for one gene

The blood-group gene has three alleles: IA, IB and i. This is multiple alleles: a population has more than two alleles of the gene, but each person carries only two, one from each parent.

How the alleles relate

Alleles IA and IB are codominant, so both show, and both are dominant over the recessive allele i. The groups are:

  • Group A: IAIA or IAi.
  • Group B: IBIB or IBi.
  • Group AB: IAIB only.
  • Group O: ii only.

Why? The antigens

Allele IA makes an antigen called A on the surface of red blood cells, IB makes antigen B, and i makes nothing. So group AB carries both antigens (codominance), and group O carries neither.

The body makes antibodies against antigens it lacks, which is why blood cannot be transfused between incompatible groups.

The Rh factor

Red blood cells carry another antigen, separate from ABO, called the Rh factor. A person who has it is positive (+) and one who lacks it is negative (−); the positive allele is dominant over the negative one. It is inherited through a different gene, so a full group is written like A+ or O−.

Shortcuts for solving

  • Two group O parents (ii×ii) have only group O children.
  • A group AB parent always gives IA or IB, so cannot have a group O child.
  • One parent AB and the other O (IAIB×ii): the children are group A or B only, half each.
  • A group O child took an i from each parent, so each parent has an i allele whatever their group.

Laws and rules

Result of IAi×IBi1:1:1:1Groups AB, A, B and O, a quarter of the children each.

Worked examples

What genotypes are possible for a person of blood group A?
Show the solution
  1. Allele IA is dominant over i.
  2. Group A appears when IA is paired with IA or with i.
Answer: IAIA or IAi
A father of group A with genotype IAi and a mother of group B with genotype IBi. What groups can their children have, and with what probability?
Show the solution
  1. Father's gametes: IA and i. Mother's gametes: IB and i.
  2. The boxes: IAIB, IAi, IBi, ii.
  3. These are groups AB, A, B and O in that order, each with probability 41​.
Answer: AB, A, B and O, each with probability 41​
A mother of group A and a father of group B have a child of group O. (a) What are the parents' genotypes? (b) What is the probability that the next child is group AB?
Show the solution
  1. The child ii took an i from each parent.
  2. The mother is group A and has an i, so she is IAi; the father is group B and has an i, so he is IBi.
  3. IAi×IBi gives AB in one box out of 4.
Answer: (a) Mother IAi, father IBi (b) 41​
Common mistakes
  • Thinking group A always means IAIA. It may be IAi, and a group O child can reveal that.
  • Treating IA as dominant over IB. They are codominant: together they both show, giving group AB.
  • Saying a person carries three alleles. The population has three alleles; a person carries only two.
  • Thinking that a child of one group changes the chance for the next child. Alleles are shuffled afresh in every pregnancy, so the chance of group AB stays 41​ whatever the earlier children were.
Summary
  • Blood groups have three alleles, IA, IB and i, and a person carries two.
  • IA and IB are codominant; i is recessive.
  • Group AB is IAIB and group O is ii only.
  • A group O child means each parent has an i allele.

Sex Determination and Sex-Linked Traits

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Explain how the sex of a baby is decided.
  • Write the genotypes of X-linked traits.
  • Solve problems with a carrier mother and an affected father.
  • Know why recessive X-linked traits are more common in males.

The sex chromosomes

A human body cell has 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs: 22 autosome pairs and one sex-chromosome pair. A female is XX and a male is XY.

Sex determination

The mother XX always gives X, while the father XY gives X or Y with equal chance. So the father's gamete decides the baby's sex: with Y it is a boy, with X it is a girl.

The chance of a boy is 21​ in every pregnancy, whatever the sex of earlier children.

Sex-linked traits

The genes for some traits, such as red-green colour blindness and haemophilia, lie on the X chromosome and have no partner on Y. We write the allele above the letter: XB is normal and Xb is the recessive allele that causes the trait.

  • A female is affected only if she is XbXb. XBXb looks normal and is a carrier.
  • A male has one copy, so XbY is affected, and a male cannot be a carrier.
  • So recessive X-linked traits show up in males more often than in females.

How the trait passes on

A male inherits his X from his mother only; he gives X to his daughters only and Y to his sons. An affected father therefore does not pass the trait to his sons; his daughters become carriers (if the mother is pure normal). An affected mother, however, has all her sons affected.

An affected son has a carrier or affected mother. The trait can skip a generation: it passes from a grandfather through his carrier daughter to his grandson.

Laws and rules

Carrier mother and normal fatherXBXb×XBYHalf the sons are affected XbY; all daughters look normal and half are carriers. Affected children are 41​ of all.
Carrier mother and affected fatherXBXb×XbYThe four boxes are equally likely: a carrier daughter, an affected daughter, a normal son and an affected son, 41​ each.

Worked examples

A father XY and a mother XX. What is the probability that the baby is a boy? Who decides the sex?
Show the solution
  1. Mother's gametes: X and X. Father's gametes: X and Y.
  2. The boxes: XX, XY, XX, XY, and two of them are boys.
  3. The father's gamete decides the sex.
Answer: 21​, decided by the father's gamete.
A woman who carries colour blindness, XBXb, marries a normal man XBY. What is the probability that a child is affected, out of all children? What is the probability for a son?
Show the solution
  1. Mother's gametes: XB and Xb. Father's gametes: XB and Y.
  2. The boxes: XBXB, XBY, XBXb, XbY.
  3. Affected XbY is one box out of 4: 41​.
  4. Sons are two boxes, XBY and XbY, so half of them are affected: 21​.
Answer: 41​ of all children, and 21​ of sons.
A colour-blind man (XbY) marries a pure normal woman (XBXB). What is the probability that their son is affected? That their daughter is a carrier?
Show the solution
  1. Father's gametes: Xb and Y. Mother's gametes: only XB.
  2. The boxes: XBXb and XBY.
  3. A son XBY is always normal: he took Y from his father and XB from his mother.
  4. A daughter XBXb is always a carrier: she took Xb from her father.
Answer: Affected son: 0 (never). Carrier daughter: 1 (all daughters are carriers).
Common mistakes
  • Writing a male as XbXb. A male has just one X: XbY.
  • Thinking an affected father passes the trait to his son. A son gets Y from his father, not X, so the trait comes from the mother.
  • Calling a male XBY a carrier. Only a hybrid female is a carrier; a male is either affected or normal.
Summary
  • A female is XX, a male is XY, and the father's gamete decides the sex.
  • The chance of a boy is 21​ in every pregnancy.
  • A male is affected by one recessive copy; a female needs two.
  • An affected father passes the trait to his daughters; a son inherits it from his mother.

Pedigrees and Human Heredity

What you will learn
  • Read the symbols of a pedigree chart.
  • Work out the inheritance pattern from a pedigree.
  • Know human chromosomes and the cause of Down syndrome.

Reading a pedigree

A pedigree is a family tree that follows a trait through several generations, because we cannot run mating experiments on people.

  • A square is a male and a circle is a female.
  • A shaded shape is an affected person; an unshaded one is unaffected.
  • A horizontal line between two shapes is a mating, with a line going down to the children.
  • Generations are arranged from top to bottom.

Finding the pattern

  • Two unaffected parents have an affected child: the trait is recessive, and if it is autosomal both parents are carriers Aa.
  • Two affected parents have an unaffected child: the trait is dominant, and both parents are Aa.
  • It appears in every generation and never skips: probably dominant. It skips generations: probably recessive.
  • An affected daughter with an unaffected father: it cannot be X-linked recessive, because a daughter takes an X from her father, who would then be affected.
  • Mostly affected males, affected sons of unaffected mothers, and no father-to-son transmission: X-linked recessive.

Chromosomes and human heredity

Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes. A picture of the chromosomes arranged in pairs (the karyotype) shows any fault in their number.

Cystic fibrosis is an example of a recessive autosomal trait, and Huntington disease of a dominant one. They are inherited by Mendel's same rules. Faults in the sex chromosomes include Klinefelter syndrome (a male XXY with 47 chromosomes) and Turner syndrome (a female with a single X and 45 chromosomes).

Down syndrome

It is caused by nondisjunction: chromosomes fail to separate when gametes form, so a gamete carries an extra copy of chromosome 21. After fertilization the cell has three copies, so the chromosome number is 47 instead of 46.

It is a fault in chromosome number, not in a single gene, so it does not follow Mendel's ratios. Its chance rises with the mother's age.

Worked examples

In a pedigree, two unaffected parents have an affected daughter. What is the inheritance pattern, and what are the parents' genotypes?
Show the solution
  1. The parents are unaffected yet the trait appeared in their daughter, so it is a recessive trait that was hidden.
  2. The affected daughter aa took an a from each parent, who both look normal, so each is Aa.
  3. The daughter is affected while her father is not, so it is not X-linked: it is autosomal.
Answer: Autosomal recessive; the parents are Aa and Aa.
How many chromosomes are in a body cell of a person with Down syndrome? How many in the extra gamete?
Show the solution
  1. A normal gamete has 23 chromosomes. The gamete with an extra copy of chromosome 21 has 23+1=24.
  2. It meets a normal gamete: 24+23=47.
Answer: 47 chromosomes in the body cell, and 24 in the extra gamete.
Two parents affected by an autosomal trait have an unaffected child. What is the inheritance pattern? What is the probability that their next child is unaffected?
Show the solution
  1. If the trait were recessive, both affected parents would be aa and could have only affected children, so an unaffected child means the trait is dominant.
  2. The unaffected child aa took an a from each parent, who are both affected, so each is Aa.
  3. Aa×Aa gives aa in one box out of 4.
Answer: Dominant, and the chance of an unaffected child is 41​.
Common mistakes
  • Reading a shaded shape as a male. Shading means affected; a male is a square.
  • Mixing autosomal recessive with X-linked recessive: both can appear from unaffected parents. Check the sex of the affected: an affected daughter of an unaffected father rules out X-linkage.
  • Treating Down syndrome as a dominant or recessive trait. It comes from an extra chromosome, not an allele.
Summary
  • Square is male, circle is female, shading is affected.
  • Two unaffected parents with an affected child: recessive. Two affected parents with an unaffected child: dominant.
  • An affected daughter with an unaffected father: not X-linked recessive.
  • Down syndrome: an extra copy of chromosome 21, making 47 in total.
