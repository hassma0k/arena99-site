Logic and Reasoning

What you will learn
  • Tell inductive reasoning from deductive reasoning.
  • Find a pattern and predict the next term, and know why this is not a proof.
  • Disprove a false statement with a counterexample.
  • Write the converse, inverse and contrapositive of a conditional statement and judge whether each is true.

Inductive reasoning

We notice a pattern in several examples and guess the general rule. In the sequence 5,9,13,17 each term is 4 more than the one before, so we expect the next term to be 21.

Induction gives a reasonable guess, not a proof: ten examples can agree with a statement and one more example can break it.

Deductive reasoning

We start from accepted facts (definitions, rules, given statements) and move by logical steps to a conclusion that must follow. If the facts are true, the conclusion is always true.

A common form is the law of detachment: if "if the hypothesis holds, the conclusion holds" is true, and the hypothesis really holds, then the conclusion holds.

Another is the law of syllogism: if "if p then q" is true and "if q then r" is true, then "if p then r" is true.

The counterexample

To prove a general statement, examples are never enough however many there are. To disprove it, one example is enough: a case that satisfies the hypothesis and breaks the conclusion. That is a counterexample.

Example: "Every prime number is odd" is false, because 2 is prime and even.

Conditional statements, converse and inverse

A conditional statement has the form "if hypothesis, then conclusion". It is false in exactly one case: the hypothesis holds and the conclusion fails. If the hypothesis does not hold at all, the statement is true, because nobody broke its promise.

From the original statement we form three more:

  • Converse: swap the hypothesis and the conclusion.
  • Inverse: negate both, without swapping.
  • Contrapositive: swap them and negate both.

The contrapositive

The contrapositive is always equivalent to the original statement: it is true when the original is true and false when it is false. The converse and the inverse can be false even when the original is true.

Example: "If a number is a multiple of 6, then it is a multiple of 3" is true. Its converse, "if it is a multiple of 3, then it is a multiple of 6", is false, and the counterexample is 9. Its contrapositive, "if it is not a multiple of 3, then it is not a multiple of 6", is true.

Laws and rules

Conditional statementhypothesis→conclusionThe hypothesis is what we assume; the conclusion is what follows. A statement is disproved only by a case where the hypothesis holds and the conclusion fails.
Contrapositivenot conclusion→not hypothesisAlways has the same truth value as the original hypothesis→conclusion. The converse conclusion→hypothesis and the inverse not hypothesis→not conclusion need not be true with it.

Worked examples

What are the next two numbers in 3,7,11,15,…?
Show the solution
  1. Find the difference between consecutive terms: 7−3=4, 11−7=4, 15−11=4.
  2. The difference is constant, so we expect the pattern to keep adding 4.
  3. Next term: 15+4=19, then 19+4=23.
Answer: 19 and 23
Find a counterexample to: "Every prime number is odd."
Show the solution
  1. A counterexample meets the hypothesis (the number is prime) and breaks the conclusion (it is not odd, so it is even).
  2. The primes are 2,3,5,7. The number 2 is divisible only by 1 and itself, so it is prime.
  3. And 2 is even, so it meets the hypothesis and breaks the conclusion.
Answer: The number 2 is a counterexample, so the statement is false.
The statement: "If a number is a multiple of 6, then it is a multiple of 3." (a) 42 is a multiple of 6; what follows? (b) Write the converse and the contrapositive, and decide whether each is true.
Show the solution
  1. (a) 42=6×7, so the hypothesis holds, and by the law of detachment the conclusion holds: 42 is a multiple of 3 because 42=3×14.
  2. (b) Converse: "if a number is a multiple of 3, then it is a multiple of 6". Test with 9: it is a multiple of 3 but not of 6, so the converse is false.
  3. Contrapositive: "if a number is not a multiple of 3, then it is not a multiple of 6". Every multiple of 6 is certainly a multiple of 3, so there is no counterexample and the statement is true, as expected since it is equivalent to the original.
Answer: (a) 42 is a multiple of 3. (b) The converse is false (counterexample 9); the contrapositive is true.
Common mistakes
  • Students think a pattern seen in three or four examples is proved. Correct thinking: it is only a guess and may fail at the next case.
  • Choosing a counterexample that does not satisfy the hypothesis, such as a number that is not a multiple of 4 when the statement is about multiples of 4. A counterexample must satisfy the hypothesis first and then break the conclusion.
  • Thinking the converse or the inverse is equivalent to the original statement. Only the contrapositive always is; test the converse with an example.
  • Calling a conditional false because its hypothesis is false, such as "if 5 is even, then 1=2". A conditional is false only when the hypothesis holds and the conclusion fails.
Summary
  • Induction: a pattern from examples is a guess, not a proof.
  • Deduction: facts and rules lead to a conclusion that must follow.
  • One counterexample disproves a general statement.
  • A conditional is false only when the hypothesis holds and the conclusion fails.
  • The contrapositive is equivalent to the original; the converse and inverse can be false.

Angles: Parallel Lines, Triangles and Polygons

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Name corresponding, alternate and co-interior angles and use their relations.
  • Use the triangle angle sum 180° and the exterior angle property.
  • Find the angle sum of any polygon and the angle of a regular one.

Angles of parallel lines

When a third line (the transversal) cuts two parallel lines, eight angles appear. Any two of them are either equal or add up to 180°.

  • Corresponding angles: in the same position at each crossing; they are equal.
  • Alternate interior angles: between the lines and on opposite sides of the transversal; they are equal.
  • Co-interior angles: between the lines and on the same side of the transversal; they add up to 180°.
  • Vertical angles are equal, and angles on a straight line add up to 180°.

Angles of a triangle

The interior angles of any triangle add up to 180°. Reason: draw through one vertex a line parallel to the opposite side, and the three angles gather on one straight line.

An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior angles far from it. In an isosceles triangle the base angles are equal, and in an equilateral triangle each angle is 60°.

Angles of a polygon

Drawing diagonals from one vertex splits a polygon with n sides into n−2 triangles, so its interior angles add up to (n−2)×180°. Its exterior angles (one at each vertex) always add up to 360°, whatever the number of sides.

In a regular polygon all angles and sides are equal, so we divide the sum by the number of sides.

  • Triangle: 180°, quadrilateral: 360°
  • Pentagon: 540°, hexagon: 720°

Laws and rules

Triangle angle sumA+B+C=180°A, B, C: the three interior angles.
Exterior angleexterior=remote interior 1+remote interior 2An exterior angle is formed by extending a side, and with its adjacent interior angle it makes 180°.
Polygon angle sumsum=(n−2)×180°n: number of sides.
Regular polygon angleangle=n(n−2)×180°​The exterior angle of a regular polygon is n360°​.

Worked examples

A line cuts two parallel lines. Two alternate interior angles measure (2x+10)° and (3x−20)°. Find x and each angle.
Show the solution
  1. Alternate interior angles are equal: 2x+10=3x−20.
  2. Move terms: 10+20=3x−2x, so x=30.
  3. First angle: 2×30+10=70.
  4. Check: 3×30−20=70, the same value.
Answer: x=30 and each angle is 70°.
In a triangle, the exterior angle at vertex C measures 120° and interior angle A measures 50°. Find interior angle B.
Show the solution
  1. The exterior angle at C equals the sum of the two remote interior angles, A and B.
  2. 120=50+B, so B=70°.
  3. Check: the interior angle at C is 180−120=60°, and 50+70+60=180.
Answer: 70°
Each interior angle of a regular polygon is 150°. How many sides does it have?
Show the solution
  1. Exterior angle =180−150=30°.
  2. Exterior angles always add up to 360°, so the number of sides is 30360​=12.
  3. Check: 12(12−2)×180​=121800​=150.
Answer: Twelve sides.
Common mistakes
  • Equating any two angles that look alike in the picture. Corresponding and alternate angles are equal only if the lines are parallel.
  • Mixing alternate angles (equal) with co-interior angles (sum 180°). Ask: are the two angles on opposite sides of the transversal or on the same side?
  • Using n×180 instead of (n−2)×180. Remember a triangle (n=3) gives 180, not 540.
  • Thinking an exterior angle equals its adjacent interior angle. It actually equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.
Summary
  • Corresponding and alternate interior angles are equal; co-interior angles add to 180° (when lines are parallel).
  • The angles of a triangle add to 180°.
  • Exterior angle = sum of the two remote interior angles.
  • Polygon angle sum = (n − 2) × 180°, and its exterior angles add to 360°.

Triangles: Inequality, Medians and Altitudes

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Decide whether three lengths can form a triangle.
  • Find the possible range of the third side.
  • Use that the centroid divides each median in the ratio 2:1.
  • Tell a median from an altitude.

The triangle inequality

Not every three lengths can be the sides of a triangle: if two sides are too short, their ends cannot meet over the long third side. The rule: the sum of any two sides is greater than the third side.

For a quick test add the two shortest lengths and compare with the longest. The sum must be strictly greater; if it is equal, the three points lie on one line and no triangle forms.

Range of the third side

Given two sides, the third side is greater than their difference and less than their sum. Also, the greatest angle of a triangle is opposite the longest side.

Medians

A median joins a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. A triangle has three medians, and they meet at one point inside the triangle, the centroid (centre of mass); a cardboard triangle balances on a pin at this point.

The centroid divides each median in the ratio 2:1 from the vertex: it is two thirds of the median from the vertex, and one third from the midpoint of the side.

Altitudes

An altitude is drawn from a vertex perpendicular to the opposite side (or its extension). The three altitudes meet at one point: inside an acute triangle, at the right-angle vertex of a right triangle, and outside an obtuse triangle.

We need the altitude to find area: area =21​× base × the altitude drawn to it.

Laws and rules

Triangle inequalityside+side>third sideIt holds for every pair; testing the two shortest against the longest is enough.
Range of the third sidea−b<x<a+ba and b: the two known sides (a the longer), x: the third side.
Centroidfrom vertex=32​×medianand its distance from the midpoint of the side is 31​ of the median.
Triangle areaarea=21​×base×altitudeThe altitude is perpendicular to the chosen base.

Worked examples

Can a triangle have sides (a) 4,6,11 cm? (b) 5,8,12 cm?
Show the solution
  1. Add the two shortest lengths and compare with the longest.
  2. (a) 4+6=10, which is less than 11, so no triangle forms.
  3. (b) 5+8=13, which is greater than 12, so a triangle forms.
Answer: (a) No. (b) Yes.
Two sides of a triangle are 7 cm and 10 cm. Find the range of the third side and its greatest whole-number value.
Show the solution
  1. Difference: 10−7=3; sum: 10+7=17.
  2. So 3<x<17.
  3. The whole numbers run from 4 to 16, and the greatest is 16 because 17 itself is not allowed.
Answer: 3<x<17, and the greatest whole value is 16 cm.
(a) A median is 18 cm long. How far is the centroid from its vertex and from the midpoint of the side? (b) The centroid is 5 cm from the midpoint of the side of another median. How long is that median?
Show the solution
  1. (a) From the vertex: 32​×18=12 cm.
  2. From the midpoint: 31​×18=6 cm, and 12+6=18.
  3. (b) The distance to the midpoint is one third, so the median is 3×5=15 cm.
  4. The centroid is 2×5=10 cm from the vertex.
Answer: (a) 12 cm and 6 cm. (b) 15 cm.
Common mistakes
  • Checking only two sides, or comparing the sum of the two longest lengths with the shortest. The quickest test: the two shortest must add up to more than the longest.
  • Writing ≤ instead of <; if the sum equals the third side, there is no triangle.
  • Reversing the ratio and putting the one third toward the vertex. The bigger part is always on the vertex side.
  • Mixing a median (ends at the midpoint of the side) with an altitude (perpendicular to the side). They do not coincide in general; only in an isosceles triangle does the median to the base coincide with the altitude to the base.
Summary
  • The sum of any two sides is greater than the third.
  • The third side lies between the difference and the sum.
  • Medians meet at a point two thirds of the way from the vertex.
  • An altitude is perpendicular to the side and is used in the area.

Quadrilaterals: Trapezoids and Rhombuses

What you will learn
  • Know the properties of a trapezoid and an isosceles trapezoid.
  • Find the length of the midsegment of a trapezoid.
  • Use the diagonals of a rhombus to find its side, perimeter and area.

The trapezoid

A trapezoid is a quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel sides, called the bases; the other two sides are the legs.

The two angles at the ends of one leg are co-interior, so they add up to 180°.

The midsegment joins the midpoints of the legs; it is parallel to the bases and its length is half the sum of the bases.

The isosceles trapezoid

Its legs are equal, the angles at each base are equal, and its diagonals are equal. If we know one angle, we know all four.

The rhombus

A rhombus is a parallelogram with four equal sides. Opposite angles are equal, and any two consecutive angles add up to 180°.

Its diagonals are perpendicular, bisect each other and bisect its angles. So the diagonals cut the rhombus into four congruent right triangles whose legs are the half-diagonals and whose hypotenuse is the side of the rhombus.

Laws and rules

Midsegment of a trapezoidmidsegment=2base 1+base 2​All lengths in the same unit.
Area of a trapezoidarea=21​×(base 1+base 2)×heightThe height is the perpendicular distance between the bases.
Side of a rhombusside=(2diagonal 1​)2+(2diagonal 2​)2​Pythagoras in the right triangle formed at the centre of the rhombus.
Area of a rhombusarea=21​×diagonal 1×diagonal 2and the perimeter =4× side. The area can also be found as base × height, since a rhombus is a parallelogram.

Worked examples

The bases of a trapezoid are 12 cm and 20 cm and its height is 8 cm. (a) Find its midsegment. (b) Find its area. (c) Another trapezoid has midsegment 15 cm and one base 11 cm; find the other base.
Show the solution
  1. (a) 212+20​=16 cm.
  2. (b) Area =21​×(12+20)×8=128 cm², which is midsegment × height: 16×8=128.
  3. (c) Let the other base be x: 15=211+x​, so 11+x=30 and x=19.
  4. Check: 211+19​=15.
Answer: (a) 16 cm. (b) 128 cm². (c) 19 cm.
A rhombus has diagonals 10 cm and 24 cm. Find its side, perimeter and area.
Show the solution
  1. The diagonals are perpendicular and bisect each other, so the halves are 5 and 12.
  2. Side =52+122​=25+144​=169​=13 cm.
  3. Perimeter =4×13=52 cm.
  4. Area =21​×10×24=120 cm².
Answer: Side 13 cm, perimeter 52 cm, area 120 cm².
In an isosceles trapezoid, one angle at the longer base measures 65°. Find the other angles.
Show the solution
  1. The angles at the longer base are equal, so the other one is 65°.
  2. The bases are parallel, so the angles at one leg are co-interior: 180−65=115°.
  3. The angles at the shorter base are also equal, each 115°.
  4. Check: 65+65+115+115=360.
Answer: 65°, 115° and 115°.
Common mistakes
  • Finding the midsegment by adding the bases and forgetting to divide by 2.
  • Taking a diagonal, or half a diagonal, for a side. The side is the hypotenuse of the right triangle whose legs are the half-diagonals.
  • Forgetting the 21​ in the rhombus area and just multiplying the diagonals.
  • Thinking the diagonals of a rhombus are equal; that is a rectangle. The diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular and usually different in length.
Summary
  • A trapezoid has exactly one pair of parallel sides.
  • Midsegment = half the sum of the bases.
  • Isosceles trapezoid: the angles at each base are equal.
  • Rhombus: four equal sides, diagonals perpendicular and bisecting each other.

Inscribed Angles in a Circle

What you will learn
  • Tell a central angle from an inscribed angle.
  • Find the inscribed angle from the arc or the central angle.
  • Use that an angle in a semicircle is a right angle.

Central angle and arc

A central angle has its vertex at the centre of the circle and two radii as sides. The arc it cuts off has the same measure as the angle, and the whole circle is 360°.

Inscribed angle

An inscribed angle has its vertex on the circle and two chords as sides. It intercepts the arc between its sides (the arc that does not contain the vertex).

An inscribed angle is half the arc it intercepts, that is, half the central angle on the same arc.

Useful results

All inscribed angles that intercept the same arc are equal.

The reason for a cyclic quadrilateral: each of two opposite angles is an inscribed angle on an arc, the two arcs together make the whole circle, 360°, and half of 360° is 180°.

  • An inscribed angle in a semicircle (intercepting an arc of 180°) is a right angle: 90°.
  • In a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle, opposite angles add up to 180°.

Laws and rules

Inscribed angleinscribed=21​×central=21​×arcThe central and inscribed angles intercept the same arc.
Semicircleinscribed angle in a semicircle=90°The intercepted arc is a diameter.
Cyclic quadrilateralangle+opposite angle=180°In a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle, for each pair of opposite angles.

Worked examples

(a) A central angle measures 84°. What is the inscribed angle that intercepts the same arc? (b) An inscribed angle measures 35°. What is the arc it intercepts, and what is the rest of the circle?
Show the solution
  1. (a) The inscribed angle is half the central angle on the same arc: 84÷2=42.
  2. (b) The arc is twice the inscribed angle: 2×35=70°.
  3. The circle is 360°, so the rest is 360−70=290°.
Answer: (a) 42°. (b) 70° and 290°.
In a circle, AB is a diameter, C is a point on the circle, and angle CAB measures 35°. Find angle ACB and angle ABC.
Show the solution
  1. Angle ACB is an inscribed angle facing the diameter AB, so it intercepts an arc of 180° and measures 90°.
  2. The angles of a triangle add up to 180°: angle ABC =180−90−35=55°.
  3. Check: 35+55+90=180.
Answer: Angle ACB =90° and angle ABC =55°.
Quadrilateral ABCD is inscribed in a circle, with angle A =85° and angle B =70°. Find angles C and D.
Show the solution
  1. Angles A and C are opposite in a cyclic quadrilateral, so they add up to 180°: C =180−85=95°.
  2. Angles B and D are opposite: D =180−70=110°.
  3. Check: the angles of a quadrilateral add up to 85+70+95+110=360.
Answer: Angle C =95° and angle D =110°.
Common mistakes
  • Making the inscribed angle equal to the central angle. It is always half of it.
  • Taking the wrong arc (the one containing the vertex) or the larger arc. The inscribed angle intercepts the arc facing it between its sides.
  • Thinking an angle in a semicircle is 180°. It is 90° because the arc is 180° and half of that is 90°.
  • In a cyclic quadrilateral, adding two neighbouring angles to get 180°. Only the opposite angles add up to 180°.
Summary
  • Arc = the central angle that cuts it.
  • Inscribed = half the arc = half the central angle.
  • Inscribed angles on the same arc are equal.
  • An angle in a semicircle is a right angle.
  • In a cyclic quadrilateral, opposite angles add up to 180°.

Slope, Line Equations, Midpoint and Distance

What you will learn
  • Find the slope of the line through two points and understand it.
  • Write a line equation in slope-intercept form.
  • Find the midpoint of a segment and the distance between two points.

Slope of a line

Slope measures steepness: how far we go up (or down) for each unit we move across. It is the rise divided by the run.

A positive slope means the line rises from left to right, a negative slope means it falls, zero means it is horizontal, and a vertical line has undefined slope.

Parallel lines have the same slope, and perpendicular lines have slopes whose product is −1.

Equation of a line

In slope-intercept form, y=mx+b, where m is the slope and b is the y-intercept (the value of y when x=0).

If we know the slope and one point (x1​,y1​) on the line, we use y−y1​=m(x−x1​) and simplify.

Midpoint and distance

The midpoint of a segment is the average of the coordinates: add the x-values and halve, add the y-values and halve.

The distance between two points comes from Pythagoras: the difference in x and the difference in y are the legs of a right triangle, and the distance is the hypotenuse.

Laws and rules

Slopem=x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​Points (x1​,y1​) and (x2​,y2​); keep the same order of subtraction on top and bottom.
Slope-intercept formy=mx+bm: slope, b: y-intercept.
Midpoint(2x1​+x2​​, 2y1​+y2​​)The average of the x-values and the average of the y-values.
Distanced=(x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​From Pythagoras.

Worked examples

Find the slope of the line through (−2,5) and (4,−1).
Show the solution
  1. Rise: −1−5=−6.
  2. Run: 4−(−2)=6.
  3. Slope =6−6​=−1, so the line falls.
Answer: −1
(a) Write the equation of the line through (1,5) and (3,11). (b) Write the equation of the line perpendicular to it through (6,1).
Show the solution
  1. (a) Slope: 3−111−5​=26​=3.
  2. Substitute (1,5) in y=3x+b: 5=3+b, so b=2 and the equation is y=3x+2. Check with (3,11): 3×3+2=11.
  3. (b) Perpendicular slopes multiply to −1, so the perpendicular slope is −31​ because 3×(−31​)=−1.
  4. Substitute (6,1) in y=−31​x+b: 1=−2+b, so b=3.
Answer: (a) y=3x+2. (b) y=−31​x+3.
The midpoint of a segment is (4,6) and one endpoint is (1,2). Find the other endpoint and the length of the segment.
Show the solution
  1. Let the other endpoint be (x,y): 21+x​=4, so 1+x=8 and x=7.
  2. 22+y​=6, so 2+y=12 and y=10.
  3. The other endpoint is (7,10).
  4. Length =(7−1)2+(10−2)2​=36+64​=100​=10.
Answer: The other endpoint is (7,10) and the length is 10.
Common mistakes
  • Dividing the wrong way (run over rise) or subtracting x from y. Remember: slope = change in y divided by change in x.
  • Reversing the order of subtraction on top but not at the bottom, which flips the sign of the slope. Use one order for both.
  • Mixing zero slope (horizontal line, y= constant) with undefined slope (vertical line, x= constant).
  • Adding the coordinates for the midpoint without halving, or forgetting the square root in the distance.
Summary
  • Slope = rise ÷ run = change in y ÷ change in x.
  • Parallel lines share a slope; perpendicular slopes multiply to −1.
  • y = mx + b: m is the slope and b the y-intercept.
  • Midpoint = average of coordinates; distance = square root of the sum of squared differences.

Linear Models, Ratios and Rates

What you will learn
  • Write a linear model from a word problem and solve it.
  • Work with ratios, percentages and percent change.
  • Find a rate and a unit rate.

The linear model

Many everyday situations are linear: one amount paid once and another amount repeated with every unit. We write this as y=mx+b.

The slope m is the rate of change: what is added for each unit (riyals per day). The intercept b is the starting value when x is zero (the fixed fee).

  • Decide what x is and what y measures.
  • Find what repeats (m) and what is paid once (b).
  • Write y=mx+b, then substitute and solve.
  • If two points on the line are given, find the slope first, then find b by substitution.

Ratios and percentages

A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind: a:b means ba​. To share an amount in a given ratio, add the parts and find the value of one part.

A percentage is a ratio with denominator 100: percentage =wholepart​×100. Percent change is the change compared with the original (old) value, not the new one.

Rates

A rate compares two quantities of different kinds, such as kilometres per hour or riyals per kilogram. A unit rate is the rate for one unit of the denominator.

To solve rate problems, multiply the unit rate by the number of units needed.

Laws and rules

Linear modely=mx+bm: the rate (increase per unit), b: the starting value or fixed fee.
Percentagepercentage=wholepart​×100To find a part of a number: part =percentage×whole÷100.
Percent changepercent change=oldnew−old​×100Positive for an increase, negative for a decrease.
Speed (a rate)speed=timedistance​For example km/h.
Unit rateunit rate=second quantityfirst quantity​For example riyals per kilogram: divide the price by the mass.

Worked examples

A taxi charges a fixed 10 riyals plus 3 riyals per kilometre. Write the linear model and find the cost of 12 km.
Show the solution
  1. Let x be the kilometres and y the cost.
  2. The 3 repeats with each kilometre, so it is the slope; the 10 is paid once, so it is the intercept: y=3x+10.
  3. Substitute x=12: y=3×12+10=36+10=46.
Answer: y=3x+10 and the cost is 46 riyals.
A gym charges 100 riyals to register and 40 riyals per month. Munira paid 340 riyals in total. For how many months did she subscribe?
Show the solution
  1. The model: y=40x+100.
  2. Put y=340: 340=40x+100.
  3. Subtract 100: 240=40x.
  4. Divide by 40: x=6.
  5. Check: 40×6+100=340.
Answer: 6 months.
(a) A class has 40 students, and the ratio of boys to girls is 3:5. How many girls? (b) A price rises from 80 riyals to 100 riyals. What is the percent increase? (c) A car travels 150 km in 2.5 hours. What is its speed, and how far does it go in 4 hours?
Show the solution
  1. (a) Parts: 3+5=8; one part is 40÷8=5 students. Girls: 5×5=25.
  2. (b) Increase 100−80=20, compared with the old price: 8020​×100=25%.
  3. (c) Speed =2.5150​=60 km/h.
  4. In 4 hours: 60×4=240 km.
Answer: (a) 25 girls. (b) 25%. (c) 60 km/h, and 240 km.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing the fixed fee with the rate and multiplying the fixed fee by x. What repeats with every unit is multiplied by x; what is paid once is added.
  • Dividing by m before subtracting the starting value b. Subtract b first, then divide by m.
  • Computing percent change against the new value. The denominator is always the old value.
  • Dividing the amount by one part of the ratio instead of the total of the parts.
Summary
  • y=mx+b: m is the rate of change and b the starting value.
  • To solve a model: substitute, remove the constant, divide by m.
  • Percentage = part ÷ whole × 100; percent change uses the old value as denominator.
  • A rate compares different kinds of quantity; speed = distance ÷ time.

Transformations and Similarity

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Apply translations, reflections and dilations to points in the coordinate plane.
  • Compose two transformations in the right order.
  • Use similarity and the scale factor for lengths, perimeter and area.

Transformations and translation

A transformation maps each point of a figure to a new point called its image. Translations and reflections keep shape and size (the image is congruent to the original); a dilation changes the size and keeps the shape.

A translation moves every point the same distance in the same direction. Moving right or left changes the x-coordinate; moving up or down changes the y-coordinate.

Right and up add; left and down subtract. Example: a translation of 3 right and 2 down maps (x,y) to (x+3, y−2).

Reflection

A reflection flips a figure over a line, like a mirror: a point and its image are at equal distances from the line of reflection, and the segment joining them is perpendicular to it.

  • Over the x-axis: (x,y)→(x, −y)
  • Over the y-axis: (x,y)→(−x, y)
  • Over the line y=x: (x,y)→(y, x)

Dilation

A dilation from the origin with scale factor k multiplies both coordinates of each point by k: (x,y)→(kx, ky). If k>1 the figure grows, and if 0<k<1 it shrinks.

The image keeps the same angles, and every length in it is the original length times k; so a figure and its image are similar.

Composition of transformations

When two transformations are applied one after the other, we take the result of the first and apply the second to it. Order matters: changing it can give a different point.

Similar polygons

Two polygons are similar if their corresponding angles are equal and their corresponding sides are proportional. The ratio of any two corresponding sides is the scale factor k.

To find an unknown side we write a proportion and cross-multiply. The perimeters are in the ratio k and the areas in the ratio k2.

Warning: for polygons with more than three sides one condition alone is not enough: two rectangles can have equal angles without being similar, and two rhombuses can have proportional sides without being similar.

Worked examples

What is the image of (−3,4) under the translation (x+5, y−6)?
Show the solution
  1. Add 5 to the x-coordinate: −3+5=2.
  2. Subtract 6 from the y-coordinate: 4−6=−2.
Answer: (2,−2)
The point (4,−2) is reflected over the x-axis, then the result is dilated from the origin with factor 3. What is the final image?
Show the solution
  1. Reflection over the x-axis changes the sign of y: (4,−2)→(4,2).
  2. The dilation then multiplies each coordinate by 3: (4×3, 2×3)=(12,6).
  3. Always follow the order given: the second transformation acts on the result of the first, never on the original point.
Answer: (12,6)
Two similar polygons: a side of 12 cm in the larger corresponds to a side of 8 cm in the smaller. The smaller has perimeter 20 cm and area 36 cm². Find the scale factor, then the perimeter and area of the larger.
Show the solution
  1. The scale factor from the corresponding sides (larger to smaller): k=812​=23​.
  2. Perimeter scales by k: 20×23​=30 cm.
  3. Area scales by k2=49​: 36×49​=81 cm².
  4. Check: 36÷4=9, then 9×9=81.
Answer: k=23​, perimeter 30 cm and area 81 cm².
Common mistakes
  • In a reflection, changing the sign of the wrong coordinate. Reflection over the x-axis changes y; over the y-axis changes x.
  • In a dilation, multiplying only one coordinate. Multiply both.
  • In a composition, applying the second transformation to the original point instead of to the result of the first.
  • Multiplying the area by k instead of k2. Lengths and perimeter scale by k; area by k2.
  • Finding the scale factor upside down. k = image length ÷ original length (or image coordinate ÷ original coordinate).
Summary
  • A translation adds to the coordinates; a reflection changes the sign of one.
  • A dilation from the origin multiplies both coordinates by k.
  • In a composition: first one first, then the second on its result.
  • For similar figures: perimeter × k, area × k².
