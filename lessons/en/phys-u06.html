Wave Types and How Waves Travel

What you will learn
  • Tell transverse waves from longitudinal waves
  • Understand how the medium's particles vibrate and why they do not travel with the wave
  • Know that sound needs a medium
  • Know what changes and what stays fixed when a wave enters another medium

What is a wave?

A wave is a disturbance that travels through a medium and carries energy from one place to another. The medium itself does not travel: each particle vibrates about its own position and returns to it.

A cork on the water rises and falls as a wave passes, but it does not ride the wave to the shore. The wave moved the energy, not the water.

Transverse or longitudinal?

The deciding question: in which direction do the particles vibrate compared with the direction the wave travels?

  • Transverse wave: the vibration is perpendicular to the travel direction. It looks like crests and troughs, like a shaken rope or water surface.
  • Longitudinal wave: the vibration is parallel to the travel direction. It looks like compressions (particles close together) and rarefactions (particles spread apart), like sound or a spring pushed and pulled along its length.

Sound needs a medium

Sound is a mechanical longitudinal wave: it travels because particles push their neighbours. So it needs matter (air, water, metal) and cannot travel through a vacuum.

Light is an electromagnetic wave and needs no medium, which is why we see the Sun but hear nothing from it.

What sets the wave speed?

The speed of a wave is set by the properties of the medium (its stiffness and density), not by the source. Sound is faster in water than in air, and faster still in steel. In air, the speed of sound rises slightly as the temperature rises.

The frequency is set by the source alone, so it stays the same when the wave crosses the boundary between two media. Since speed equals frequency times wavelength, a change in speed changes the wavelength by the same ratio.

Laws and rules

Crossing a boundaryf=λv​Frequency f (Hz) stays constant; speed v (m/s) is set by the medium; if v increases, the wavelength λ (m) increases by the same ratio.

Worked examples

A cork floats on a pond and a water wave passes it. How does the cork move? Does the wave carry it to the shore?
Show the solution
  1. A water surface wave is a transverse wave.
  2. The water particles, and the cork with them, vibrate perpendicular to the travel direction: up, then down.
  3. The wave carries energy, not matter, so the cork returns to its place.
Answer: It moves up and down about its position and does not travel with the wave.
State the type of wave in each case: (a) moving the end of a rope up and down, (b) pushing and pulling the end of a spring along its length, (c) sound in water.
Show the solution
  1. (a) The vibration is perpendicular to the rope: transverse.
  2. (b) The vibration is along the spring: longitudinal, with compressions and rarefactions.
  3. (c) Sound in any medium is a longitudinal wave, and it needs a medium (water here).
Answer: (a) transverse, (b) longitudinal, (c) longitudinal.
A wave has speed 20m/s and frequency 10Hz in a first medium. It enters a second medium where its speed is 30m/s. Find its frequency and wavelength in the second medium.
Show the solution
  1. Wavelength in the first medium (for comparison): λ=1020​=2m.
  2. The source sets the frequency, so it does not change: 10Hz.
  3. New wavelength: λ=1030​=3m.
  4. Check: the speed grew from 20 to 30 (by 1.5 times) and the wavelength grew from 2 to 3 by the same ratio.
Answer: Frequency 10Hz, wavelength 3m.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the wave carries the medium's particles along. In fact the particles vibrate in place; what travels is energy.
  • Saying sound is transverse because its graph is wavy. The graph shows pressure or displacement; sound in air is always longitudinal.
  • Saying the frequency changes in a new medium. The frequency comes from the source and stays fixed; speed and wavelength change.
  • Believing sound can travel through empty space. No material medium means no sound.
Summary
  • A wave carries energy, not matter.
  • Transverse: vibration perpendicular to travel. Longitudinal: parallel to it.
  • Sound is a longitudinal wave and needs a medium.
  • The medium sets the speed; the source sets the frequency.

Wave Properties: Frequency, Period and Wavelength

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Identify amplitude and wavelength on a wave drawing
  • Convert between frequency and period
  • Calculate wave speed, wavelength or frequency
  • Read the wavelength from a count of crests and the distance between them

Four quantities describe a wave

Every periodic wave is described by four quantities that keep returning in questions:

  • Amplitude: the largest displacement of a particle from its rest position (measured from the rest line to a crest, not from trough to crest).
  • Wavelength: the length of one full wave, the distance between two successive crests or two successive troughs (m).
  • Frequency: the number of full waves passing a point each second (Hz).
  • Period: the time one full wave takes to pass the point (s).

Frequency and period are reciprocals

If 5 waves pass every second, each wave takes one fifth of a second. So the period is the reciprocal of the frequency, and the frequency is the reciprocal of the period.

A period of 0.02s means a frequency of 0.021​=50Hz.

Wave speed

In one period the wave advances one wavelength. Speed = distance ÷ time = wavelength ÷ period. Because 1 ÷ period is the frequency, speed = frequency × wavelength.

This one relation gives everything: knowing two of (speed, frequency, wavelength) gives the third. It works for water too: a water wave of speed 3m/s and frequency 1.5Hz has wavelength 1.53​=2m.

In one medium the speed stays constant, so if the frequency rises the wavelength gets shorter (an inverse relation). In a longitudinal wave the wavelength is the distance between two successive compressions.

Reading the drawing

When counting on a drawing, be careful: between the first and last crest the number of wavelengths is the number of crests minus one. Five crests mean four wavelengths.

Amplitude tells the energy and the height of the wave. It changes neither the speed nor the frequency.

Laws and rules

Frequency and periodf=T1​f is the frequency in Hz; T is the period in s.
Wave speedv=fλv is the speed in m/s; f the frequency in Hz; λ the wavelength in m.
Wavelengthλ=fv​λ is the wavelength in m.

Worked examples

The period of a wave is 0.02s. What is its frequency?
Show the solution
  1. We want the frequency; we know the period.
  2. f=0.021​.
  3. 0.021​=2100​=50.
Answer: 50Hz.
A sound wave has speed 340m/s and frequency 680Hz. What is its wavelength?
Show the solution
  1. λ=fv​.
  2. 680340​=21​=0.5.
  3. Check: 680×0.5=340m/s, the given speed.
Answer: 0.5m.
Five successive crests appear on water, and the distance from the first to the last is 12m. The wave frequency is 2Hz. Find the wavelength, speed and period.
Show the solution
  1. Five crests mean 4 wavelengths between the first and the last.
  2. λ=412​=3m.
  3. v=2×3=6m/s.
  4. T=21​=0.5s.
  5. Check: wavelength ÷ period =0.53​=6m/s.
Answer: Wavelength 3m, speed 6m/s, period 0.5s.
Common mistakes
  • Dividing the distance by the number of crests. Divide by the number of crests minus one: we count gaps, not crests.
  • Taking the crest-to-trough distance as the wavelength. That is only half a wavelength.
  • Thinking a bigger amplitude means a faster wave. The medium sets the speed; amplitude relates to energy.
  • Forgetting unit conversions: centimetres to metres, milliseconds to seconds, before substituting.
Summary
  • Frequency is the reciprocal of the period: f=T1​.
  • Speed = frequency × wavelength.
  • Wavelength: between successive crests; amplitude: from rest line to crest.
  • Wavelengths between crests = number of crests - 1.

Hooke's Law, Springs and Pendulums

What you will learn
  • Apply Hooke's law to a stretched spring
  • Know what does and does not affect the period of a spring and a pendulum
  • Predict how the period changes when length or mass changes
  • Find a pendulum's length from its period

Hooke's law

A stretched spring pulls back with a force that tries to restore its original length. Within its elastic limit this force is directly proportional to the extension: double the extension and the force doubles.

The spring constant measures stiffness (newtons per metre); a stiff spring has a large constant. Note that the unit uses metres, so convert centimetres to metres before substituting.

The restoring force opposes the extension, which is why it is sometimes written with a negative sign; in calculations we take its magnitude. On a graph of force against extension, the slope of the straight line equals the spring constant.

Restoring force and oscillation

When a mass or bob is displaced from equilibrium, a force pulls it back. It overshoots, returns, and so on: a repeating vibration with a steady period.

In a mass-spring system: a larger mass is slower (longer period) and a stiffer spring is faster (shorter period). The period follows the square root of the mass: a spring with a 2s period carrying 1kg gets a 4s period if the mass becomes 4kg. The period does not change with amplitude or with gravity.

The simple pendulum

A pendulum is a bob on a string swinging through a small angle. Its period depends on two things only: the string length and the gravitational acceleration g.

The bob's mass and material do not matter, and a small amplitude hardly changes the period. A longer string increases the period, but through a square root: four times the length gives only twice the period.

If the pendulum is taken to the Moon, where gravity is weaker, its period gets longer.

Finding the length from the period

From the pendulum law, square both sides and isolate the length. A pendulum with a 2 s period is about 1 m long (with g=10 and π2≈10), the well-known seconds pendulum.

Laws and rules

Hooke's lawF=kxF is the force in N; k the spring constant in N/m; x the extension in m.
Pendulum periodT=2πgL​​T in s; L the length in m; g the gravitational acceleration in m/s² (about 10).
Mass-spring periodT=2πkm​​m is the mass in kg; k the spring constant in N/m.
Pendulum lengthL=4π2gT2​Obtained by squaring the pendulum law and isolating L.

Worked examples

A spring with constant 200N/m is stretched 5cm. What is the pulling force?
Show the solution
  1. Convert the extension to metres: 5cm=1005​=0.05m.
  2. F=200×0.05.
  3. 200×0.05=10.
Answer: 10N.
A simple pendulum has a period of 2s. If its string becomes four times as long, what is the new period?
Show the solution
  1. T=2πgL​​ and the length is under the root.
  2. Multiplying L by 4 multiplies T by 4​=2.
  3. New period =2×2=4s.
  4. Check: the common wrong answer is 8 s, from multiplying T by 4; the period follows the square root of the length.
Answer: 4s.
Take g=10m/s2 and π2≈10. A simple pendulum has a period of 2s. What is its length?
Show the solution
  1. Start from T=2πgL​​.
  2. Square both sides: T2=4π2×gL​.
  3. Isolate L: L=4π2gT2​.
  4. Substitute: L=4×1010×4​=4040​=1.
Answer: 1m.
Common mistakes
  • Using centimetres in Hooke's law without converting to metres; the answer comes out 100 times too large.
  • Believing the bob's mass changes the period. Mass does not appear in the pendulum law.
  • Thinking the period is proportional to the length. It is proportional to the square root of the length, so four times the length gives twice the period.
  • Thinking a heavier mass on a spring oscillates faster. A heavier mass is slower, so the period is longer.
Summary
  • Spring force = spring constant × extension (in metres).
  • A pendulum's period depends only on length and g, not on mass.
  • The period follows the square root of the length (and of the mass for a spring).
  • Pendulum length L=4π2gT2​.

Sound: Echoes, Thunder, Pitch, Loudness and Decibels

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Calculate echo distance and echo time, and tell it apart from the thunder problem
  • Distinguish pitch from loudness
  • Calculate the change in decibel level from an intensity ratio
  • Find the intensity ratio from a change in decibels

Speed of sound and distance

The speed of sound in air is about 340m/s. As for any uniform motion: distance = speed × time.

Echo: the distance is covered twice

An echo is sound reflected from an obstacle (a mountain or wall). The sound goes to the obstacle and comes back to you, so the total distance it travels is twice the obstacle's distance.

So: obstacle distance = speed × time ÷ 2, and the time between the shout and hearing the echo = 2 × distance ÷ speed.

The ear can tell an echo from the original sound if about 0.1s or more passes between them, so the obstacle must be about 2340×0.1​=17m away or more.

Thunder and lightning: the distance is covered once

Light reaches us almost instantly, while the sound of thunder starts its trip with the flash and reaches your ear later. Here the sound covers the distance only once, so there is no division by two: distance = speed × the time between seeing the flash and hearing the thunder.

Pitch and loudness

Pitch (high or low note) is set by the frequency: a higher frequency gives a higher pitch. Loudness (loud or quiet) is set by the amplitude and intensity: a larger amplitude gives a louder sound.

Changing the pitch does not change the loudness, and the reverse is also true.

Decibel level

The ear hears an enormous range of intensities, so we measure it on a logarithmic scale relative to the faintest sound the ear can hear (the hearing threshold, 0dB; a whisper is about 30, normal conversation about 60, and pain starts near 120). Each time the intensity is multiplied by 10, the level rises by 10 dB: ×10 means +10, ×100 means +20, ×1000 means +30.

For intensity doubled: change =10log2≈3dB. Doubling the intensity does not double the decibels: two identical speakers at 60 dB give about 63 dB, not 120.

Laws and rules

Distance, speed, timed=vtd in m; v in m/s; t in s. Used directly for thunder and lightning.
Obstacle distance in an echod=2vt​t is the time between the shout and hearing the echo.
Change in sound levelΔβ=10logI1​I2​​The change is in dB; I₁ and I₂ are the old and new intensities. Use log 10 = 1, log 100 = 2, log 1000 = 3, log 2 ≈ 0.3.
Sound levelβ=10logI0​I​β is the level in dB; I is the intensity; I₀ = 10−12W/m2 is the hearing threshold.

Worked examples

Take the speed of sound as 340m/s. (a) A hunter shouts in front of a mountain and hears the echo after 2s. How far is the mountain? (b) How long until you hear the echo of your voice from a wall 170m away?
Show the solution
  1. (a) Total distance =340×2=680m.
  2. This is there and back, so the mountain is 2680​=340m away.
  3. (b) Total distance =2×170=340m.
  4. Time =340340​=1s.
Answer: (a) 340m, (b) 1s.
You see lightning and hear the thunder 1.5s later. Light is instant and the speed of sound is 340m/s. How far is the lightning?
Show the solution
  1. The sound travels from the lightning to your ear once, so we do not divide by 2.
  2. d=340×1.5.
  3. 340×1.5=340+170=510.
Answer: 510m.
(a) A sound's intensity becomes 100 times larger. By how many dB does its level rise? (b) Take log2≈0.3. A speaker gives 60dB and an identical speaker is switched on with it. What is the new level? (c) The level rises by 30dB. By what factor did the intensity grow?
Show the solution
  1. (a) Change =10log100=10×2=20dB.
  2. (b) The intensity doubled: change ≈10×0.3=3dB.
  3. New level ≈60+3=63dB.
  4. (c) 30=10log(ratio), so log(ratio)=3.
  5. So the ratio =103=1000.
Answer: (a) 20dB, (b) about 63dB, (c) 1000 times.
Common mistakes
  • Dividing the thunder problem by 2. The sound travels from the lightning to your ear only once.
  • Forgetting to divide by 2 in an echo. The measured time includes the trip there and back.
  • Mixing pitch with loudness: pitch comes from frequency, loudness from amplitude and intensity.
  • Thinking that doubling the intensity doubles the decibels. In fact the decibel is the logarithm of the ratio: doubling the intensity adds only 3 dB, and multiplying it by 10 adds 10.
Summary
  • Distance = speed × time.
  • Echo: divide by 2 because the sound goes and returns. Thunder: do not divide.
  • Pitch comes from frequency; loudness from amplitude.
  • Change in dB =10log (intensity ratio): ×10 means +10, ×2 means about +3.

The Doppler Effect

What you will learn
  • Describe what the Doppler effect means
  • Explain its cause through crests bunching and spreading
  • Decide the heard frequency when approaching, passing and receding

What is the effect?

The Doppler effect is the change in the frequency the observer receives when there is relative motion between the source and the observer. The famous example: an ambulance siren sounds higher as it approaches and lower as it leaves.

Relative motion is enough: the source, the observer, or both may move at different speeds.

Why does the heard frequency change?

The source emits wave crests at a steady rate. But if it moves toward the observer, it keeps catching up with the crests it emitted a moment ago, so crests bunch up ahead of it: the wavelength is shorter and more crests reach the observer each second, giving a higher frequency and a higher pitch.

Behind the moving source the crests spread apart, so the wavelength is longer and fewer crests reach the observer each second: a lower frequency and a lower pitch.

Key point: the source did not change its vibration frequency, and the speed of sound did not change (the medium sets it). What changed is the number of crests reaching the observer each second.

At the moment of passing

An ambulance approaches a standing person, passes, and moves away: while approaching, the person hears a frequency higher than the siren's true one; at the moment of passing the heard frequency drops suddenly; then a lower frequency is heard while it recedes.

If you run toward a fixed loudspeaker you hear a higher frequency, and if you run away you hear a lower one.

Worked examples

An ambulance sounds its siren at a steady frequency. Describe the frequency heard by a person standing on the pavement as it approaches, passes and recedes.
Show the solution
  1. While approaching: the crests bunch up, so a frequency higher than the true one is heard.
  2. At passing: the heard frequency drops suddenly.
  3. While receding: the crests spread out, so a frequency lower than the true one is heard.
Answer: Higher while approaching, a sudden drop at passing, lower while receding.
A student says: "When the siren approaches, the vibration frequency of its source increases." Correct the statement.
Show the solution
  1. The source vibrates at a fixed frequency that its motion does not change.
  2. What changes is the frequency the observer receives, because of the relative motion.
  3. The reason: crests bunch up ahead of the moving source, so more crests reach the observer every second.
Answer: The source frequency is constant; the heard frequency is what changes.
Two cars travel at the same speed in the same direction, and the front car sounds its horn. Does the frequency heard by the driver of the rear car change?
Show the solution
  1. The distance between the cars stays constant, so there is no relative motion between source and observer.
  2. The crests neither bunch up nor spread out at the observer.
Answer: No change: the driver hears the horn's true frequency.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the source itself changes its frequency. In fact the emitted frequency is fixed; the heard one changes.
  • Thinking the speed of sound grows as the source approaches. The medium sets the speed and it does not change.
  • Forgetting that the frequency drops at the moment of passing: higher before, lower after.
  • Thinking the effect is only for sound. It happens for all waves, including light.
Summary
  • Doppler: the received frequency changes because of relative motion.
  • Approaching gives a higher frequency; receding gives a lower one.
  • Cause: crests bunch up ahead of a moving source and spread out behind it.
  • The source frequency and the speed of sound do not change.

Superposition and Beats

What you will learn
  • Apply the superposition principle to two meeting waves
  • Tell constructive from destructive interference
  • Calculate the beat frequency of two close frequencies
  • Find an unknown tuning fork's frequency using beats

The superposition principle

When two waves meet in the same place, the displacement of the medium at each instant is the sum of the two displacements, keeping the sign (a crest is positive, a trough negative). After meeting, each wave carries on unaffected.

Constructive and destructive

Constructive overlap: crest with crest (or trough with trough) makes a larger amplitude: crests of 3 and 2 give 5.

Destructive overlap: crest with trough reduces or cancels the amplitude: 3 with -2 gives 1, and two equal opposite pulses give zero at the instant they meet. The waves are not destroyed: they separate afterwards and each continues on its way.

Beats

If two tuning forks with very close frequencies sound together, their sounds overlap: sometimes the crests of the two meet and the sound grows loud (constructive), and sometimes a crest of one meets a trough of the other and the sound fades (destructive). So the heard sound alternates between loud and soft, and each loud-soft cycle is one beat.

The number of beats per second equals the difference between the larger and smaller frequency. Forks of 256 Hz and 260 Hz give 4 beats per second.

Finding an unknown fork

If the beats with a known 440 Hz fork are 3 per second, the unknown fork is either 443 Hz or 437 Hz. To decide, change the unknown fork slightly: a little wax lowers its frequency.

If the beats increase after the wax, the unknown was lower than the known fork (it moves further away). If they decrease, the unknown was higher (it moves closer).

Laws and rules

Superpositiony=y1​+y2​Displacements are added with their sign: a crest is positive, a trough negative.
Beat frequencyfbeat​=fhigh​−flow​In Hz, which is the number of beats per second.

Worked examples

Two crests of amplitudes 3cm and 2cm meet. What is the maximum displacement at the moment they meet? What if the second is a trough of 2cm?
Show the solution
  1. Crest with crest: 3+2=5cm.
  2. Crest with trough: 3+(−2)=1cm.
Answer: 5cm, then 1cm.
Two tuning forks of 256Hz and 260Hz sound together. What is the beat frequency?
Show the solution
  1. Beat frequency = larger frequency - smaller frequency.
  2. 260−256=4.
  3. Check: it is not the average 258, nor the sum 516.
Answer: 4Hz, that is 4 beats per second.
A known 440Hz fork and an unknown fork give 3 beats per second. After a little wax is put on the unknown fork (which lowers its frequency slightly), the beats become 5 per second. What was the unknown fork's original frequency?
Show the solution
  1. A difference of 3 means the unknown is 443 or 437 Hz.
  2. If it were 443, lowering it would bring it closer to 440 and the beats would decrease.
  3. If it were 437, lowering it moves it further from 440 and the beats increase, which is what happened.
  4. Check: after the wax it is 435 Hz and 440−435=5.
Answer: 437Hz.
Common mistakes
  • Finding the beat frequency by averaging or adding. The correct way is the difference between the larger and smaller frequency.
  • Thinking the waves destroy each other in destructive overlap. They pass through each other and continue unchanged.
  • Taking one solution for an unknown fork: there are two possibilities, and we need the wax or file test to choose.
  • Forgetting the negative sign of a trough when adding displacements.
Summary
  • Resultant displacement = sum of the two displacements, with sign.
  • Crest + crest increases; crest + trough reduces or cancels.
  • Beat frequency = difference between the two frequencies.
  • An unknown fork has two answers; a small change to its frequency picks the right one.

Standing Waves and Resonance in Strings and Pipes

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Understand nodes and antinodes in a standing wave
  • Calculate the fundamental and harmonics of a string fixed at both ends
  • Calculate the frequencies of an open pipe
  • Calculate the frequencies of a closed pipe and know its harmonics are odd only

A standing wave

When a wave reflects in a string or pipe and meets itself, the two opposite waves overlap and form a standing wave that stays in place. It has points that never move, called nodes, and points of maximum vibration, called antinodes.

The distance between two successive nodes (or antinodes) is half a wavelength.

A string fixed at both ends

Both ends are fixed, so each is a node. The simplest pattern (the fundamental frequency, the first harmonic) has one antinode between two nodes, so the string length is half a wavelength.

Harmonic number n has n antinodes and n+1 nodes (including the ends), and its frequency is n times the fundamental. If the fundamental is 150 Hz, the third harmonic is 450 Hz; counting starts at 1, not at zero.

A guitar string that is shorter, tighter or lighter gives a higher fundamental frequency, that is, a higher pitch.

A pipe open at both ends

An antinode forms at each open end. The simplest pattern has two antinodes with a node between them, which is half a wave: λ=2L. Its rules match the string: every harmonic is present, 1,2,3…

An open pipe 1 m long with the speed of sound 340 m/s: the wavelength is 2 m and the fundamental is 170 Hz.

A pipe closed at one end

The closed end is a node and the open end is an antinode. The simplest pattern is a quarter wave: λ=4L and the fundamental is 4Lv​.

This arrangement allows only odd quarters: 4λ​, then 43λ​, then 45λ​. So its harmonics are odd only: 1, 3, 5, with frequencies 1, 3 and 5 times the fundamental, and there is no second harmonic.

Resonance

Resonance is a large-amplitude response that occurs when the source frequency equals one of the natural frequencies of the string or pipe, so the sound is heard louder.

Laws and rules

Harmonics of a string fixed at both endsfn​=nf1​n = 1, 2, 3 ...; frequencies in Hz.
Pipe open at both endsf1​=2Lv​v in m/s and L in m. Harmonics n = 1, 2, 3 ... times f₁.
Pipe closed at one endf1​=4Lv​Only odd harmonics exist: n = 1, 3, 5 ... times f₁.

Worked examples

The fundamental frequency of a string fixed at both ends is 150Hz. What is the frequency of the third harmonic? How many nodes does it have, including the ends?
Show the solution
  1. The third harmonic means n = 3.
  2. Frequency =3×150=450Hz.
  3. Number of nodes =n+1=4.
Answer: 450Hz and 4 nodes.
Take the speed of sound as 340m/s. A pipe open at both ends is 0.5m long. What is the frequency of its second harmonic?
Show the solution
  1. Fundamental wavelength =2×0.5=1m.
  2. Fundamental frequency =1340​=340Hz.
  3. Second harmonic =2×340=680Hz.
  4. Check: an open pipe carries every harmonic, so the second is double the fundamental.
Answer: 680Hz.
A pipe closed at one end has a fundamental frequency of 100Hz. (a) What is the lowest resonance frequency after the fundamental? (b) What is its length if the speed of sound is 340m/s?
Show the solution
  1. (a) In a closed pipe the harmonics are odd: 1, then 3.
  2. Next frequency =3×100=300Hz.
  3. (b) From the fundamental: L=4×100340​=400340​.
  4. 400340​=0.85.
Answer: (a) 300Hz, (b) 0.85m.
Common mistakes
  • Starting the harmonic count at zero. The fundamental is the first harmonic; the third means multiplying the fundamental by 3.
  • Treating a closed pipe like an open one: double the fundamental does not exist in it, and the next after the fundamental is three times it.
  • Using λ = 4L for the open pipe or λ = 2L for the closed one. Open is half a wave; closed is a quarter wave.
  • Counting nodes without the ends in a fixed string. Each fixed end is a node.
Summary
  • A standing wave has fixed nodes and antinodes; half a wavelength lies between two nodes.
  • Fixed string and open pipe: every harmonic, n× the fundamental.
  • Closed pipe: odd harmonics only, fundamental =4Lv​.
  • The fundamental is the first harmonic.
