Why Cells Divide: Size and Interphase

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Explain why a cell cannot keep growing forever
  • Put the stages of the cell cycle in order and say what happens in each
  • Understand how the chromosome number stays constant in the new cells

The limit on cell size

As a cell grows, its volume increases much faster than its surface area. Food, oxygen and wastes all pass through the surface, but the cell's needs depend on its volume.

So as a cell grows, its surface-area-to-volume ratio falls and transport becomes too slow. That is why a cell stops growing at a certain size and divides into two smaller cells.

  • First reason to divide: the surface-to-volume ratio gets smaller as the cell grows.
  • Second reason: the nucleus holds a limited amount of DNA and cannot control a very large cell.
  • Example: if the side of a cube doubles, its surface area becomes 4 times larger and its volume 8 times larger, so the ratio is halved.

The cell cycle and interphase

The cell cycle is the series of stages in a cell's life from its birth until it divides. A cell spends most of its time in interphase and divides in the M phase.

Interphase has three stages in this order: G1​, then S, then G2​; division (M) follows.

  • G1​: the cell grows, its organelles work and it makes proteins.
  • S: DNA is copied, so each chromosome becomes two identical chromatids.
  • G2​: the cell prepares to divide, makes the proteins for the spindle and checks the copied DNA.
  • M: division (mitosis, then cytokinesis).

How does the chromosome number stay constant?

Copying in the S phase doubles the amount of DNA, but the number of chromosomes does not change: a copied chromosome is still one chromosome with two sister chromatids joined at the centromere.

In mitosis the chromatids separate, so each daughter cell receives a complete set. That is why a daughter cell has the same chromosome number as the mother cell.

In terms of DNA amount: if the DNA in G1​ is one unit, it is two units after the S phase, and back to one unit in each daughter cell.

The resting phase

Some cells, such as nerve cells and heart muscle cells, leave the cycle and enter a stage called G0​ (the resting phase). The cell does its job but does not head toward copying or division.

Some G0​ cells can return to the cycle when they get the right signal, as liver cells do after an injury.

Surface-to-volume ratioratio=volumesurface area​Surface area in cm2, volume in cm3.
Cube of known sidesurface area=6×side2 , volume=side3Side in cm; a cube has six equal faces.
Ratio for a cuberatio=side6​Side in cm; the larger the side, the smaller the ratio.

Worked examples

A cube-shaped cell has a side of 2cm. Find its surface-area-to-volume ratio.
Show the solution
  1. Surface area =6×22=6×4=24cm2.
  2. Volume =23=8cm3.
  3. Ratio =824​=3.
Answer: The ratio is 3.
Cell A has a side of 1cm and cell B a side of 3cm (both cubes). Which exchanges materials faster, and by how many times?
Show the solution
  1. Cell A: surface =6×1=6, volume =1, ratio =6.
  2. Cell B: surface =6×9=54, volume =27, ratio =2754​=2.
  3. Compare: 26​=3.
Answer: Cell A exchanges faster; its ratio is three times larger, which is why small cells suit exchange.
A human cell has 46 chromosomes in G1​. How many chromosomes and how many chromatids does it have after the S phase?
Show the solution
  1. Copying doubles DNA but not the chromosome count, so there are still 46 chromosomes.
  2. Each chromosome now has two chromatids, so chromatids =46×2=92.
Answer: 46 chromosomes and 92 chromatids.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the chromosome number doubles in the S phase. In fact the amount of DNA doubles and chromatids form, but the number stays the same.
  • Thinking a bigger cell exchanges better because its surface is larger. Its surface area is larger, but its volume grows faster, so the ratio drops.
  • Mixing up G0​ and G1​: in G0​ the cell has left the cycle; in G1​ it is still in the cycle and growing.
  • Thinking interphase is a resting stage. In fact it is the longest part of the cycle, in which the cell grows, copies its DNA and prepares to divide.
Summary
  • Cells divide because the surface-to-volume ratio falls as they grow.
  • Cycle order: G1​, S, G2​, M.
  • DNA is copied only in the S phase.
  • The chromosome number stays the same after copying; each chromosome becomes two chromatids.
  • G0​ cells do their job without dividing.

Controlling the Cell Cycle and Apoptosis

What you will learn
  • Identify the three checkpoints and what each checks
  • Explain the role of cyclins in driving the cycle
  • Understand what apoptosis is and why the body needs it

Checkpoints

The cell cycle does not run unsupervised; it has inspection stations called checkpoints. If everything is fine, the cell is allowed to pass; if not, it stops to fix the error.

  • G1​ checkpoint (before S): Is the cell large enough? Are nutrients and growth signals present? Is the DNA undamaged?
  • G2​ checkpoint (before M): Was the DNA copied completely and without errors?
  • Spindle checkpoint (in metaphase): Are all chromosomes attached to spindle fibers?

Cyclins

Cyclins are proteins whose level rises and falls regularly during the cycle. When they build up, they activate enzymes called kinases, which push the cell into the next stage.

They act like an on-switch: without the right cyclin, the cell cannot pass a checkpoint. After a cyclin is used its level drops, which resets the system for the next cycle.

Signals from outside the cell

The cycle is also influenced by signals from outside the cell, such as growth factors, which are proteins released by other cells that stimulate the cell to divide. In healthy tissue a cell stops dividing when it is crowded by neighbouring cells touching it on every side.

The spindle checkpoint

Before the chromatids separate in anaphase, the system checks that every chromosome is attached to spindle fibers from both sides. If one is not attached, the cell waits until it is.

This checkpoint prevents chromosomes from being shared wrongly between the two daughter cells.

Apoptosis

Apoptosis (programmed cell death) is a program inside the cell that makes it die and break down in an orderly way without harming its neighbours. It happens when a damaged cell cannot be repaired, and also during normal development.

Examples: the disappearance of the webbing between a fetus's fingers and the loss of a tadpole's tail. It differs from death by injury, where the cell swells and bursts and causes inflammation.

Worked examples

A cell at the end of G1​ is found to have damaged DNA. What happens?
Show the solution
  1. The checkpoint at the end of G1​ checks DNA integrity.
  2. When damage is found, the cell halts the cycle and does not enter the S phase.
  3. The cell tries to repair the damage; if it succeeds, the cycle continues.
  4. If repair fails, the cell carries out apoptosis.
Answer: It stops at the checkpoint, then either repairs itself or dies by apoptosis.
In a cell, one chromosome is not attached to the spindle fibers in metaphase. Which checkpoint acts, and what does it do?
Show the solution
  1. Metaphase comes before anaphase, and the spindle checkpoint sits there.
  2. This checkpoint makes sure every chromosome is attached to the fibers.
  3. Since one is unattached, the cell is blocked from entering anaphase until it attaches.
Answer: The spindle checkpoint; it delays separation until the chromosome is attached.
A cell cycle lasts 24 hours: G1​=10, S=8, G2​=4 and M=2 hours. What fraction of the time is spent in interphase?
Show the solution
  1. Interphase =10+8+4=22 hours.
  2. Fraction =2422​=1211​.
  3. So the cell spends more than 90% of its time in interphase.
Answer: 1211​ of the cycle (about 92%).
Common mistakes
  • Thinking apoptosis means disease. In fact it is a normal, orderly process needed for development and for removing damaged cells.
  • Thinking cyclin levels stay constant. In fact they rise and fall during the cycle, and that is what drives it.
  • Mixing up checkpoint positions: the first is before S, the second before M, and the spindle checkpoint before anaphase.
Summary
  • The three checkpoints are G1​, G2​ and the spindle checkpoint.
  • Cyclins activate kinases, which drive the cycle.
  • The spindle checkpoint confirms chromosome attachment before separation.
  • Apoptosis is an orderly death of a damaged or unneeded cell.

Cancer: When Control of the Cycle Is Lost

What you will learn
  • Define cancer and its cause at the cell level
  • Link cancer to the failure of checkpoints
  • Calculate the effect of a shorter cycle on cell numbers

What is cancer?

Cancer is cell division with no control. A cancer cell ignores stop signals and checkpoints, so it divides again and again and forms a mass called a tumour.

Loss of control

The root cause is mutations in the genes that regulate the cycle: genes that push division get stuck on, or genes that stop it are disabled.

When checkpoints fail, a cell with damaged DNA keeps dividing, and it also fails to undergo apoptosis, so mutations pile up in its descendants. A healthy cell stops dividing when it is crowded by its neighbours; a cancer cell piles up on top of them.

What causes the mutations?

Mutations become more likely with exposure to carcinogens such as ultraviolet light, tobacco, certain chemicals and some viruses, and a mutation can also be inherited.

Treatment often targets fast-dividing cells, as in chemotherapy and radiotherapy. That is why it also harms healthy fast-dividing cells, such as hair follicle cells.

Cancer and cycle length

The cancer cell's cycle is often shorter, or the cell keeps dividing without entering the resting phase. So its numbers multiply faster than neighbouring healthy cells.

In a malignant tumour, cells may break away, travel in the blood or lymph to other organs and form new tumours; a benign tumour stays in place.

Laws and rules

Cells after several divisionscells=2number of divisionsCounting starts from one cell; number of divisions = total time ÷ cycle length.

Worked examples

A healthy cell has a cycle of 24 hours and a cancer cell a cycle of 12 hours. How many cells does each produce after 48 hours?
Show the solution
  1. Healthy: divisions =2448​=2, so cells =22=4.
  2. Cancer: divisions =1248​=4, so cells =24=16.
  3. Check: 16÷4=4, so the cancer cell produced four times as many.
Answer: Healthy: 4 cells; cancer: 16 cells.
A tumour begins with one cancer cell whose cycle lasts 8 hours, and it divides without stopping. How many cells does it contain after two days?
Show the solution
  1. Two days =2×24=48 hours.
  2. Divisions =848​=6.
  3. Cells =26=64.
Answer: 64 cells.
A mutation disables the G1​ checkpoint in a cell whose DNA is damaged. What is expected to happen?
Show the solution
  1. The G1​ checkpoint is what halts a damaged cell before DNA is copied.
  2. If it fails, the cell does not stop; it enters S and copies the damaged DNA.
  3. The cell passes the damage on to its daughters, and does not die by apoptosis if that is also disabled.
  4. Mutations accumulate and a tumour may form.
Answer: The damaged cell keeps dividing and passes on the damage; cancer may develop.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking every stage of the cycle is faster in a cancer cell. In fact the problem is lost control: the cell does not wait for signals or halt at checkpoints, so its cycle is often shorter and repeats without stopping.
  • Thinking cancer is contagious. It arises from mutations in the body's own cells.
  • Thinking every tumour is cancer. A benign tumour does not spread to other organs.
Summary
  • Cancer is cell division out of control.
  • It is caused by mutations that disable cycle-regulating genes and checkpoints.
  • Cancer cells ignore apoptosis and stop signals.
  • A shorter cycle means cell numbers multiply faster.

Mitosis

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Put the four phases of mitosis in order and describe each
  • Know the role of spindle fibers and cytokinesis
  • Explain the importance and outcome of mitosis

What is mitosis?

Mitosis is the division of the nucleus that produces two genetically identical nuclei. It is followed by cytokinesis, which splits the mother cell into two daughter cells, each with the same number of chromosomes.

Its importance: growth of the body, replacing damaged cells and healing wounds, and asexual reproduction in some organisms.

The four phases

After the S phase each chromosome is made of two chromatids. The phases go in this order:

  • Prophase: chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes, the nuclear envelope and nucleolus disappear, spindle fibers form, and in animal cells the centrosomes move toward the poles.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes line up at the cell's equator, each chromatid attached to a fiber from the pole opposite its sister.
  • Anaphase: the centromere splits and the sister chromatids separate; the spindle pulls them to opposite poles.
  • Telophase: each set reaches a pole, a new nuclear envelope forms around each set and the nucleolus reappears, the chromosomes uncoil into chromatin and the spindle disappears.

Spindle fibers

Spindle fibers are tiny protein tubes that grow in animal cells from the centrosomes (plant cells form them without centrosomes) and extend toward the poles. They attach to chromosomes at the centromere and shorten, pulling the separated chromatids to opposite sides.

Cytokinesis

It usually begins during telophase. In an animal cell a cleavage furrow pinches inward until the cytoplasm splits; in a plant cell a cell plate forms in the middle and becomes a new wall.

Laws and rules

Cells after several divisionscells=2number of divisionsCounting starts from one cell; each mitosis doubles the number.

Worked examples

A body cell with 8 chromosomes divides by mitosis. How many chromosomes does each daughter cell have?
Show the solution
  1. Mitosis produces two cells identical to the mother cell.
  2. Each daughter receives a complete set, so the number does not change.
Answer: 8 chromosomes.
A human body cell has 46 chromosomes. How many chromosomes are in the cell during anaphase?
Show the solution
  1. Before anaphase (in prophase and metaphase): 46 chromosomes, each with two chromatids, so 92 chromatids.
  2. In anaphase the centromere splits, so each chromatid becomes its own chromosome.
  3. Total in the cell =46×2=92 chromosomes, with 46 moving to each pole.
Answer: 92 chromosomes in the whole cell.
A cell divides by mitosis 3 times in a row. How many cells result, and how many chromosomes does each have if the mother cell had 6?
Show the solution
  1. Cells =23=8.
  2. Each cell carries the same number as the mother cell.
  3. Check step by step: 1→2→4→8.
Answer: 8 cells, each with 6 chromosomes.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up metaphase and anaphase: in metaphase chromosomes line up; in anaphase the chromatids separate.
  • Thinking chromosomes are copied in anaphase. In fact their number doubles because each chromatid becomes an independent chromosome, but the copying of DNA took place in the S phase.
  • Thinking cytokinesis is a phase of mitosis. It is a separate division of the cytoplasm that follows nuclear division.
Summary
  • Mitosis produces two identical cells with the mother's chromosome number.
  • Order: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase.
  • Spindle fibers pull the chromatids to the poles.
  • Cytokinesis follows nuclear division.
  • Importance of mitosis: growth, replacement and repair.

Meiosis

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell haploid cells from diploid cells
  • Follow meiosis I and II and their products
  • Explain how crossing over and independent assortment create genetic variety

Haploid and diploid

A body cell carries two of each chromosome: one from the father and one from the mother. These two chromosomes, similar in shape and genes, are called homologous chromosomes, and the cell is diploid (2n).

A gamete (egg or sperm) carries one chromosome from each pair, so it is haploid (n). In humans 2n=46 and n=23. At fertilisation the diploid number is restored: n+n=2n.

Products of meiosis

Meiosis is two successive divisions of one nucleus with only one round of DNA copying. It produces four haploid cells that differ genetically from each other and from the parent. It takes place in the reproductive organs to make gametes.

In males one cell gives four sperm; in females it gives one large egg and three small polar bodies that break down.

Meiosis I

This is the division that halves the number, because homologous chromosomes (not chromatids) separate in it.

In prophase I each chromosome pairs with its homologue, forming tetrads (each tetrad has four chromatids), and crossing over occurs. In metaphase I the pairs line up, and in anaphase I each pair separates without the centromeres splitting, giving two haploid cells whose chromosomes still have two chromatids each.

Two sources of variety

  • Crossing over: exchange of pieces between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes, producing a chromosome that mixes father's and mother's genes.
  • Independent assortment: each homologous pair lines up at the equator randomly and independently of the others, so the mix of chromosomes in the gametes varies.

Meiosis II

It resembles mitosis: the centromere splits and the sister chromatids separate, with no new DNA copying before it. The two cells become four haploid cells, and each chromosome now has a single chromatid.

Laws and rules

Possible gamete combinationscombinations=2nn = number of homologous pairs (not counting crossing over).

Worked examples

A human body cell with 46 chromosomes enters meiosis. How many cells result, and how many chromosomes does each have?
Show the solution
  1. Meiosis produces four cells.
  2. Each cell is haploid: 246​=23 chromosomes.
Answer: 4 cells, each with 23 chromosomes.
An organism's body cells have 6 chromosomes. How many kinds of gametes can independent assortment alone produce? And in humans?
Show the solution
  1. Number of homologous pairs n=26​=3, so combinations =23=8.
  2. In humans n=23, so combinations =223.
  3. 210=1024 and 213=8192, so 223=1024×8192=8388608.
Answer: 8 kinds in the organism; in humans 8388608 combinations (over 8 million).
A human body cell enters meiosis. How many chromosomes and chromatids does each cell have in metaphase I, and in metaphase II?
Show the solution
  1. Before meiosis DNA is copied in the S phase: 46 chromosomes, each with two chromatids, so 92 chromatids.
  2. In metaphase I the count is unchanged: 46 chromosomes and 92 chromatids, lined up as 23 tetrads.
  3. In meiosis I the homologues separated, so each cell has 23 chromosomes, each with two chromatids, so 46 chromatids.
  4. In metaphase II: 23 chromosomes and 46 chromatids.
Answer: Metaphase I: 46 chromosomes and 92 chromatids. Metaphase II: 23 chromosomes and 46 chromatids.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking sister chromatids separate in meiosis I. In fact homologous chromosomes separate; chromatids separate in meiosis II.
  • Mixing up homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids: homologues come from different parents; sisters are identical copies of one chromosome.
  • Thinking DNA is copied before meiosis II. There is no copying between the two divisions.
Summary
  • Body cells are diploid (2n); gametes are haploid (n).
  • Meiosis is two divisions giving four haploid cells.
  • In meiosis I homologous chromosomes separate, and this is what halves the number.
  • Crossing over and independent assortment are the two sources of genetic variety.

Mitosis vs Meiosis and Nondisjunction

What you will learn
  • Compare mitosis and meiosis in number, products and purpose
  • Understand nondisjunction and its effect on chromosome number

Quick comparison

  • Divisions: mitosis is one division, meiosis is two.
  • Products: mitosis gives two cells, meiosis gives four.
  • Chromosome number: stays the same in mitosis (2n to 2n) and is halved in meiosis (2n to n).
  • Genetic identity: mitosis cells are identical; meiosis cells are different.
  • Place and purpose: mitosis in body cells for growth and repair; meiosis in reproductive cells to make gametes.
  • Crossing over and independent assortment: in meiosis only; homologues do not pair in mitosis.

Nondisjunction

Nondisjunction is an error in which homologous chromosomes fail to separate in meiosis I, or sister chromatids fail to separate in meiosis II. Both go into one cell.

This gives abnormal gametes: some have an extra chromosome (n+1) and some lack one (n−1). If one joins a normal gamete, the embryo has an abnormal chromosome number.

The place of the error matters: if it happens in meiosis I, all four gametes are abnormal; if it happens in meiosis II in one of the two cells, two gametes are normal and two are abnormal.

A well-known example is Down syndrome: three copies of chromosome 21, making 47 chromosomes in total.

Worked examples

A body cell has 8 chromosomes. Compare the chromosome number in the cells produced by mitosis and by meiosis.
Show the solution
  1. Mitosis: two cells, each with 8 chromosomes.
  2. Meiosis: four cells, each with 28​=4 chromosomes.
Answer: Mitosis: 8 in each of two cells; meiosis: 4 in each of four cells.
Nondisjunction of chromosome pair 21 during egg formation produces an egg with 24 chromosomes. If a normal sperm (with 23) fertilises it, how many chromosomes does the zygote have? What if the egg were missing one chromosome (22)?
Show the solution
  1. First case: 24+23=47 chromosomes.
  2. Second case: 22+23=45 chromosomes.
Answer: 47 in the first case and 45 in the second; both differ from the normal 46.
A body cell has 4 chromosomes (two homologous pairs). One pair fails to separate in meiosis I. How many chromosomes does each of the four gametes have? And if the same error happened in meiosis II?
Show the solution
  1. The normal gamete number is n=24​=2.
  2. In meiosis I the two chromosomes of the affected pair went together into one cell, which received 2+1=3 chromosomes, while the other received just one.
  3. Meiosis II is normal, so each cell gives two identical gametes: the gametes have 3, 3, 1 and 1.
  4. If the error happened in meiosis II in one of the cells, the healthy cell would give two gametes with 2 and 2, and the other cell two gametes with 3 and 1.
Answer: The gametes have 3, 3, 1 and 1, all abnormal. In the second case: 2, 2, 3 and 1.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking nondisjunction changes genes. It changes the number of chromosomes, that is, the amount of genetic material, in the cell.
  • Thinking mitosis produces genetically different cells. Mitosis produces identical cells; variety is a feature of meiosis.
  • Thinking meiosis happens in all body cells. It happens only in reproductive cells.
Summary
  • Mitosis: one division, two identical cells, number kept.
  • Meiosis: two divisions, four different cells, number halved.
  • Nondisjunction is an error in chromosome distribution that gives gametes with extra or missing chromosomes.
  • Down syndrome is an example of an extra copy of chromosome 21.
