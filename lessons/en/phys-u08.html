Electric Charge and Ways of Charging

What you will learn
  • Decide whether the force between two charges is attraction or repulsion
  • Explain charging by friction, contact and induction
  • Tell conductors from insulators
  • Find charge from a number of electrons

Two kinds of charge

An atom has positive protons in the nucleus and negative electrons around it. A neutral object has as many protons as electrons.

Like charges repel and unlike charges attract. In every method of charging only electrons move; the protons stay fixed in the nucleus.

A charged object also attracts a neutral object nearby, because the neutral object's charges shift slightly and the opposite charge ends up closer. So repulsion, not attraction, is the sure sign that both objects carry the same kind of charge.

  • A negative object has a surplus of electrons.
  • A positive object has a shortage of electrons.

Three ways to charge

  • Friction: rub two different materials together. Electrons move from one to the other, so one becomes positive and the other negative, with equal amounts of charge.
  • Contact: touch a conductor with a charged object. Charge flows between them, so the conductor gets the same kind of charge as the object.
  • Induction: bring a charged object near a conductor without touching it, earth the conductor for a moment, remove the earth connection first, and only then take the charged object away. The conductor is left with the opposite charge to the object.

Conductors and insulators

A conductor, such as copper or aluminium, has free electrons that move easily, so charge spreads over its whole surface. In an insulator, such as plastic, glass or rubber, the electrons are held by their atoms, so the charge stays where it is.

That is why we hold a charged conductor by an insulating handle: its charge cannot leak through our hand to the ground.

Quantisation and conservation of charge

Charge cannot have just any value. It is always a whole number of the charge of one electron, which is 1.6×10−19C.

The total charge of an isolated system stays constant: what one object loses another gains, and charge is never created from nothing.

Laws and rules

Charge quantisationQ=n×1.6×10−19Cn is the number of extra or missing electrons (a whole number); Q is in coulombs.
Contact between identical spheresQfinal​=2Q1​+Q2​​Two identical spheres share their total charge equally after touching; keep the signs of Q1​ and Q2​.

Worked examples

An object has 20 extra electrons. What is its charge?
Show the solution
  1. Charge = number of electrons × charge of one electron.
  2. Q=20×1.6×10−19.
  3. 20×1.6=32, so Q=32×10−19=3.2×10−18C.
  4. The electrons are in surplus, so the sign is negative.
Answer: Q=−3.2×10−18C
A rod gains 3×1012 electrons by rubbing. What is its charge?
Show the solution
  1. Q=3×1012×1.6×10−19.
  2. Multiply the numbers: 3×1.6=4.8.
  3. Add the exponents: 1012×10−19=10−7.
  4. Q=4.8×10−7C, and it is negative because the rod gained electrons.
Answer: Q=−4.8×10−7C
Two identical metal spheres carry +8μC and −2μC. They touch and are then separated. What is the charge on each?
Show the solution
  1. Add the charges with their signs: 8+(−2)=6μC.
  2. The spheres are identical, so they share the charge equally.
  3. Each sphere has 26​=3μC.
  4. Check: 3+3=6, the same total as before, so charge is conserved.
Answer: Each sphere carries +3μC
Common mistakes
  • Some students think protons move when objects are rubbed. Only electrons move; negative means a surplus of electrons.
  • Induction leaves the conductor with the opposite charge to the nearby object, while contact leaves it with the same kind. Do not mix them up.
  • Concluding that two objects carry opposite charges just because they attract. One of them may be neutral; repulsion, however, always means like charges.
  • In contact problems add the charges with their signs before dividing by two; do not drop a negative sign.
Summary
  • Like charges repel and unlike charges attract.
  • Only electrons move; negative = surplus of electrons.
  • Friction leaves the two objects with equal and opposite charges, contact gives the same kind of charge, and induction gives the opposite kind.
  • Charge is a whole multiple of 1.6×10−19C and is conserved.

Coulomb's Law and the Electric Field

What you will learn
  • Find the force between two charges with Coulomb's law
  • Understand what an electric field is and which way it points
  • Find the field around a point charge
  • Find the force on a charge placed in a field

Coulomb's law

The electric force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

Doubling one charge doubles the force. Doubling the distance makes the force one quarter, because distance enters the law squared.

  • The two forces on the pair are equal in size and opposite in direction, even if one charge is much larger than the other.
  • In calculations use the sizes of the charges; the signs only tell you whether the force is attraction or repulsion.
  • With more than two charges, add the forces (and likewise the fields) taking direction into account; two forces can cancel each other.

The electric field

The electric field is the region around a charge where it exerts an electric force on any other charge. We measure it by placing a small positive test charge and dividing the force by that charge.

The field points the way a positive test charge would be pushed, so field lines leave positive charges and enter negative ones. Where the lines are closer together, the field is stronger.

Force on a charge in a field

A charge placed in a field feels a force equal to the charge times the field. A positive charge moves along the field and a negative charge moves against it.

Laws and rules

Coulomb's lawF=kr2q1​q2​​F in newtons, q1​,q2​ in coulombs, r the distance in metres, k=9×109Nm2/C2.
Electric field strengthE=qF​E in N/C, F in newtons, q the test charge in coulombs.
Field of a point chargeE=kr2Q​Q is the source charge in coulombs, r the distance from it in metres.
Force on a charge in a fieldF=qEF in newtons, q in coulombs, E in N/C.

Worked examples

Two charges of 2×10−6C and 3×10−6C are 0.3m apart. What is the repulsive force?
Show the solution
  1. Write the law: F=kr2q1​q2​​.
  2. Numerator: 9×109×2×10−6×3×10−6=54×10−3=0.054.
  3. Denominator: r2=0.32=0.09.
  4. F=0.090.054​=0.6N.
Answer: F=0.6N
A point charge is 4×10−9C. Find the field 0.3m from it, and the force on a 2×10−6C charge placed there.
Show the solution
  1. Field E=kr2Q​.
  2. Numerator: 9×109×4×10−9=36; denominator: 0.32=0.09.
  3. E=0.0936​=400N/C.
  4. Force F=qE=2×10−6×400=8×10−4N.
Answer: E=400N/C and F=8×10−4N
The repulsive force between two charges is 2N. One charge is doubled and the distance is halved. What is the new force?
Show the solution
  1. Doubling one charge multiplies the force by 2.
  2. The distance becomes 21​, so its square is 41​; since distance is in the denominator the force is multiplied by 4.
  3. Total factor =2×4=8.
  4. New force =2×8=16N.
Answer: F=16N
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting to square the distance: doubling the distance makes the force a quarter, not a half.
  • Thinking the larger charge pushes harder on the smaller one. The two forces are always equal in size.
  • Using centimetres in the formula without converting to metres first.
  • Getting the field direction wrong: field lines leave a positive charge and enter a negative one, and the force on a negative charge is opposite to the field.
Summary
  • Force between charges: proportional to the product of the charges, inversely proportional to the distance squared.
  • The field is force per unit test charge; it leaves positive and enters negative charges.
  • The field of a point charge is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from it.
  • Force in a field = charge × field.

Potential Difference, Work and the Uniform Field

What you will learn
  • Define potential difference and its unit, the volt
  • Link work to charge and potential difference
  • Link a uniform field to voltage and distance

What is potential difference?

The potential difference between two points is the work needed to move one coulomb of charge between them. Its unit is the volt, and one volt is one joule per coulomb.

A battery keeps a steady potential difference between its terminals, and this is what pushes charges around a circuit.

Work and potential difference

When a charge moves between two points with a known potential difference, the work equals the charge times the potential difference. More charge or more voltage means more work.

A positive charge moves by itself from higher to lower potential, like a ball rolling downhill.

If a charge starts from rest and is moved only by the potential difference, this work becomes kinetic energy, so more charge or more voltage gives a faster particle.

The uniform field between two plates

Between two parallel plates with opposite charges the field is uniform: it has the same size and direction everywhere between them, from the positive plate to the negative plate.

Its strength equals the potential difference across the plates divided by their separation. That is why the field can be measured in V/m, which equals N/C.

Laws and rules

Potential differenceV=qW​V in volts, W the work in joules, q the charge in coulombs.
Work and voltageW=qVW in joules, q in coulombs, V in volts.
Uniform field between platesE=dV​E in V/m (equal to N/C), V the voltage across the plates, d the separation in metres.
Kinetic energy gainedEk​=qVFor a charge released from rest. Ek​ in joules, q in coulombs, V in volts.

Worked examples

A charge of 2C moves through a potential difference of 12V. How much work is done?
Show the solution
  1. W=qV.
  2. W=2×12=24J.
Answer: W=24J
Two parallel plates are 0.02m apart with 200V between them. What is the field strength between them?
Show the solution
  1. E=dV​.
  2. E=0.02200​.
  3. 0.02200​=10000V/m, which is 10000N/C.
Answer: E=10000N/C
Two plates are 0.05m apart with 500V between them. A charge of 2×10−6C moves from one plate to the other. Find the field, the force and the work.
Show the solution
  1. E=0.05500​=10000N/C.
  2. F=qE=2×10−6×104=0.02N.
  3. W=Fd=0.02×0.05=0.001J.
  4. Check another way: W=qV=2×10−6×500=0.001J, the same result.
Answer: E=10000N/C, F=0.02N, W=0.001J
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up voltage and current. Voltage (volts) is work per coulomb; current (amperes) is charge per second.
  • Forgetting to change centimetres to metres when finding the field from the voltage.
  • Thinking the field between the plates changes from point to point. A uniform field has the same value everywhere between the plates.
  • Forgetting that moving the plates further apart at the same voltage weakens the field.
Summary
  • Potential difference = work per coulomb; 1V=1J/C.
  • W=qV.
  • In a uniform field E=V/d.
  • V/m is the same unit as N/C.
  • The work done on a charge released from rest becomes kinetic energy, equal to charge × voltage.

Capacitors and Capacitance

What you will learn
  • Define capacitance and its unit, the farad
  • Know what affects a capacitor's capacitance
  • Find the energy stored in a capacitor
  • Combine capacitors in series and in parallel

The capacitor and capacitance

A capacitor stores charge. It is made of two conducting plates separated by an insulator. Connected to a battery, one plate carries positive charge and the other an equal negative charge.

Capacitance is the ratio of the stored charge to the potential difference across the plates. Its unit is the farad, and we usually meet its fractions: one microfarad is 10−6 farad.

What affects capacitance?

  • Capacitance rises when the plate area is larger.
  • Capacitance falls when the plates are further apart.
  • Capacitance rises when an insulating material replaces air between the plates.
  • Capacitance belongs to the capacitor itself, so it does not change when the charge or voltage changes.
  • If the capacitor stays connected to the battery, the voltage is fixed and the charge changes; if it is disconnected, the charge is fixed and the voltage changes.

Energy of a capacitor

A charged capacitor stores electrical energy, because charging the plates takes work. The energy grows with the capacitance and with the square of the voltage.

Connecting capacitors

Capacitors behave opposite to resistors. In parallel the capacitances add, because the total plate area grows and the voltage is the same. In series the total capacitance falls and every capacitor carries the same charge.

Laws and rules

CapacitanceC=VQ​C in farads, Q in coulombs, V in volts.
Stored energyU=21​CV2U in joules, C in farads, V in volts.
Capacitors in parallelCtotal​=C1​+C2​The voltage is the same across each capacitor; the charges add.
Two capacitors in seriesCtotal​=C1​+C2​C1​C2​​The charge is the same on each capacitor; the voltage is shared. The total is smaller than the smallest capacitor. In general Ctotal​1​=C1​1​+C2​1​.

Worked examples

A capacitor holds 6×10−6C at 3V. What is its capacitance?
Show the solution
  1. C=VQ​.
  2. C=36×10−6​=2×10−6F=2μF.
Answer: C=2μF
A 4μF capacitor is connected to a 10V battery. How much energy does it store?
Show the solution
  1. U=21​CV2.
  2. V2=100 and C=4×10−6F.
  3. U=21​×4×10−6×100=2×10−4J.
Answer: U=2×10−4J
Capacitors of 3μF and 6μF are connected to a 12V battery. Find the total capacitance and total charge in parallel and in series.
Show the solution
  1. In parallel: Ctotal​=3+6=9μF.
  2. Total charge =9×12=108μC.
  3. In series: Ctotal​=3+63×6​=918​=2μF.
  4. Charge =2×12=24μC, and this same charge sits on each capacitor.
Answer: Parallel: 9μF and 108μC. Series: 2μF and 24μC on each capacitor
Common mistakes
  • Applying the resistor rules to capacitors unchanged. It is the other way round: capacitances add in parallel, and in series you use the reciprocals.
  • Forgetting that the voltage is squared in the energy law: doubling the voltage makes the energy four times larger, not twice.
  • Thinking capacitance changes with charge. It depends only on plate area, separation and the insulator.
  • Thinking that moving the plates apart raises the capacitance. The opposite is true: a larger gap gives a smaller capacitance.
Summary
  • C=Q/V, measured in farads.
  • Capacitance rises with area and insulator, and falls with separation.
  • Energy =21​CV2.
  • Parallel adds capacitances; series gives less than the smallest.

Current, Ohm's Law and Resistance

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Define and calculate electric current
  • Apply Ohm's law
  • Know what affects the resistance of a wire
  • Use an ammeter and a voltmeter correctly
  • Understand the effect of a battery's internal resistance

Electric current

Electric current is the amount of charge that passes through a cross-section of wire each second. Its unit is the ampere, and one ampere is one coulomb per second.

A current needs a closed circuit and a potential difference, supplied by the battery, to push the charges. Conventional current flows from the positive terminal to the negative one outside the battery, opposite to the real motion of the electrons.

Ohm's law

For a fixed resistor the current is directly proportional to the voltage across it. A graph of voltage against current is a straight line through the origin, and its slope is the resistance.

Resistance measures how hard it is for current to pass; its unit is the ohm. At the same voltage, more resistance means less current.

What decides the resistance of a wire?

  • Length: resistance is directly proportional to length.
  • Cross-section area: resistance is inversely proportional to area, so a thicker wire has less resistance.
  • Material: described by the resistivity, which is small for copper and huge for insulators.
  • Temperature: the resistance of metals rises as they get hotter, because the atoms vibrate more and obstruct the electrons more.

Ammeter and voltmeter

An ammeter measures current and is connected in series with the device; its resistance is very small so it does not change the current. A voltmeter measures voltage and is connected in parallel across the device; its resistance is very large so it draws almost no current.

Internal resistance of a battery

A battery has a small internal resistance. When current flows, some voltage is lost inside the battery itself, so the voltage across its terminals is less than its emf. The larger the current, the larger this loss.

Laws and rules

CurrentI=tQ​I in amperes, Q in coulombs, t in seconds.
Ohm's lawV=IRV in volts, I in amperes, R in ohms.
Resistance of a wireR=ρAL​R in ohms, ρ the resistivity of the material (in Ωm), L the length in metres, A the cross-section area in m2.
Current with internal resistanceI=R+rε​ε is the emf in volts, R the external resistance and r the internal resistance, both in ohms.
Terminal voltageV=ε−IrV is the voltage across the battery terminals in volts, I the current in amperes.

Worked examples

A charge of 30C passes through a 4Ω resistor in 10s. Find the current and the voltage across the resistor.
Show the solution
  1. I=tQ​=1030​=3A.
  2. V=IR.
  3. V=3×4=12V.
Answer: I=3A and V=12V
A wire has a resistance of 8Ω. A wire of the same material is twice as long and has twice the diameter. What is its resistance?
Show the solution
  1. Area is proportional to diameter squared, so it becomes 22=4 times larger.
  2. Doubling the length doubles the resistance; four times the area divides it by four.
  3. Factor =42​=21​.
  4. New resistance =8×21​=4Ω.
Answer: R=4Ω
A battery with emf 12V and internal resistance 1Ω is connected to a 5Ω resistor. Find the current and the terminal voltage.
Show the solution
  1. Total resistance =5+1=6Ω.
  2. I=612​=2A.
  3. Voltage lost inside the battery =2×1=2V.
  4. Terminal voltage =12−2=10V. Check: 2×5=10V across the external resistor.
Answer: I=2A and terminal voltage =10V
Common mistakes
  • Connecting an ammeter in parallel: an ammeter goes in series, and a voltmeter goes in parallel.
  • Thinking a longer or thinner wire has less resistance. It is the opposite: more length and less area give more resistance.
  • Assuming the terminal voltage always equals the emf. That is true only when no current flows.
  • Mixing resistance with resistivity: resistivity belongs to the material, resistance belongs to a particular piece of wire.
Summary
  • Current = charge ÷ time, in amperes.
  • V=IR.
  • Resistance rises with length and temperature, and falls with area.
  • Ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel.
  • Internal resistance makes the terminal voltage lower than the emf when current flows.

Combining Resistors and Kirchhoff's Rules

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Find the equivalent resistance in series and in parallel
  • Solve a combined circuit step by step
  • Apply the junction rule and the loop rule

Resistors in series

In series, resistors are joined one after another along a single path. The same current flows through all of them, the total voltage is shared among them, and the total resistance is their sum.

If any part of the circuit breaks, the current stops everywhere.

Resistors in parallel

In parallel, resistors are joined between the same two points. The voltage across each branch is the same, and the total current splits between the branches, with more current in the branch of lower resistance.

Adding a branch lowers the total resistance because it opens another path for current. So the total resistance in parallel is smaller than the smallest branch. If one branch breaks, the others keep working.

Combined circuits

Solve a combined circuit in steps: replace each parallel group by its equivalent resistance, then add what is now in series to get the total resistance. Next find the total current, then work back to find the voltage and current in each part.

Kirchhoff's rules

  • Junction rule: the total current into a junction equals the total current out, because charge does not pile up.
  • Loop rule: around any closed loop, the sum of the emfs equals the sum of the voltage drops (IR) across the resistors, because energy is conserved.
  • To apply the loop rule, choose a direction around the loop: an emf counts positive if we pass through the battery from its negative to its positive terminal, and the drop across a resistor counts negative if we go in the direction of the current.

Laws and rules

Equivalent resistance in seriesRtotal​=R1​+R2​+R3​Resistances in ohms. The current is the same through all; the voltages add up to the total.
Two resistors in parallelRtotal​=R1​+R2​R1​R2​​Resistances in ohms. The voltage is the same across both, and the currents add. n equal resistors of value R in parallel give nR​. In general Rtotal​1​=R1​1​+R2​1​.
Junction rule∑Iin​=∑Iout​Currents in amperes at any branching point.
Loop rule∑ε=∑IRAround a closed loop, in volts.

Worked examples

Resistors of 2, 3 and 5Ω are in series with a 20V battery. Find the current and the voltage across each resistor.
Show the solution
  1. Rtotal​=2+3+5=10Ω.
  2. I=1020​=2A, the same in every resistor.
  3. Voltages: 2×2=4V, 2×3=6V, 2×5=10V.
  4. Check: 4+6+10=20V, the battery voltage.
Answer: I=2A; voltages 4, 6 and 10V
Resistors of 6Ω and 3Ω are in parallel with a 12V battery. Find the total resistance, each branch current and the total current.
Show the solution
  1. Rtotal​=6+36×3​=918​=2Ω.
  2. First branch: 612​=2A; second branch: 312​=4A.
  3. Total current =2+4=6A.
  4. Check: 212​=6A using the equivalent resistance.
Answer: Rtotal​=2Ω; branch currents 2A and 4A; total 6A
A 12V battery is connected to a resistor R1​=4Ω in series with a parallel pair R2​=6Ω and R3​=3Ω (as in the figure). Find the current in each resistor.
Show the solution
  1. Equivalent of the parallel pair: 6+36×3​=2Ω.
  2. Now it is a series circuit: Rtotal​=4+2=6Ω.
  3. Total current =612​=2A, which is the current in the 4Ω resistor.
  4. Voltage across the parallel pair =2×2=4V, so the 6Ω carries 64​=32​A and the 3Ω carries 34​A.
  5. Check with the junction rule: 32​+34​=2A; and with the loop rule: 8+4=12V.

Common mistakes
  • Adding resistances directly in parallel. The total in parallel is smaller than the smallest branch; if your answer is bigger, it is wrong.
  • Thinking the current differs between resistors in series. The current is the same; the voltage is what differs.
  • Thinking the voltage is shared among parallel branches. Each branch has the full voltage; the current is what is shared.
  • In a combined circuit, finding the voltage across the parallel group by multiplying the total current by only one of its resistors, instead of by the equivalent resistance of the group.
Summary
  • Series: same current, voltage shared, resistances add.
  • Parallel: same voltage, current shared, total resistance smaller than the smallest branch.
  • For a combined circuit: parallel first, then series, then work back.
  • Junction rule for currents; loop rule for voltages.

Electric Power, Energy and Household Circuits

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Calculate electric power with the three formulas
  • Calculate electrical energy and its cost
  • Explain the heating effect of current
  • Understand household circuits and safety devices

Electric power

Power is the rate at which electrical energy is converted each second. Its unit is the watt. A device rated 100W converts 100J every second.

Power equals current times voltage. With Ohm's law we get two more forms, and we pick the one that fits the data.

  • If the same current flows through the devices (series), use current and resistance: more resistance means more power.
  • If the same voltage is across the devices (parallel), use voltage and resistance: less resistance means more power.

Electrical energy

The energy a device uses equals its power times the time it runs. It is in joules when the time is in seconds, and in kilowatt-hours when the power is in kilowatts and the time in hours.

The kilowatt-hour is the unit on the bill and equals 3.6×106J. Power is in watts or kilowatts; energy is in joules or kilowatt-hours. Never mix them up.

The heating effect of current

When current flows through a resistance, part of the electrical energy becomes heat, because the moving electrons collide with the atoms. This is how heaters, irons and glowing filaments work.

The heat equals the current squared times the resistance times the time, so doubling the current gives four times the heat.

Household circuits and safety

Appliances in a home are connected in parallel, so each one gets the full supply voltage and works independently. The more appliances you switch on, the larger the total current in the circuit.

A fuse or circuit breaker cuts the circuit automatically if the current goes above a safe limit, preventing overheating and fire. The earth wire carries any leaking current to the ground instead of through a person. The fuse is placed on the live wire so that the appliance is cut off from the supply when it melts. Never touch electrical devices with wet hands.

Laws and rules

Electric powerP=IVP in watts, I in amperes, V in volts.
Two other forms of powerP=I2R=RV2​R in ohms. Use the first when the current is fixed and the second when the voltage is fixed.
Electrical energyE=PtE in joules when P is in watts and t in seconds, or in kilowatt-hours when P is in kilowatts and t in hours.
Heat produced by currentQ=I2RtQ in joules, I in amperes, R in ohms, t in seconds.

Show the solution
  1. I=VP​=2202200​=10A.
  2. R=IV​=10220​=22Ω.
  3. Check: 222202​=2248400​=2200W.

  2. Cost =6×0.2=1.2 riyals.

  1. Find each resistance from its rating: R=PV2​.
  2. First lamp: 1001002​=100Ω; second lamp: 251002​=400Ω.

Common mistakes
  • Mixing up the kilowatt (power) with the kilowatt-hour (energy). The bill charges for energy, so multiply power by time.
  • Forgetting to convert watts to kilowatts before finding kilowatt-hours: 2000W=2kW.
  • For two bulbs in series the one with the larger resistance glows brighter, because the current is the same. In parallel the one with the smaller resistance glows brighter.
  • Thinking that adding appliances in parallel raises the voltage. The voltage stays the same; the total current rises, which is why a fuse may blow.
Summary
  • Power = current × voltage, in watts.
  • Energy = power × time; bills use kilowatt-hours.
