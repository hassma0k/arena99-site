Mixtures, Solutions and Colloids

What you will learn
  • Tell homogeneous from heterogeneous mixtures
  • Identify the solvent and solute in any solution
  • Tell a solution from a colloid and a suspension by particle size

What is a mixture?

A mixture is two or more substances combined without a chemical reaction, so each keeps its properties and can be separated by physical means. Water with sand, air and sugar water are all mixtures.

  • Homogeneous mixture: looks like one substance and its parts cannot be told apart. This is a solution.
  • Heterogeneous mixture: you can see its parts or they are spread unevenly, such as a suspension.

Solvent and solute

A solution is a homogeneous mixture made of a solvent and a solute. The solvent is the component present in the greatest amount; the solute is the component present in the smaller amount that spreads through it.

In sugar water, water is the solvent and sugar is the solute. In air, nitrogen is the solvent because it is about 78% of air, and the other gases are solutes.

Solution, colloid or suspension?

The difference is particle size. Solution particles are atoms, ions or tiny molecules, so they are invisible, pass through filters, never settle and do not scatter light.

Colloid particles are larger: they scatter light so a beam is visible inside (the Tyndall effect), they do not settle, yet they pass through ordinary filter paper. Suspension particles are much larger: they settle when left still and filter paper traps them.

Worked examples

Classify each: (a) sugar in water, (b) sand in water, (c) milk.
Show the solution
  1. Sugar in water: sugar dissolves and its molecules spread among water molecules, so the mixture is uniform and never settles.
  2. Sand in water: sand grains are large and settle, so it is a suspension.
  3. Milk: its particles are larger than solution particles, scatter light and do not settle quickly, so it is a colloid.
Answer: (a) solution, (b) suspension, (c) colloid.
Air is a gas solution with 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen and 1% other gases. Which is the solvent, and which are the solutes?
Show the solution
  1. The solvent is the component present in the greatest amount.
  2. Compare: 78%>21%>1%.
  3. So nitrogen is the greatest and the others are smaller.
Answer: The solvent is nitrogen; the solutes are oxygen and the other gases.
A mixture passes through filter paper, does not settle when left still, and a light beam is visible inside it. What is it?
Show the solution
  1. It passes through filter paper, so its particles are not large like a suspension.
  2. The beam is visible, so the particles scatter light. A true solution would not do this.
  3. Medium particles that do not settle and scatter light are the signs of a colloid.
Answer: A colloid.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking a solution must be a liquid. Solutions can be gases like air or solids like alloys.
  • Calling milk a solution because it looks uniform. Light gives it away: the beam is visible, so it is a colloid.
  • Thinking the solvent is the component with the bigger particles. The solvent is the component present in the greatest amount.
  • Saying filter paper separates a colloid. Its particles pass through; only a suspension is trapped.
Summary
  • A solution is a homogeneous mixture: solvent (greater amount) and solute (smaller amount).
  • Suspension: large particles that settle and are filtered out.
  • Colloid: medium particles that scatter light and do not settle.
  • Solution: tiny particles that neither scatter light nor get filtered.

Solubility and Types of Solutions

What you will learn
  • Define solubility and read a solubility curve
  • Classify a solution as unsaturated, saturated or supersaturated
  • Calculate the mass dissolved and the mass crystallizing on cooling

Solubility

Solubility is the greatest mass of solute that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a stated temperature, usually written in grams per 100g of water. A salt with solubility 36g per 100g water at 20∘C means the water accepts no more than that.

Three types of solution

Compare the dissolved mass with the solubility at the same temperature:

  • Unsaturated: less solute than the solubility, so it can take more.
  • Saturated: solute equals the solubility exactly; it takes no more.
  • Supersaturated: more solute than the solubility. It is unstable, made by carefully cooling a saturated solution, and one small crystal added makes the excess crystallize at once.

Cooling and crystallizing

The solubility of most salts rises with temperature. If a saturated solution is cooled, the new solubility is lower, and whatever exceeds it comes out as crystals.

The mass of crystals equals the solute held at the high temperature minus the solute still dissolved at the low temperature.

Laws and rules

Solubility and amount of solventmax solute=S×100msolvent​​S = solubility in g per 100g water; msolvent​ in g. The result is the mass of solute in g.

Worked examples

A salt has solubility 36g per 100g water at 20∘C. 30g of it is dissolved in 100g water. What type of solution is it?
Show the solution
  1. The maximum in 100g water is 36g.
  2. The actual solute is 30g.
  3. Compare: 30<36.
Answer: Unsaturated; it can take 6g more.
A salt has solubility 40g per 100g water. What is the greatest mass that dissolves in 50g water?
Show the solution
  1. The water is half of 100g, so the mass is half the solubility.
  2. 40×10050​=20.
Answer: 20g.
A saturated solution of KNOX3​ in 200g water at 60∘C (solubility 110g per 100g water) is cooled to 20∘C (solubility 30g per 100g water). How many grams crystallize?
Show the solution
  1. At 60∘C, 200g water holds 110×2=220g.
  2. At 20∘C, 200g water keeps 30×2=60g dissolved.
  3. Crystals: 220−60=160g.
  4. Check: with 100g water 110−30=80g would crystallize; double the water, double the crystals: 160.
Answer: 160g.
Common mistakes
  • Subtracting the two solubilities without multiplying by the amount of water. First scale each solubility by water mass ÷ 100.
  • Calling a solution saturated just because it contains solute. Saturated means it has reached the solubility exactly at that temperature.
  • Forgetting that solubility changes with temperature. Always compare at the same temperature.
  • Thinking a supersaturated solution is stable. One small crystal is enough to crystallize the excess.
Summary
  • Solubility is the greatest mass dissolving in a given amount of solvent at one temperature.
  • Unsaturated is below the solubility, saturated equals it, supersaturated is above it.
  • On cooling a saturated solution, the difference between the solubilities, scaled by the water, crystallizes.

What Affects Dissolving?

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Explain «like dissolves like» and how salts dissolve
  • Separate what changes the speed of dissolving from what changes solubility
  • Apply the effect of temperature and pressure, and Henry's law, to gases

Like dissolves like

Polar solvents dissolve polar solutes and nonpolar solvents dissolve nonpolar solutes. Water is polar, so it dissolves sugar, alcohol and salts, but not oil, which is nonpolar.

The reason is that dissolving happens when the attractions between solvent and solute make up for the attractions each had on its own, and this works best when the forces are of the same kind: polar with polar, nonpolar with nonpolar.

Dissolving ionic compounds

Table salt NaCl contains positive and negative ions. The polar water molecule has a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end, so it surrounds the ions and pulls them out of the crystal (hydration).

The crystal breaks apart and the ions spread out, each wrapped in water molecules, giving a solution that conducts electricity. Not every salt dissolves easily: if the ions attract each other in the crystal more strongly than water attracts them, the salt is only slightly soluble.

What speeds dissolving and what changes solubility

Stirring and crushing the solute increase the contact area, so they only speed up dissolving; they do not change the greatest amount that dissolves.

Temperature does change solubility: most solids become more soluble when heated, so a saturated solution of such a solid becomes unsaturated when heated. A few salts, such as table salt, change only slightly.

Solubility of gases

Gases behave opposite to most solids: when the temperature rises, solubility falls because hotter molecules escape from the liquid. That is why a warm fizzy drink loses its bubbles faster.

Pressure raises gas solubility. Henry's law says the solubility of a gas is directly proportional to its pressure above the liquid at constant temperature. Opening a bottle lowers the pressure, so the gas leaves as bubbles.

Laws and rules

Henry's lawP1​S1​​=P2​S2​​S = solubility of the gas (g/L), P = pressure of the gas above the liquid (atm). Temperature constant.

Worked examples

Which solvent suits iodine IX2​ (a nonpolar molecule) better: water or carbon tetrachloride CClX4​?
Show the solution
  1. Iodine is nonpolar, so we look for a solvent of the same kind.
  2. Water is polar, while CClX4​ is a nonpolar molecule.
  3. Like dissolves like, so choose the nonpolar solvent.
Answer: Carbon tetrachloride CClX4​.
A gas has solubility 2g/L in water at 1atm. What is it at 3atm, same temperature?
Show the solution
  1. Solubility is directly proportional to pressure.
  2. The pressure is 3 times larger: 13​=3.
  3. New solubility: 2×3=6.
Answer: 6g/L.
A fizzy drink holds carbon dioxide with solubility 3g/L at 5atm. The bottle is opened and the pressure becomes 1atm. How many grams of gas leave each liter?
Show the solution
  1. Write the ratio: 53​=1S2​​.
  2. New solubility: S2​=53​=0.6g/L.
  3. The gas that leaves is the difference: 3−0.6=2.4.
  4. Check: the pressure became one fifth, so the solubility became one fifth: 3÷5=0.6.
Answer: 2.4g from each liter.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking stirring increases solubility. Stirring only speeds up dissolving.
  • Applying «heating increases solubility» to gases. Gases become less soluble when heated.
  • Believing water dissolves everything. Water is polar, so it does not dissolve oils and fats.
  • Using Henry's law while the temperature changes. It holds at constant temperature only.
Summary
  • Like dissolves like: polar in polar, nonpolar in nonpolar.
  • Water pulls ions out of a salt crystal and surrounds them, so the salt dissolves.
  • Stirring and crushing only speed dissolving; temperature changes solubility.
  • For a gas, heating lowers solubility and pressure raises it in direct proportion (Henry).

Molarity and Dilution

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Calculate molarity from moles and volume
  • Find the mass of solute needed to prepare a solution of known concentration
  • Use the dilution equation to find a concentration or a volume

What is molarity?

Molarity is the number of moles of solute in one liter of solution; its unit is mol/L, written M. A 2M solution holds 2mol of solute in every liter.

Note the denominator is the volume of the whole solution in liters, not of the water alone. If the volume is given in mL, divide by 1000.

From mass to moles

Often the mass of solute is given. Convert it to moles by dividing by the molar mass. For example the molar mass of NaOH is 40g/mol, so every 40g is one mole.

Preparing a solution

To prepare a known volume at a known concentration: moles = molarity × volume in liters, then mass = moles × molar mass. Dissolve the solute in a little water, then add water up to the mark in a volumetric flask.

Dilution

Dilution means adding water to a concentrated solution. The water adds volume but no solute, so the moles of solute stay the same and the concentration falls.

Since moles = concentration × volume, the moles before dilution equal the moles after.

Laws and rules

MolarityM=Vn​M in mol/L, n = moles of solute, V = volume of solution in liters.
Molesn=Mr​m​m = mass in g, Mr​ = molar mass in g/mol.
DilutionM1​V1​=M2​V2​M = concentration (molarity), V = volume (same unit on both sides). 1 = before dilution, 2 = after.

Worked examples

The molar mass of NaOH is 40g/mol. 20g of it is dissolved in water to make 500mL of solution. What is its molarity?
Show the solution
  1. Moles: 4020​=0.5mol.
  2. Volume in liters: 1000500​=0.5L.
  3. Molarity: 0.50.5​=1.
Answer: 1M.
How many grams of NaOH (molar mass 40g/mol) are needed to prepare 0.5L of a 0.2M solution?
Show the solution
  1. Moles: 0.2×0.5=0.1mol.
  2. Mass: 0.1×40=4g.
  3. Check: 404​=0.1mol.
Answer: 4g.
How many mL of a 6M concentrated solution are needed to make 300mL of 1M solution? How much water is added?
Show the solution
  1. Dilution equation: 6×V1​=1×300.
  2. V1​=6300​=50mL.
  3. Water added: 300−50=250mL.
  4. Check: moles before 6×0.05=0.3; moles after 1×0.3=0.3.
Answer: 50mL of concentrate and 250mL of water.
Common mistakes
  • Putting milliliters into the molarity formula without converting to liters. The denominator is always liters.
  • Dividing the moles by the volume of added water instead of the final solution volume.
  • Thinking dilution changes the number of moles. Moles stay constant; volume and concentration change.
  • Mixing up mass and moles: mass must be divided by molar mass before finding molarity.
Summary
  • Molarity = moles of solute ÷ liters of solution.
  • Moles = mass ÷ molar mass, and mass = moles × molar mass.
  • Dilution keeps the moles constant: M1​V1​=M2​V2​.
  • In dilution the volume unit may be mL on both sides, but molarity itself needs liters.

Molality and Percent Concentration

What you will learn
  • Calculate molality and see why temperature does not affect it
  • Calculate percent by mass
  • Calculate percent by volume

Molality

Molality is the number of moles of solute in one kilogram of solvent; its unit is mol/kg. A solution with 0.5mol of solute in 250g of water, that is 0.25kg, has molality 0.250.5​=2mol/kg.

The difference from molarity is that the denominator is the mass of solvent, not the volume of solution. Mass does not change on heating but volume expands, so molality stays constant when the temperature changes while molarity does not.

Percent by mass

It tells the mass of solute in every 100g of solution: mass of solute ÷ mass of solution × 100. The mass of solution = mass of solute + mass of solvent, so do not forget to add them.

Percent by volume

It is used when the solute is a liquid, such as alcohol in water: volume of solute ÷ volume of solution × 100. The volume here is the final volume of the solution, not the volume of the water.

Laws and rules

Molalitym=kg of solventnsolute​​Unit mol/kg.
Percent by mass%=msolution​msolute​​×100msolution​=msolute​+msolvent​, in g.
Percent by volume%=Vsolution​Vsolute​​×100Both volumes in the same unit (mL).

Worked examples

20g of salt is dissolved in 80g of water. What is the percent by mass of the salt?
Show the solution
  1. Mass of solution: 20+80=100g.
  2. Percent: 10020​×100=20%.
Answer: 20%.
40mL of alcohol is added to water to make 200mL of solution. What is the percent by volume?
Show the solution
  1. The final volume of the solution is 200mL.
  2. 20040​=0.2.
  3. 0.2×100=20%.
Answer: 20% alcohol by volume.
11.7g of NaCl (molar mass 58.5g/mol) is dissolved in 400g of water. What is the molality?
Show the solution
  1. Moles: 58.511.7​=0.2mol.
  2. Mass of water in kg: 1000400​=0.4kg.
  3. Molality: 0.40.2​=0.5.
  4. Check: 0.5×0.4=0.2mol.
Answer: 0.5mol/kg.
Common mistakes
  • Dividing by the volume of the solution in molality. The denominator is the mass of the solvent in kilograms.
  • Forgetting to convert grams of water to kilograms.
  • Dividing the solute mass by the solvent mass alone in the percent. The denominator is the whole solution.
  • Thinking molality changes on heating. Mass is constant, so molality is constant.
Summary
  • Molality = moles of solute ÷ kg of solvent, and it does not depend on temperature.
  • Percent by mass = mass of solute ÷ mass of solution × 100.
  • Percent by volume = volume of solute ÷ volume of solution × 100.
  • Mass of solution = solute + solvent.

Colligative Properties: Boiling and Freezing

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Know that colligative properties depend on the number of particles, not their kind
  • Count the particles each solute produces
  • Calculate boiling-point elevation and freezing-point depression

What are colligative properties?

Colligative properties are properties of a solution that depend on the number of solute particles in a given amount of solvent, not on what the particles are. Boiling-point elevation and freezing-point depression are examples.

The reason is that solute particles hinder solvent molecules from escaping as vapour (the vapour pressure falls), so the solution needs more heat to boil and more cooling to freeze.

Dissolving a nonvolatile solute in water makes the solution boil above 100∘C and freeze below 0∘C. This is why salt is spread on icy roads: it lowers the freezing point of water, so the ice melts.

Counting particles

Molecular substances such as sugar and glucose do not break up, so each unit gives one particle. Ionic compounds dissociate into ions: NaCl gives 2 (NaX+ and ClX−), CaClX2​ gives 3, and Al(NOX3​)X3​ gives 4 (AlX3+ and three NOX3​X−).

The effective particle concentration = molality × number of particles per unit. The solution with more particles boils higher and freezes lower.

Calculation

The change in boiling or freezing point = constant × molality × number of particles per unit. For water, Kb​=0.512∘C⋅kg/mol and Kf​=1.86∘C⋅kg/mol.

New freezing point = 0 minus the depression; new boiling point = 100 plus the elevation.

Laws and rules

Boiling-point elevationΔTb​=Kb​miKb​=0.512∘C⋅kg/mol for water, m = molality (mol/kg), i = particles per unit. The result is in ∘C.
Freezing-point depressionΔTf​=Kf​miKf​=1.86∘C⋅kg/mol for water.

Worked examples

Rank these aqueous solutions, each 0.1mol/kg, from highest to lowest freezing point: glucose, NaCl, CaClX2​.
Show the solution
  1. Glucose is molecular: one particle, so 0.1 particle concentration.
  2. NaCl: two particles, so 0.2.
  3. CaClX2​: three particles, so 0.3.
  4. More particles means a larger drop in freezing point.
Answer: Glucose, then NaCl, then CaClX2​ (lowest freezing point).
NaCl is dissolved in water to give 0.5mol/kg. What is the freezing point? (Kf​=1.86∘C⋅kg/mol)
Show the solution
  1. Particles from NaCl: 2.
  2. Depression: 1.86×0.5×2=1.86∘C.
  3. Freezing point: 0−1.86=−1.86∘C.
Answer: −1.86∘C.
By how much does the boiling point rise for a 1mol/kg NaCl solution in water, and what is the boiling point? (Kb​=0.512∘C⋅kg/mol)
Show the solution
  1. Particles: 2.
  2. Elevation: 0.512×1×2=1.024∘C.
  3. Boiling point: 100+1.024=101.024∘C.
  4. Check: without dissociation it would be 0.512; two ions double it.
Answer: It rises 1.024∘C; boiling at about 101.02∘C.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking a heavier solute or bigger molecule has a larger effect. The effect follows the number of particles, not their mass or kind.
  • Treating NaCl as one particle. It gives two ions, which doubles the effect.
  • Assuming the higher concentration always has the larger effect. Compare particle counts: 0.2mol/kg sugar has the same effect as 0.1mol/kg NaCl.
  • Adding the depression to 0 instead of subtracting it; freezing point goes down.
Summary
  • Colligative properties depend on the number of particles, not their kind.
  • Ions multiply the count: NaCl gives two, CaClX2​ three.
  • A solute raises the boiling point and lowers the freezing point.
  • Change = constant × molality × particles per unit.
