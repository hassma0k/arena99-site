Binomial Naming and Taxonomic Levels

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Explain why scientists use a two-word scientific name
  • Write a scientific name correctly
  • Order the taxonomic levels from broadest to narrowest
  • Tell which of two organisms is more closely related from the levels they share

Why do we need scientific names?

Common names change from country to country, and the same common name may be used for two different organisms. A scientific name is the same in every language, so scientists everywhere understand it without confusion.

Linnaeus created binomial naming: every species gets a name of two words, the first is the genus and the second is the species.

How is a scientific name written?

Example: the lion is Panthera leo and the tiger is Panthera tigris. The first word (genus) is the same because they belong to one genus; the second word (species) tells them apart.

  • The first word (genus) starts with a capital letter.
  • The second word (species) is all lowercase.
  • The whole name is italicised, or underlined when handwritten.
  • Genus comes first, then species, never the reverse.
  • The second word alone is not a species name; both words are needed, because the same second word can recur in different genera.

Taxonomic levels

Scientists arrange organisms in graded levels, each inside the one above it. A high level is broad and holds many different organisms; the lower we go, the fewer the organisms and the more they resemble each other.

From broadest to narrowest: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.

  • Species: the narrowest level; its members are very alike and can breed to produce fertile offspring.
  • Domain: the broadest level; it holds several kingdoms whose species are very different.

Relationship from shared levels

The narrower the level two organisms share, the closer their relationship. Two organisms in the same family are more closely related than two that share only an order, because the family lies inside the order.

Worked examples

Order these levels from broadest to narrowest: genus, phylum, species, kingdom, family.
Show the solution
  1. Recall the order: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
  2. Keep only the requested levels in the same order.
Answer: Kingdom, phylum, family, genus, species.
Organisms A and B share a family; organisms C and D share only an order. Which pair is more closely related?
Show the solution
  1. Find the shared level: family for the first pair, order for the second.
  2. Family is narrower than order; it lies inside it.
  3. Sharing a narrower level means more similarity and a more recent common ancestor.
Answer: A and B are more closely related.
The lion is Panthera leo, the tiger Panthera tigris, the house cat Felis catus. What is the narrowest level shared by lion and tiger? Do lion and cat share a genus?
Show the solution
  1. The first word is the same for lion and tiger (Panthera), so they share a genus.
  2. The species differ, so the narrowest shared level is the genus.
  3. The cat’s name starts with a different word (Felis), so its genus differs from the lion’s.
  4. Lion and cat share a broader level, the cat family.
Answer: Genus for lion and tiger; no, lion and cat are in different genera.
Common mistakes
  • Capitalising both words, or writing the genus in lowercase. Correct: genus capitalised, species all lowercase.
  • Thinking a common name is more precise than a scientific one. The scientific name is universal; common names vary by language and region.
  • Reversing the order of levels. Remember: domain is the biggest group, species the smallest.
  • Assuming two organisms in one genus are one species. A genus can hold several species, each with its own second word.
Summary
  • A scientific name has two words, genus then species, and is the same worldwide.
  • Genus is capitalised, species is lowercase, the name is italic.
  • Broadest to narrowest: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
  • The narrower the shared level, the closer the relationship.

Cladograms and Dichotomous Keys

What you will learn
  • Read a cladogram and find the closest relative
  • Understand derived traits and branch points
  • Use a dichotomous key to identify an organism
  • Know how molecular evidence changed modern classification

Cladograms

A cladogram is a tree showing how groups of organisms descended from common ancestors. Each branch point stands for the most recent common ancestor of the two branches leaving it.

The golden rule: relatedness is measured by how recent the shared branch point is, not by outer appearance or by the order of organisms on the page. Two organisms sharing the most recent branch point are the closest relatives.

  • Derived trait: a new trait that appeared in an ancestor and was inherited by all its descendants; it is marked on the branch.
  • The organism that split off first is the most distant relative of the rest.
  • The two branches at a branch point can be swapped without changing relatedness.

Dichotomous keys

A dichotomous key is a tool for identifying an unknown organism. Each step gives two opposite statements; you choose the one that fits the organism, and it sends you to the next step or to the organism’s name.

It works by choosing between two contrasting descriptions, such as “has a backbone” versus “has no backbone”, and each step rules out a group of possibilities.

Modern classification

Classification once relied on outward appearance, but similar looks can mislead. Today scientists compare DNA, proteins and other biochemical evidence, because the number of differences shows true relatedness.

Modern classification is built on cladograms that reflect evolutionary history, so each group contains organisms descended from one common ancestor.

The number of large groups has changed with new evidence: Linnaeus began with two kingdoms, plants and animals, then there were five and later six. Comparing ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequences showed that bacteria and archaea are very different, so the level of domain was added above kingdom.

Worked examples

In the cladogram shown, which organism is the closest relative of the mouse?
Show the solution
  1. Look for the organism sharing the most recent branch point with the mouse.
  2. Mouse and lizard leave the same coloured point, the most recent one.
  3. The frog splits off at an older point, and the fish at the oldest.
Answer: The lizard.
In the same cladogram, which organisms inherited the trait “limbs with digits”? Why does the fish lack it?
Show the solution
  1. The mark for the trait sits on the branch after the first split, before the frog separates.
  2. Everything that branches after this mark inherits it: frog, lizard and mouse.
  3. The fish split off before the trait appeared, so it did not inherit it.
Answer: Frog, lizard and mouse; the fish split off before it appeared.
Dichotomous key: (1) a: backbone, go to 2. b: no backbone, go to 3. (2) a: feathers: falcon. b: fins: fish. (3) a: six legs: bee. b: eight legs: spider. Which organism has no backbone and eight legs? How many steps identify the falcon?
Show the solution
  1. Our organism has no backbone: choose 1b and go to 3.
  2. It has eight legs: choose 3b, which gives the spider.
  3. For the falcon: 1a, then 2, then 2a, which is two steps.
Answer: The spider; the falcon is identified after two steps.
Common mistakes
  • Judging relatedness by how close two organisms are drawn. Correct: look only at the most recent shared branch point.
  • Thinking the organism at the last tip is the most advanced. All tips are modern; no organism is “higher” on a cladogram.
  • Choosing both statements in a key step. Choose only one, the one that fits.
  • Classifying by looks alone. Two organisms may look alike yet be distant relatives.
Summary
  • A cladogram is a tree of ancestors; a branch point is the most recent common ancestor.
  • The closest relatives share the most recent branch point.
  • A derived trait is a new trait inherited by all descendants of the ancestor where it arose.
  • A dichotomous key works by choosing between two opposite descriptions at each step.
  • Modern classification relies on DNA and molecular evidence.

Domains of Life and Kingdoms

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Name the three domains and what sets each apart
  • Tell prokaryotes from eukaryotes
  • Place an organism in its kingdom from its features
  • List the features of the plant and animal kingdoms

Prokaryotes and eukaryotes

The biggest difference between cells is the nucleus. A prokaryotic cell has no membrane-bound nucleus, so its genetic material lies free in the cytoplasm; it has no membrane-bound organelles, is small and is usually single-celled. A eukaryotic cell keeps its genetic material in a nucleus and has membrane-bound organelles.

  • Prokaryotes: bacteria and archaea.
  • Eukaryotes: protists, fungi, plants and animals.

The three domains

A domain is the broadest group in classification, above the kingdom. There are three: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya.

Archaea resemble bacteria in being prokaryotes but differ from them chemically, and many live in harsh places such as hot springs and very salty lakes.

The six kingdoms

Organisms are spread over six kingdoms: Eubacteria (domain Bacteria), Archaebacteria (domain Archaea), and four kingdoms in domain Eukarya: protists, fungi, plants and animals.

  • Eubacteria: prokaryotes whose cell walls contain peptidoglycan; they live almost everywhere.
  • Archaebacteria: prokaryotes whose walls lack peptidoglycan; many live in harsh environments.
  • Protists: eukaryotes, mostly single-celled (a few, such as large algae, are multicellular), and very varied: some resemble animals, such as amoeba, and some resemble plants, such as algae.
  • Fungi: eukaryotes with chitin walls that absorb food from their surroundings instead of making it.

The plant and animal kingdoms

Plants are multicellular eukaryotes whose cells have cellulose walls and chloroplasts. They are autotrophs that make food by photosynthesis and do not move from place to place.

Animals are multicellular eukaryotes whose cells have no cell wall and no chloroplasts. They are heterotrophs that ingest food, and most can move at some stage of life.

Worked examples

A single-celled organism with no nucleus lives in a hot spring, and its cell wall chemistry differs from bacterial walls. Which domain?
Show the solution
  1. Single-celled with no nucleus: a prokaryote, so bacteria or archaea.
  2. A harsh habitat such as a hot spring and a chemically different wall point to archaea.
Answer: Domain Archaea.
A multicellular eukaryote has cell walls, no chloroplasts, and absorbs food from its surroundings. Which kingdom? Why is it not a plant?
Show the solution
  1. Multicellular eukaryote: rules out bacteria and archaea.
  2. It absorbs food instead of making it, which is a fungus feature.
  3. A plant needs chloroplasts and makes food by photosynthesis; this organism has none.
Answer: Fungi, because it does not make its own food.
A single-celled organism has a nucleus and chloroplasts, makes its food by photosynthesis, and lives in a pond. Is it a plant? Which kingdom?
Show the solution
  1. It has a nucleus, so it is a eukaryote, in domain Eukarya.
  2. A plant is multicellular and this organism is single-celled, so it is not a plant even though it photosynthesises.
  3. It is not an animal or a fungus because it makes its own food; a eukaryote that fits none of the other three kingdoms goes in the protists.
Answer: No, it is not a plant; it is a protist in domain Eukarya.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking archaea are animals or eukaryotes. They are prokaryotes like bacteria.
  • Putting fungi with plants. Fungi do not photosynthesise, and their walls are chitin, not cellulose.
  • Thinking every single-celled organism is a bacterium. Some single-celled organisms are eukaryotes (protists).
  • Mixing up domain and kingdom: the domain is broader, and kingdoms lie under it.
  • Thinking every photosynthetic organism is a plant. Single-celled algae photosynthesise and are protists.
Summary
  • There are three domains: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya.
  • Prokaryotes have no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotes have both.
  • Six kingdoms: Eubacteria, Archaebacteria, protists, fungi, plants, animals.
  • Plants are autotrophs with cellulose walls; animals are heterotrophs with no walls.

Viruses and Their Two Cycles

What you will learn
  • Describe the structure of a virus
  • Tell a virus from a bacterium
  • Put the stages of the lytic cycle in order
  • Explain what makes the lysogenic cycle different

What is a virus?

A virus is a very tiny particle, much smaller than a bacterium. It is not a cell: it has no cytoplasm, no ribosomes and no means of producing energy.

Its structure is simple: genetic material (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein coat called the capsid. Some viruses have an extra fatty envelope, and some have a tail and fibres that attach them to a cell.

A virus cannot reproduce alone; it needs a host cell and uses its machinery. That is why many scientists place viruses on the border of life.

Virus versus bacterium

A bacterium is a complete living cell; it feeds and reproduces by itself through binary fission. A virus is not a cell and reproduces only inside a living cell.

  • Bacterium: prokaryotic cell with cytoplasm and ribosomes, reproduces alone, is killed by antibiotics.
  • Virus: genetic material in a capsid, reproduces only inside a host, is not affected by antibiotics.

The lytic cycle

In the lytic cycle the virus quickly destroys the cell. It attaches to the cell and injects its genetic material, and the cell builds new viral parts instead of doing its usual work. The parts then assemble into complete viruses, and the cell bursts (lysis), releasing viruses that go on to infect other cells. Scientists often study these cycles in viruses that infect bacteria, called bacteriophages.

The lysogenic cycle

In the lysogenic cycle the cell is not destroyed at once. The virus’s genetic material joins the host cell’s chromosome, becoming part of it, and is copied along with it every time the cell divides, so all the daughter cells carry it and stay alive.

The virus stays quiet until a trigger such as stress or a harmful substance switches it to the lytic cycle.

Laws and rules

Virus multiplication in the lytic cyclenew viruses=infected cells×yield per cellYield per cell is the number of new viruses released when one cell bursts.

Worked examples

A particle has ribosomes and cytoplasm, reproduces by itself in nutrient broth, and is killed by an antibiotic. Is it a virus or a bacterium?
Show the solution
  1. A virus has no ribosomes or cytoplasm; this particle has both.
  2. A virus cannot reproduce outside a living cell; this one reproduces alone.
  3. An antibiotic kills bacteria and does not affect viruses.
Answer: A bacterium.
Order the lytic cycle: release of viruses as the cell bursts, attachment to the cell, assembly of parts into whole viruses, injection of genetic material, cell making viral parts.
Show the solution
  1. It always begins with attachment to the cell.
  2. Then the virus injects its genetic material.
  3. Then the cell makes viral parts, following the viral instructions.
  4. Then the parts assemble, and finally the cell bursts and the viruses are released.
Answer: Attachment, injection, making parts, assembly, release by bursting.
One virus infects a cell in the lytic cycle and the cell releases 100 viruses. If each virus infects a new cell and each cell releases 100 viruses, how many new viruses are there after the second cycle? In the lysogenic cycle, how many cells carry the viral genetic material after three divisions of one infected cell?
Show the solution
  1. After the first cycle: 1×100=100 viruses.
  2. Second cycle: 100×100=10000 viruses.
  3. In the lysogenic cycle the genetic material is copied at each division, so carrier cells double: 1→2→4→8.
  4. After three divisions: 23=8 cells carry it, and none has died.
Answer: 10000 viruses after the second cycle; 8 cells in the lysogenic cycle.
Common mistakes
  • Treating a virus as a cell. A virus has no cytoplasm, no ribosomes and no independent life.
  • Thinking antibiotics treat viruses. Antibiotics act on bacteria only.
  • Thinking the lysogenic cycle kills the cell at once. The cell lives, divides and carries the viral material.
  • Mixing the two cycles: bursting of the cell belongs to the lytic cycle only.
Summary
  • A virus is genetic material in a protein capsid, and it is not a cell.
  • A virus reproduces only inside a host cell; a bacterium is a cell that reproduces alone.
  • Lytic cycle: attach, inject, make parts, assemble, burst.
  • Lysogenic cycle: genetic material joins the host chromosome and is copied with each division until triggered.

Natural Selection and Antibiotic Resistance

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Explain the ideas of natural selection in order
  • Define fitness and compute the proportion of survivors
  • Explain why bacteria resist antibiotics
  • Tell a change in an individual from a change in a population

The ideas of natural selection

Darwin proposed that species change over generations by natural selection, which rests on four steps in sequence:

  • Overproduction: organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support.
  • Genetic variation: individuals differ in their traits, and some of the differences are inherited.
  • Competition: resources are scarce, so offspring compete for food and space.
  • Differential survival and reproduction: individuals with better-suited traits survive and leave more offspring, so their traits spread.

Fitness and variation

In biology, fitness is not strength or size but an individual’s success in leaving surviving offspring. The fittest individual passes on its genes to the next generation more than others do. An inherited trait that raises an individual’s chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment is called an adaptation.

Variation comes from mutations, which arise at random, and from sexual reproduction, which shuffles genes. Natural selection does not create a new trait; it selects among traits already present in the population. Without variation, selection cannot work.

What changes is the population over generations, not the individual during its life; an individual cannot change its genes to suit its habitat.

Antibiotics and resistance

An antibiotic is a drug that kills bacteria or stops their growth; it does not affect viruses. In a large bacterial population a few individuals carry a mutation that makes them resistant, and the mutation arose long before the drug was used.

When the antibiotic is used it becomes a selective pressure: the sensitive bacteria die, the resistant ones survive and multiply, and after a few generations most of the population is resistant. Stopping treatment early or misusing the drug leaves resistant survivors and speeds this selection up.

Laws and rules

Proportion of survivorsproportion=totalsurvivors​Survivors and total are both counts of individuals; compare the proportion before and after selection to see which trait increases.

Worked examples

A beetle population has 40 green and 10 brown beetles. Birds eat 30 green and 5 brown. How many of each colour remain? What share of the population was brown before and after?
Show the solution
  1. Green remaining: 40−30=10.
  2. Brown remaining: 10−5=5.
  3. Before: total 40+10=50, brown share 5010​=51​.
  4. After: total 10+5=15, brown share 155​=31​.
  5. The share rose from one fifth to one third because brown beetles were better hidden from the birds.
Answer: 10 green and 5 brown remain; the brown share rose from 51​ to 31​.
A bacterial population holds one million cells, of which 10 are resistant to an antibiotic. The antibiotic is given and all sensitive cells die. If each surviving cell divides every 20 minutes, how many cells are there after one hour?
Show the solution
  1. Only the resistant cells survive, so there are 10.
  2. One hour is 60 minutes, so 60÷20=3 divisions.
  3. Each division doubles the number: 10×2×2×2=80.
  4. All 80 cells are resistant, so the population went from a tiny fraction resistant to fully resistant.
Answer: 80 cells, all resistant.
Mouse A is big and strong but leaves 2 surviving offspring; mouse B is small but leaves 6. Which has higher fitness? Would mouse A’s fur change colour to match the soil if it moved to a new habitat?
Show the solution
  1. Fitness is measured by surviving offspring, not size or strength.
  2. Mouse B left 6, which is more than 2.
  3. An individual cannot change its genes to suit its habitat; change appears in the population over generations through selection.
Answer: Mouse B has higher fitness; no, an individual does not change its genes.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the strongest or biggest has the highest fitness. Fitness is the number of surviving offspring.
  • Thinking an individual adapts and passes on what it acquired. Traits come from inherited genes, not from effort during life.
  • Thinking the antibiotic causes the resistance mutation. The mutation exists beforehand; the drug only selects it.
  • Thinking mutations happen because the organism “needs” them. Mutations are random; the environment does the selecting.
Summary
  • Natural selection: overproduction, genetic variation, competition, survival and reproduction of the best suited.
  • No selection without genetic variation; variation comes from mutation and gene shuffling.
  • Fitness is success in leaving surviving offspring.
  • An antibiotic kills sensitive bacteria so resistant ones spread: this is natural selection.
  • Populations evolve, not individuals.

Evidence for Evolution

What you will learn
  • Recall the main evidence for evolution
  • Tell homologous from analogous structures
  • Explain vestigial structures and convergent evolution
  • Read biochemical evidence to judge relatedness

The fossil record

Fossils are remains or traces of ancient organisms preserved in sedimentary rock. In undisturbed layers the lower layer is older and the upper is younger, so the order of fossils shows how organisms changed over time. Scientists estimate the actual age of rocks by radiometric dating, which relies on radioactive elements that decay at a steady rate.

Some fossils combine features of two groups; these are transitional fossils and point to an evolutionary link between the two groups.

Homologous and vestigial structures

Homologous structures are similar in structure although their functions differ, such as the bones of a human arm, a bat wing and a whale flipper. Their similarity is evidence of a common ancestor that passed the structure down.

Vestigial structures are shrunken organs that have lost their original function or most of it, such as the small pelvic bones of whales and the human tailbone. Their presence shows the organism has an ancestor in which these organs worked.

Another line of evidence is the similarity of early embryos in vertebrates: all have pharyngeal arches and a tail early on, which points to a common ancestor.

Convergent evolution and analogous structures

Two distant organisms may come to look alike, or work alike, because they live in similar environments and nature selects similar solutions. This is convergent evolution, and it produces analogous structures: the same function but a different origin, such as a bird wing and an insect wing, or the streamlined body of a dolphin and a shark.

So similar function alone does not show kinship, whereas similar internal structure does.

Biochemical evidence

Scientists compare DNA and the order of amino acids in proteins such as cytochrome c. The fewer the differences between two organisms, the closer their relationship and the more recently they separated from their common ancestor. These are numbers, so they settle what appearance alone cannot.

Worked examples

What kind of structure is each pair: (a) a human arm and a bat wing, (b) a dolphin flipper and a fish fin?
Show the solution
  1. (a) The bone structure is similar but the functions differ (grasping and flying): homologous structures, evidence of a common ancestor.
  2. (b) The function is the same (swimming) but the origin differs: a dolphin is a mammal and a fish is not, so these are analogous structures from convergent evolution.
Answer: (a) Homologous; (b) analogous, from convergent evolution.
Differences in cytochrome c compared with human: chimpanzee 0, dog 10, chicken 13, tuna 21. Which is the closest relative of humans? Which is the most distant? What is the difference between chicken and tuna?
Show the solution
  1. Relatedness grows as differences shrink.
  2. The fewest differences belong to the chimpanzee with 0, so it is the closest.
  3. The most belong to tuna with 21, so it is the most distant.
  4. The gap between the two numbers: 21−13=8.
Answer: Closest: chimpanzee; most distant: tuna; the difference is 8.
A fossil with teeth and a long tail like reptiles and feathers like birds is found in a layer younger than one containing only reptiles. What does it show? If another fossil lies in a layer beneath it, which is older?
Show the solution
  1. The fossil combines reptile and bird features, so it is a transitional fossil.
  2. This supports an evolutionary link between reptiles and birds (a common ancestor).
  3. In undisturbed strata the lower layer is older, so the fossil in the lower layer is older.
Answer: A transitional fossil supporting an evolutionary link between reptiles and birds; the one in the lower layer is older.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up homologous and analogous. Homologous: one structure, different functions. Analogous: one function, different origin.
  • Thinking vestigial structures grow or are heavily used. They are shrunken, of little use or none.
  • Judging kinship by outward looks alone. Convergent evolution makes unrelated organisms look alike.
  • Thinking more molecular differences mean closer kinship. Fewer differences show a closer relationship.
Summary
  • Fossils show change over time; the lower layer is older.
  • Homologous structures are one structure with different functions and point to a common ancestor.
  • Vestigial structures are shrunken and have lost their function.
  • Convergent evolution gives analogous structures in similar environments without kinship.
  • Fewer differences in DNA or protein mean a closer relationship.

The Origin of Species

What you will learn
  • Define a species by the biological concept
  • Explain the steps of geographic speciation
  • Tell temporal isolation from geographic and behavioural isolation
  • Explain adaptive radiation

The species concept

A species is a group of individuals that mate in nature and produce fertile offspring. If two kinds mating produce sterile offspring (like the mule, from a horse and a donkey), they are different species.

The definition has limits: it does not fit organisms that reproduce asexually, nor fossils, whose mating cannot be tested.

Reproductive isolation

Reproductive isolation is whatever stops individuals of two populations from mating and producing fertile offspring, and it is what keeps them separate species. A physical barrier such as a mountain or river is geographic isolation: it only separates the populations, but it often paves the way for reproductive isolation.

  • Temporal isolation: the two species breed at different times (different seasons or hours), so they never meet.
  • Behavioural isolation: courtship rituals and signals differ.
  • Mechanical isolation: reproductive structures do not fit.

Geographic speciation

Geographic speciation begins when a barrier such as a mountain, river or sea splits one population into two. Gene flow between them stops, each gathers different mutations, and the different environments select different traits.

After many generations the two populations drift so far apart that if they met again they would not mate, or their offspring would not be fertile: they have become two species.

Adaptive radiation

Adaptive radiation is the quick rise of many species from one ancestor, each adapting to a different habitat or food, as with Darwin’s finches on the Galapagos Islands, whose beaks differ with their diets. It often happens when a population enters a new environment with many unused opportunities.

Worked examples

A horse and a donkey mate and produce a sterile mule. Are horse and donkey one species?
Show the solution
  1. Members of one species mate and produce fertile offspring.
  2. The mule is sterile, so the offspring cannot continue.
  3. So mating between horse and donkey does not give fertile offspring.
Answer: No, they are two different species.
Order the steps of geographic speciation: accumulation of genetic differences, appearance of two species, splitting of the population by a barrier, end of gene flow.
Show the solution
  1. First the population is split by a barrier.
  2. Then gene flow between the groups stops.
  3. Then mutations accumulate and the two environments select different traits, so differences build up.
  4. Finally reproductive isolation is complete and two species appear.
Answer: Barrier, end of gene flow, accumulation of differences, two species.
One ancestral bird reaches an island, and after many generations four species arise from it, each with a different beak suited to different food. What is this process called? If two of the species breed in different seasons on the same island, what kind of isolation is that?
Show the solution
  1. One ancestor gave several species quickly, each fitting its own habitat and food: adaptive radiation.
  2. The two species share the same place, so there is no physical barrier, but their timing differs.
  3. Different breeding times are temporal isolation.
Answer: Adaptive radiation; temporal isolation.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking two kinds become one just because they mate. The test is whether the offspring are fertile.
  • Mixing up temporal and geographic isolation: temporal is a difference in time in one place, geographic is a physical barrier.
  • Thinking a barrier changes the genes of individuals. A barrier only cuts gene flow; the differences build up over generations.
  • Mixing adaptive radiation with convergent evolution: radiation is one ancestor giving varied species, convergence is distant species growing alike.
Summary
  • A species: individuals that mate and produce fertile offspring.
  • Reproductive isolation blocks mating or fertile offspring: temporal, behavioural, mechanical; a physical barrier is geographic isolation.
  • Geographic speciation: barrier, end of gene flow, growing differences, two species.
  • Adaptive radiation: one ancestor gives many species for different habitats.
