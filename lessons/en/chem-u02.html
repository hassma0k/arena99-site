Bond types and bond polarity

What you will learn
  • Decide whether a bond is ionic, covalent or metallic from the two atoms.
  • Calculate the electronegativity difference and judge bond polarity.
  • Tell which atom carries the partial negative charge.

Why do atoms bond?

Atoms become stable by filling the outer energy level, usually with eight valence electrons. They get there by giving electrons, taking electrons, or sharing them.

The way an atom reaches stability decides the bond type.

The three types

  • Ionic bond: between a metal and a nonmetal. Valence electrons move completely from the metal to the nonmetal, forming positive and negative ions that attract. Example: NaCl.
  • Covalent bond: between two nonmetals. The atoms share one or more electron pairs. Examples: ClX2​ and HX2​O.
  • Metallic bond: between atoms of the same metal. Positive ions sit in a sea of free valence electrons. Example: a copper wire.

Electronegativity and bond polarity

Electronegativity is how strongly an atom pulls the bonding electrons toward itself. It rises as you move toward fluorine in the periodic table.

If both atoms have equal electronegativity, the electrons are shared equally: a nonpolar covalent bond, as in Cl−Cl. If they differ a little, the electrons sit closer to the more electronegative atom. It gets a partial negative charge (δ−) and the other gets a partial positive charge (δ+): a polar covalent bond.

Judging by the difference

These limits are approximate and are used for comparing. The first rule is still the type of atoms: two nonmetals always form a covalent bond, even with a large difference as in H−F, which is simply the most polar bond among those compared.

  • From 0 up to about 0.4: nonpolar covalent.
  • Above 0.4 up to about 1.7: polar covalent.
  • Above about 1.7: ionic.

Laws and rules

Electronegativity differenceΔEN=ENlarger​−ENsmaller​Subtract the smaller electronegativity from the larger one (no unit), so the result is never negative.

Worked examples

Sodium has electronegativity 0.9 and chlorine 3.0. Classify the bond in ClX2​ and in NaCl.
Show the solution
  1. In ClX2​ the atoms are identical, so the difference is 3.0−3.0=0.
  2. A difference of zero means the electrons are shared equally: nonpolar covalent.
  3. In NaCl: 3.0−0.9=2.1.
  4. 2.1 is above 1.7, and it is a metal with a nonmetal: ionic.
Answer: ClX2​ is nonpolar covalent and NaCl is ionic.
Hydrogen has electronegativity 2.1 and chlorine 3.0. Which atom carries the partial negative charge in H−Cl?
Show the solution
  1. The difference is 3.0−2.1=0.9, between 0.4 and 1.7, so the bond is polar.
  2. The electrons are pulled toward the more electronegative atom, chlorine.
  3. Chlorine carries δ− and hydrogen carries δ+.
Answer: The chlorine atom carries the partial negative charge.
Rank these bonds from most polar to least polar: C−H, H−Cl, H−F, H−H. Electronegativity: H=2.1, C=2.5, Cl=3.0, F=4.0.
Show the solution
  1. H−F: 4.0−2.1=1.9.
  2. H−Cl: 3.0−2.1=0.9.
  3. C−H: 2.5−2.1=0.4.
  4. H−H: 2.1−2.1=0.
  5. A bigger difference means a more polar bond, so the order is 1.9>0.9>0.4>0.
Answer: H−F>H−Cl>C−H>H−H
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up "polar" and "ionic". A polar bond is still covalent: the electrons are shared but sit closer to one atom. An ionic bond has a full transfer.
  • Judging only from the difference and ignoring the atoms. Two nonmetals are always covalent, and a metal with a nonmetal is ionic.
  • Giving the partial negative charge to the less electronegative atom. The partial negative charge is on the more electronegative atom.
Summary
  • Metal + nonmetal = ionic, nonmetal + nonmetal = covalent, metal + metal = metallic.
  • The electronegativity difference measures polarity: the bigger it is, the more polar the bond.
  • The more electronegative atom carries δ− and the other carries δ+.
  • A bond between identical atoms is nonpolar because the difference is zero.

Ions and ionic compounds

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Find the ion an atom forms from its place in the periodic table.
  • Write and name the formula of an ionic compound.
  • Explain ionic properties: melting point, conduction and brittleness.

How ions form

Metals lose valence electrons and become positive ions. Nonmetals gain electrons and become negative ions. The group number tells how many.

  • Group 1: charge +1, such as NaX+. Group 2: +2, such as MgX2+. Group 13: +3, such as AlX3+.
  • Group 15: charge −3. Group 16: −2, such as OX2−. Group 17: −1, such as ClX−.
  • A chlorine atom gains one electron and becomes ClX−, never ClX+.

Writing the formula

An ionic compound is electrically neutral, so the total positive charge equals the total negative charge. Choose the smallest numbers of ions that do this, the "crisscross" idea: the charge of one ion becomes the count of the other.

Polyatomic ions such as SOX4​X2−, NOX3​X− and OHX− stay together as one unit. If more than one is needed, put it in brackets, as in Ca(NOX3​)X2​.

Naming

  • Name the negative ion first, then the positive ion: sodium chloride.
  • A single-atom negative ion ends in "-ide": chloride, oxide, sulfide.
  • Polyatomic ions have their own names: sulfate SOX4​X2−, nitrate NOX3​X−, carbonate COX3​X2−, hydroxide OHX−.
  • If the metal can have more than one charge, its charge goes in brackets: iron(III) oxide is FeX2​OX3​.

Properties of ionic compounds

The ions pack into a regular crystal called a lattice, each positive ion surrounded by negative ions and the other way round. The attraction between opposite ions is very strong.

So ionic compounds are hard solids with high melting points. Bigger charges mean stronger attraction: MgO (charges 2+ and 2−) melts at about 2852 °C, while NaCl (charges 1+ and 1−) melts at about 801 °C.

A solid crystal does not conduct because its ions are fixed in place, but a melt or a water solution does because the ions can move. Ionic solids are brittle: if a layer slides, like-charged ions meet, repel, and the crystal shatters.

Laws and rules

Neutral ionic compoundcount+​×charge+​=count−​×∣charge−​∣Compare the size of the charges: adjust the number of each ion until both sides are equal, so the compound is neutral.

Worked examples

Write the formula of the compound formed by MgX2+ and ClX−, and name it.
Show the solution
  1. Magnesium has charge +2 and chloride has charge −1.
  2. Two chloride ions balance +2: 2×1=2.
  3. One magnesium ion gives 1×2=2, so the total is zero.
  4. Write the count of chloride as a subscript, and do not write the number 1.
Answer: MgClX2​, called magnesium chloride.
Write the formula of the compound formed by AlX3+ and OX2−.
Show the solution
  1. The charges are +3 and −2; their lowest common multiple is 6.
  2. Aluminium: 6÷3=2 ions, so the total is 2×3=+6.
  3. Oxide: 6÷2=3 ions, so the total is 3×2=−6.
  4. The sum is +6−6=0, so the compound is neutral.
Answer: AlX2​OX3​, called aluminium oxide.
Write the formula of calcium nitrate and of iron(III) oxide.
Show the solution
  1. Calcium nitrate: calcium is CaX2+ and nitrate is NOX3​X−.
  2. Each CaX2+ needs two nitrate ions. Nitrate is polyatomic, so it goes in brackets: Ca(NOX3​)X2​.
  3. Iron(III) oxide: iron is FeX3+ and oxide is OX2−.
  4. As with aluminium oxide, 2×3=3×2=6, so we take two iron ions and three oxide ions.
Answer: Ca(NOX3​)X2​ and FeX2​OX3​.
Common mistakes
  • Writing MgX2​Cl by putting the counts on the wrong ions. The count of each ion equals the charge size of the other, so MgX2+ gives two chlorines: MgClX2​.
  • Forgetting brackets around a polyatomic ion, or mixing counts: Na(SOX4​)X2​ is wrong; NaX2​SOX4​ is right because the 2− charge needs two NaX+.
  • Saying solid NaCl has no ions. It has ions, but they are fixed and cannot move, so it does not conduct until it melts or dissolves.
  • Writing MgX2​OX2​ for magnesium oxide. Swapping the charges 2+ and 2− gives counts that can be reduced, so reduce them to the simplest ratio: MgO.
Summary
  • Metals form positive ions and nonmetals form negative ions; the charge follows the group number.
  • An ionic compound is neutral: its charges add to zero.
  • Name the negative ion first, then the positive one; a metal with several charges gets a number in brackets.
  • High melting points come from strong ion attraction, and grow with bigger charges.
  • Ions are fixed in the solid (no conduction) but move in a melt or solution (conduction).

Metallic bonding

What you will learn
  • Describe the electron-sea model.
  • Explain why metals conduct electricity and can be hammered and drawn into wires.

The electron-sea model

Metal atoms easily give up their valence electrons. The atoms become positive ions arranged in a lattice, and the valence electrons move freely among all the ions, belonging to no single atom.

A metallic bond is the attraction between the positive ions and the sea of free electrons. It differs from an ionic bond because the negative charge here is mobile, and from a covalent bond because the electrons are not held between just two atoms.

Properties of metals

  • Conduct electricity and heat: free electrons move and carry charge and energy, even in the solid state.
  • Malleable and ductile: when hammered, layers of ions slide over each other within the electron sea, so the attraction continues and the metal does not break.
  • Lustrous: free electrons reflect light.
  • Often high melting points because the attraction between the ions and the sea is strong, and it grows with the number of valence electrons released: magnesium melts at about 650 °C, well above sodium at about 98 °C.

Worked examples

Why does a solid copper wire conduct electricity?
Show the solution
  1. Copper has a sea of valence electrons not tied to any one atom.
  2. When the wire is connected to a source, these electrons drift in one direction.
  3. Moving charges mean an electric current, even though the wire is solid.
Answer: Because its valence electrons are free to move.
A salt crystal shatters when struck, but a piece of copper just changes shape. Explain.
Show the solution
  1. In salt, when a layer slides, like-charged ions line up, repel, and the crystal breaks.
  2. In copper, when a layer slides, the positive ions are still surrounded by the same free electrons.
  3. So the attraction continues after the slide, and the shape changes without separating.
Answer: Metal ions slide within the electron sea without repelling, so metals can be hammered and drawn.
A model of a magnesium piece has 4 atoms, and each atom gives 2 valence electrons to the sea. How many positive ions and free electrons are there, and what is the total charge?
Show the solution
  1. Each atom becomes MgX2+, so there are 4 positive ions.
  2. Free electrons: 4×2=8.
  3. Positive charge 4×2=+8 and negative charge −8.
  4. The sum is +8−8=0.
Answer: 4 ions and 8 free electrons; the total charge is zero, so the metal is neutral.
Common mistakes
  • Saying a metal contains positive and negative ions like salt. All the ions are positive; the negative charge comes from the free electrons.
  • Explaining conduction by moving positive ions. The free electrons move, while the ions stay in place.
Summary
  • A metal is positive ions in a sea of free valence electrons.
  • Free electrons explain electrical and thermal conduction.
  • Layers sliding while attraction continues explains hammering and drawing.
  • A metal is electrically neutral overall.

Lewis structures and multiple bonds

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Draw the Lewis structure of a molecule in clear steps.
  • Identify bonding pairs, lone pairs, and single, double and triple bonds.
  • Name simple molecules from their formulas.

What is a Lewis structure?

A Lewis structure is a drawing that shows the valence electrons in a molecule. A line is a bonding pair (two shared electrons) and two dots on an atom are a lone pair, which is not part of a bond.

The number of valence electrons equals the group number: oxygen (group 16) has 6, hydrogen has 1, nitrogen has 5, and carbon has 4.

Steps for drawing

Follow the steps in order, and remember two exceptions: hydrogen needs only two electrons, and in BFX3​ boron has only 6 electrons around it.

  • 1) Add up the valence electrons of all the atoms (adjust for an ion charge if there is one).
  • 2) Put the least electronegative atom in the centre (hydrogen is always on the outside) and join the atoms with single bonds.
  • 3) Complete the octets of the outer atoms with lone pairs, then put what is left on the central atom.
  • 4) If the central atom has fewer than eight, turn a lone pair from an outer atom into a double or triple bond.
  • 5) Check that the electrons you drew equal the total from step 1.

Multiple bonds

A single bond is one shared pair, a double bond is two pairs, and a triple bond is three pairs. The more pairs shared between two atoms, the shorter and stronger the bond, so it takes more energy to break. That is why C≡C is shorter and stronger than C=C, which is shorter and stronger than C−C.

NX2​ has a triple bond. COX2​ has two double bonds, so it has 4 shared pairs.

Naming simple molecules

A molecule of two nonmetals is named with number prefixes (mono 1, di 2, tri 3, tetra 4, penta 5) before the second element, whose name ends in "-ide", and then the first element is named: carbon monoxide CO, carbon dioxide COX2​, sulfur dioxide SOX2​, sulfur trioxide SOX3​, carbon tetrachloride CClX4​.

The first element takes a prefix only if it has more than one atom, and "mono-" is dropped there: NX2​OX4​ is dinitrogen tetroxide and NX2​OX5​ is dinitrogen pentoxide. Reading back, the prefix before "-ide" gives the number of atoms of the second element.

Laws and rules

Number of pairs in a moleculepairs=2total valence electrons​Bonding pairs plus lone pairs in the final drawing must equal this number.

Worked examples

Draw the Lewis structure of ammonia NHX3​ and count the bonding and lone pairs around nitrogen.
Show the solution
  1. Total: 5+3×1=8 electrons, which is 4 pairs.
  2. Nitrogen is in the centre, bonded to three hydrogens: 3 bonding pairs use 6 electrons.
  3. 8−6=2 electrons remain: one lone pair on nitrogen.
  4. Around nitrogen: 3×2+2=8, so its octet is complete.
Answer: 3 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair.
Draw the Lewis structure of carbon dioxide COX2​.
Show the solution
  1. Total: 4+6+6=16 electrons, which is 8 pairs.
  2. Carbon in the centre: O−C−O with two single bonds uses 4 electrons.
  3. Give each oxygen 6 electrons (3 lone pairs), using 12, but then carbon has only 4 electrons around it.
  4. Turn one lone pair from each oxygen into a second bond: O=C=O.
  5. Check: 4 bonding pairs and 4 lone pairs, 8 pairs in total, and every atom has 8 electrons.
Answer: O=C=O with two double bonds and 4 shared pairs.
Draw the Lewis structure of nitrogen NX2​. Which is stronger, the NX2​ bond or a single H−H bond?
Show the solution
  1. Total: 5+5=10 electrons, which is 5 pairs.
  2. One single bond leaves each atom with only 6 electrons, so share more pairs.
  3. With a triple bond (3 pairs), 2 pairs remain: one lone pair on each nitrogen.
  4. Each nitrogen has 6+2=8 electrons, and the total is 3+1+1=5 pairs.
  5. A triple bond is shorter and stronger than a single bond.
Answer: N≡N with one lone pair on each nitrogen, and the triple bond of NX2​ is stronger.
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting lone pairs when counting electrons. Oxygen in water has two lone pairs and nitrogen in ammonia has one.
  • Mixing up electrons and pairs. COX2​ has 16 electrons but 8 pairs, of which 4 are shared.
  • Thinking more shared pairs make a longer, weaker bond. It is the opposite: shorter and stronger.
  • Believing every central atom always has eight. In BFX3​ boron has 6, and hydrogen needs only 2.
Summary
  • A line in a Lewis structure is a bonding pair; two dots are a lone pair.
  • Add the valence electrons first, then place them and check the total.
  • Single, double and triple bonds share one, two and three pairs.
  • More shared pairs make a shorter, stronger bond.
  • Name a two-element molecule with a prefix for its second element, such as sulfur dioxide SOX2​.

Molecular shapes and bond angles

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Predict a molecule's shape from the bonding and lone pairs around the central atom.
  • Remember the angles 180°, 120° and 109.5° and why some are smaller.
  • Rank the bond angles in CHX4​, NHX3​ and HX2​O.

The repulsion idea

Electron pairs around the central atom are negatively charged, so they repel and move as far apart as possible in space. That spreading gives the molecule its shape.

Count the "electron groups" around the central atom: each bond (single, double or triple) is one group, and each lone pair is one group.

The key shapes

  • Two groups, no lone pair: linear, angle 180∘, as in COX2​.
  • Three groups, no lone pair: trigonal planar, angle 120∘, as in BFX3​.
  • Four groups, no lone pair: tetrahedral, angle 109.5∘, as in CHX4​.
  • Three bonding pairs and one lone pair: trigonal pyramidal, angle about 107∘, as in NHX3​.
  • Three groups and one lone pair: bent, angle slightly below 120∘, as in SOX2​.
  • Two bonding pairs and two lone pairs: bent, angle about 104.5∘, as in HX2​O.
  • Five groups: trigonal bipyramidal, as in PClX5​. Six groups: octahedral, angle 90∘, as in SFX6​.

The effect of lone pairs

The shape names only the arrangement of the atoms. Lone pairs are not seen, but they still push. A lone pair takes up more room than a bonding pair, so it squeezes the bonds closer.

So with four groups the angle shrinks as lone pairs increase: CHX4​ (none) 109.5∘, then NHX3​ (one) 107∘, then HX2​O (two) 104.5∘.

This is why water is bent and not linear: the two lone pairs on oxygen repel.

Laws and rules

Number of electron groupsgroups=bonded atoms+lone pairsA double or triple bond counts as one group. This number sets the general arrangement of the pairs.

Worked examples

What is the shape of methane CHX4​, and what is its bond angle?
Show the solution
  1. Carbon is bonded to 4 hydrogens: 4 bonding pairs.
  2. Total valence electrons 4+4=8, all in bonds, so there are no lone pairs.
  3. Four groups with no lone pair spread out as far as possible.
Answer: Tetrahedral, with angle 109.5∘.
What is the shape of water HX2​O, and why is it not linear?
Show the solution
  1. Total valence electrons 6+2=8, which is 4 pairs around oxygen.
  2. Two are bonding pairs with hydrogen and two are lone pairs, so there are 4 groups.
  3. The four pairs arrange tetrahedrally, but only the three atoms draw the shape.
  4. The two lone pairs push the bonds closer, to about 104.5∘.
Answer: Bent, because oxygen has two lone pairs that repel the two bonds.
Rank the bond angles in CHX4​, NHX3​ and HX2​O from largest to smallest and explain. Then what shape has 3 bonding pairs and one lone pair?
Show the solution
  1. All have 4 groups around the central atom; they differ in lone pairs: 0, 1, 2.
  2. A lone pair squeezes the bonds more than a bonding pair, so more lone pairs mean a smaller angle.
  3. Order: CHX4​ 109.5∘, then NHX3​ 107∘, then HX2​O 104.5∘.
  4. Three bonding pairs and one lone pair is the NHX3​ case: trigonal pyramidal.
Answer: CHX4​>NHX3​>HX2​O, and the shape is trigonal pyramidal.
Common mistakes
  • Judging shape from the number of atoms alone. COX2​ is linear but HX2​O is bent even though both have three atoms; lone pairs are the reason.
  • Counting a double bond as two groups. It is one group, so carbon in COX2​ has two groups and is linear.
  • Saying the angle in water is 109.5∘. It is about 104.5∘ because of the two lone pairs.
  • Mixing up BFX3​ (trigonal planar, no lone pair) and NHX3​ (pyramidal, one lone pair).
Summary
  • Pairs repel and spread out, and that decides the shape.
  • Two groups: linear 180∘; three: trigonal planar 120∘; four: tetrahedral 109.5∘.
  • One lone pair with 3 bonds: pyramidal; two lone pairs with 2 bonds: bent.
  • Lone pairs squeeze the angle: CHX4​>NHX3​>HX2​O.

Molecular polarity and intermolecular forces

What you will learn
  • Judge whether a molecule is polar from its shape and symmetry.
  • Name the force between molecules: dispersion, dipole-dipole or hydrogen bond.
  • Explain why water boils at a high temperature.

Molecular polarity

Each polar bond has an arrow pointing toward the more electronegative atom. The polarity of the whole molecule is the sum of these arrows, so it depends on the shape, not on bond polarity alone.

If the arrows are arranged symmetrically and cancel, the molecule is nonpolar even though its bonds are polar. If a net arrow is left, the molecule is polar.

  • Nonpolar: COX2​ (linear), CHX4​ and BFX3​, because the outer atoms are identical, the shape is symmetric, and the central atom has no lone pairs.
  • Polar: HX2​O and NHX3​, because lone pairs on the central atom break the symmetry.
  • Polar: CHX3​Cl, because chlorine differs from hydrogen and breaks the symmetry even though the shape is tetrahedral.
  • A diatomic molecule of one element, such as ClX2​, is nonpolar; one of two elements, such as HCl, is polar.

Intermolecular forces

The covalent bonds inside a molecule are strong, but molecules attract each other by much weaker forces. These forces decide the boiling and melting points of molecular substances.

  • Dispersion forces: present between all molecules, and the only force between nonpolar molecules such as CHX4​. They grow with molecular size and mass.
  • Dipole-dipole forces: between polar molecules, where the positive end of one molecule attracts the negative end of another, as in HCl.
  • Hydrogen bond: the strongest of these. It forms when hydrogen is bonded to F, O or N and is attracted to a lone pair on one of these atoms in another molecule. Examples: HF, HX2​O, NHX3​.

Why is the boiling point of water so high?

A molecule of HX2​S is heavier than water, yet HX2​S boils at about −60 °C. The reason is that water molecules form hydrogen bonds and HX2​S does not.

Boiling water overcomes only the forces between molecules. It does not break the O−H bonds inside each molecule.

Worked examples

Why is COX2​ nonpolar although both C=O bonds are polar?
Show the solution
  1. Each C=O bond is polar, with its arrow toward oxygen, which is more electronegative.
  2. COX2​ is linear, so the two arrows are equal in size and opposite in direction.
  3. Their sum is zero, so the molecule has no positive end and negative end.
Answer: Its symmetric linear shape cancels the bond dipoles.
Methane CHX4​ is nonpolar but CHX3​Cl is polar. Why?
Show the solution
  1. Both are tetrahedral.
  2. In CHX4​ the four outer atoms are identical, so the sum cancels.
  3. In CHX3​Cl chlorine is more electronegative than hydrogen, so the C−Cl bond differs from the others and the sum does not cancel.
Answer: Chlorine breaks the symmetry, so the molecule is polar.
Name the strongest intermolecular force in CHX4​, HCl and HF. Why does HF boil far higher than HCl?
Show the solution
  1. CHX4​ is nonpolar: dispersion forces only.
  2. HCl is polar, and hydrogen is not bonded to F, O or N: dipole-dipole forces.
  3. HF has hydrogen bonded to fluorine: hydrogen bonds, which are stronger.
  4. Stronger forces between molecules need more energy to separate them, so HF boils higher.
Answer: CHX4​: dispersion; HCl: dipole-dipole; HF: hydrogen bonds, which raise its boiling point.
Common mistakes
  • Saying polar bonds mean a polar molecule. Look at the shape: COX2​, BFX3​ and CHX4​ are nonpolar.
  • Thinking boiling breaks O−H bonds. Boiling overcomes the forces between molecules.
  • Calling every hydrogen compound a hydrogen bond. Hydrogen must be bonded to F, O or N; in CHX4​ and HCl there is none.
  • Forgetting dispersion forces. They exist in every substance and are the only force in nonpolar ones.
Summary
  • Polarity is the sum of bond directions: a symmetric shape is nonpolar.
  • Lone pairs on the central atom, or different outer atoms, give a polar molecule.
  • Intermolecular forces: dispersion, then dipole-dipole, then the strongest, the hydrogen bond.
  • A hydrogen bond needs H bonded to F, O or N.
  • Boiling overcomes forces between molecules, not the bonds inside them.

Sigma and pi bonds and hybridization

What you will learn
  • Tell a σ bond from a π bond and count them in a molecule.
  • Find the hybridization of a central atom from its number of groups.

Sigma and pi bonds

A σ bond forms when two orbitals overlap head-on, along the line between the two nuclei. It is stronger and is present in every covalent bond; a single bond is always one σ bond.

A π bond forms when two p orbitals overlap sideways, above and below the axis. It is weaker and is found only in double and triple bonds.

  • Single: 1 σ.
  • Double: 1 σ+1 π.
  • Triple: 1 σ+2 π.

Hybridization

Carbon has only two unpaired electrons, yet it forms four identical bonds in CHX4​. Hybridization explains this: it is the mixing of orbitals on the same atom to make new equal orbitals that point toward the corners of the shape with least repulsion. We name it by the orbitals mixed: sp3 means one s orbital with three p orbitals.

The number of hybrid orbitals equals the number of groups around the atom, that is the σ bonds plus lone pairs. π bonds are not counted, because they use the p orbitals that were not hybridized.

  • Two groups: sp, linear 180∘, as in carbon of COX2​.
  • Three groups: sp2, trigonal planar 120∘, as in carbon of ethene.
  • Four groups: sp3, tetrahedral 109.5∘, as in carbon of CHX4​ and nitrogen of NHX3​ and oxygen of HX2​O.

Laws and rules

Number of hybrid orbitalshybrid orbitals=σ bonds+lone pairsA count of 2 gives sp, 3 gives sp2, and 4 gives sp3.

Worked examples

Count the σ and π bonds in ethyne HC≡CH.
Show the solution
  1. There are two single C−H bonds: two σ bonds.
  2. Between the carbons is a triple bond: one σ and two π.
  3. Total σ: 2+1=3. Total π: 2.
Answer: 3 σ bonds and 2 π bonds.
What is the hybridization of carbon in CHX4​, in COX2​ and in ethene HX2​C=CHX2​? How many σ bonds are in ethene?
Show the solution
  1. Carbon in CHX4​: 4 σ bonds and no lone pairs, so the count is 4: sp3.
  2. Carbon in COX2​: two σ bonds (the two π bonds are not counted), so the count is 2: sp.
  3. Carbon in ethene: two C−H bonds plus the σ bond of C=C, so the count is 3: sp2.
  4. σ bonds in ethene: 4 from C−H and 1 from C=C.
Answer: sp3, sp and sp2; ethene has 5 σ bonds.
In propene CHX2​=CH−CHX3​ count the σ and π bonds and give the hybridization of each carbon.
Show the solution
  1. Hydrogen atoms: 2+1+3=6, so there are 6 C−H bonds, all σ.
  2. The C=C bond is 1 σ and 1 π; the single C−C bond is 1 σ.
  3. Total σ: 6+1+1=8. Total π: 1.
  4. The first and second carbons are on the double bond, with 3 groups each: sp2. The third has 4 σ bonds: sp3.
Answer: 8 σ bonds and 1 π bond; the carbons are sp2, sp2 and sp3.
Common mistakes
  • Counting each double bond as two σ bonds. A double bond is one σ and one π; a triple bond is one σ and two π.
  • Counting π bonds among the groups for hybridization. Count only σ bonds and lone pairs.
  • Forgetting the C−H bonds when counting. Each one is a σ bond.
  • Thinking a π bond is stronger than a σ bond. Sideways overlap is weaker.
Summary
  • Every single bond is one σ bond.
  • Double: σ and π. Triple: σ and two π.
  • A σ bond is head-on and stronger; a π bond is sideways and weaker.
  • Hybridization comes from (σ bonds + lone pairs): 2 means sp, 3 means sp2, 4 means sp3.
