Structure of DNA and RNA

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Know what a nucleotide is made of and how a DNA strand is built
  • Use base pairing and Chargaff's rule to find percentages and complementary strands
  • Compare DNA with RNA and tell the three types of RNA apart

The nucleotide: the building block

DNA and RNA are nucleic acids. Each is a long chain of small repeating units called nucleotides.

A nucleotide has three parts: a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base. Sugars and phosphates of neighbouring nucleotides link up to form the backbone, and the bases point inward and carry the genetic information.

Base pairing and Chargaff's rule

DNA has four bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C). DNA is two strands twisted around each other, joined by hydrogen bonds in a fixed pattern: A with T, and G with C.

The partners fit by size: a large base (purine: adenine or guanine) faces a small one (pyrimidine: thymine or cytosine), so the helix keeps a constant width.

  • A and T are joined by two hydrogen bonds.
  • G and C are joined by three hydrogen bonds, so they hold more strongly.
  • Chargaff's rule: in any double-stranded DNA the amount of A equals the amount of T, and the amount of G equals the amount of C. So A+G=T+C, each side being 50% of the bases. The rule holds for double-stranded DNA only, not for single-stranded RNA.
  • Hydrogen bonds are weak and come apart easily during replication; the bond between sugar and phosphate inside a strand is a strong covalent bond. The two strands run in opposite directions (antiparallel).

The complementary strand

Since every base has one partner, knowing one strand lets you write the other by swapping each base for its partner: A for T, T for A, G for C, and C for G.

DNA versus RNA

RNA is also a chain of nucleotides, but it differs from DNA in four ways:

  • Sugar: ribose in RNA, deoxyribose (one oxygen atom fewer) in DNA.
  • Strands: RNA is usually a single strand; DNA has two.
  • Bases: uracil (U) replaces thymine in RNA, so A pairs with U.
  • Location: DNA stays in the nucleus (it is also found in mitochondria and chloroplasts); RNA is made in the nucleus and then moves to the cytoplasm.

Types of RNA

Three types of RNA work together to build proteins:

  • mRNA (messenger): carries a copy of one gene from the nucleus to the ribosome.
  • tRNA (transfer): brings amino acids to the ribosome and has three bases at one end called the anticodon.
  • rRNA (ribosomal): joins proteins to form the ribosome.

Laws and rules

Chargaff's rule (1)A=TThe percentage (or number) of adenine equals that of thymine in double-stranded DNA.
Chargaff's rule (2)G=CThe percentage (or number) of guanine equals that of cytosine.
Sum of percentagesA+T+G+C=100%The four base percentages of double-stranded DNA add up to the whole.

Worked examples

Thymine is 22% of a double-stranded DNA. What percentage is guanine?
Show the solution
  1. Chargaff's rule: A=T=22%.
  2. Together: 22+22=44%.
  3. The rest is guanine and cytosine: 100−44=56%.
  4. G=C, so each is half: 56÷2=28%.
Answer: Guanine is 28%. Check: 22+22+28+28=100.
One DNA strand reads TACGGCATT. Write the complementary strand.
Show the solution
  1. Swap each base for its partner: T→A, then A→T, then C→G.
  2. Continue: G→C, G→C, C→G, A→T, T→A, T→A.
  3. Join the results in the same order.
Answer: The complementary strand is ATGCCGTAA.
A double-stranded DNA piece has 200 nucleotides, and 20% of them are adenine. How many hydrogen bonds join the two strands?
Show the solution
  1. A is 20% of 200, which is 40; T is also 40.
  2. G+C=200−80=120, so each is 60.
  3. 40 A-T pairs with two bonds each: 40×2=80.
  4. 60 G-C pairs with three bonds each: 60×3=180.
  5. Total: 80+180=260.
Answer: There are 260 hydrogen bonds.
Common mistakes
  • Subtracting the adenine percentage from 100 to get thymine. Correct: thymine equals adenine, because it is its direct partner.
  • Putting uracil in DNA or thymine in RNA. Uracil exists only in RNA.
  • Applying Chargaff's rule to RNA. The rule is for double-stranded DNA; in single-stranded RNA adenine need not equal uracil.
  • Writing uracil in the complement of DNA. The complement made in replication is DNA and keeps thymine.
Summary
  • Nucleotide = sugar + phosphate + nitrogenous base.
  • In DNA: A pairs with T by two bonds, G with C by three.
  • Chargaff: the amount of A equals T, and G equals C.
  • RNA has ribose, is usually single-stranded and contains uracil.
  • RNA types: mRNA carries the code, tRNA carries amino acids, rRNA is part of the ribosome.

DNA Replication

What you will learn
  • Understand why replication is called semiconservative
  • Explain the results of the Meselson-Stahl experiment
  • Know what each replication enzyme does

Semiconservative replication

Before a cell divides it must make a full copy of its DNA. This is replication. The two strands separate, and each old strand serves as a template for a new complementary strand built by base pairing.

The result is two DNA molecules, each with one old strand and one new strand. That is why replication is called semiconservative: half of the original molecule is kept in every copy.

Replication starts at specific origins on the DNA. Eukaryotic chromosomes have many origins, so a long DNA molecule is copied in a short time.

The Meselson-Stahl experiment

The two scientists grew bacteria for several generations in a medium with heavy nitrogen, so all their DNA became heavy. They then moved the bacteria to light nitrogen and separated the DNA of each generation by density, using a centrifuge.

In generation 1 there was one band of intermediate density; in generation 2 there were two bands: intermediate and light. If replication were conservative (the original molecule stays intact and a fully new one is built), generation 1 would show two bands: heavy and light. A single intermediate band proves that every molecule has one old and one new strand. Two bands in generation 2 also rule out dispersive replication (old and new pieces mixed along each strand), which would give a single band lighter than intermediate.

Replication enzymes

Replication needs a team of enzymes:

  • Helicase: unwinds the helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between the strands, forming the replication fork.
  • Primase: makes a short RNA primer where building starts.
  • DNA polymerase: adds nucleotides complementary to the template and proofreads mistakes.
  • Ligase: joins the pieces and seals gaps in the sugar-phosphate backbone.

Leading and lagging strands

DNA polymerase works in one direction only, so one new strand is built continuously (the leading strand) and the other in short pieces called Okazaki fragments (the lagging strand), which ligase joins.

Laws and rules

Number of molecules after replicationmolecules=2nn: the number of times one molecule replicates.
Old strandsmolecules holding an old strand=2One original molecule has only two old strands, so they sit in only two molecules however many rounds follow.

Worked examples

A DNA molecule with both strands heavy is placed in light nitrogen and replicates 3 times. How many molecules result, and how many carry a heavy strand?
Show the solution
  1. Molecules: 23=8.
  2. The two old heavy strands stay intact, each in a different molecule.
  3. So only two molecules carry a heavy strand (hybrids); the other six are completely light.
Answer: 8 molecules result, and only 2 carry a heavy strand.
Which bands appear in the tube after generation 2 of the Meselson-Stahl experiment, and in what proportion?
Show the solution
  1. After two rounds there are 22=4 molecules from the original one.
  2. The two old heavy strands remain in two hybrid molecules (heavy + light).
  3. The other two molecules are entirely light.
Answer: Two bands: intermediate (50%) and light (50%). No heavy band appears.
A DNA molecule of 1000 base pairs replicates twice in a row. How many new nucleotides are built in total?
Show the solution
  1. One molecule has 1000×2=2000 nucleotides (two strands).
  2. After two rounds there are 22=4 molecules, so 4×2000=8000 nucleotides.
  3. The old nucleotides are those of the original molecule: 2000.
  4. New =8000−2000=6000.
Answer: 6000 new nucleotides are built.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking one of the two products holds both old strands. That is conservative replication; correct: each molecule has exactly one old strand.
  • Mixing up helicase, which unwinds, with ligase, which joins pieces.
  • Counting molecules by adding 2 each round. Correct: the number doubles, so multiply by 2 each round.
  • Expecting a heavy band in generation 1. The new strand is light, so no fully heavy molecule remains.
Summary
  • Replication is semiconservative: each new molecule = one old strand + one new strand.
  • Meselson-Stahl: one intermediate band in generation 1, then intermediate and light in generation 2.
  • Helicase unwinds, primase starts, DNA polymerase builds, ligase joins.
  • After n rounds there are 2n molecules, and only two hold an old strand.

Transcription and mRNA Processing

What you will learn
  • Describe how RNA is built from a DNA template
  • Know the role of the promoter in starting transcription
  • Explain why mRNA is modified before leaving the nucleus

Transcription: from DNA to RNA

Transcription is building an RNA molecule from the sequence of a gene in DNA. In eukaryotes it happens in the nucleus. The enzyme RNA polymerase opens a short stretch of the helix, reads one strand called the template strand, and adds RNA nucleotides complementary to it.

The matching RNA base is: template A gives U, T gives A, G gives C, and C gives G. The other strand (non-template) has the same sequence as the mRNA, with T where the mRNA has U.

Starting transcription

Transcription does not start just anywhere. In front of each gene lies a DNA sequence called the promoter, where RNA polymerase binds (in eukaryotes with the help of transcription factors). Once bound, the enzyme opens the helix and building begins.

Transcription has three stages: initiation at the promoter, elongation as RNA nucleotides are added one by one, and termination at a terminator sequence, where the RNA is released from the template.

mRNA processing in eukaryotes

The first RNA product cannot be translated directly, because it contains introns (non-coding regions) between exons (coding regions). It is modified in the nucleus in three steps:

  • A cap is added at the first end; it protects the mRNA and helps the ribosome attach.
  • A tail of adenine bases is added at the other end; it makes the mRNA last longer.
  • Introns are cut out and exons are spliced together.

The mature mRNA

Now the mRNA is mature and leaves the nucleus through nuclear pores into the cytoplasm. Bacterial genes have no introns, so bacteria need no such processing.

Laws and rules

Length of mature mRNAmature=sum of exonsLength is counted in nucleotides, and introns are not counted.

Worked examples

The template strand of a gene reads TACGGCATT. What is the mRNA?
Show the solution
  1. Match each template base with an RNA base: thymine with adenine, adenine with uracil, cytosine with guanine, guanine with cytosine.
  2. The first triplet TAC gives AUG, the second GGC gives CCG, the third ATT gives UAA.
  3. Join the results in the same order.
Answer: mRNA: AUGCCGUAA.
A gene has three exons of 150, 90 and 60 nucleotides and two introns of 120 and 80. What is the length of the first RNA product? And of the mature mRNA (without cap and tail)?
Show the solution
  1. Sum of exons: 150+90+60=300.
  2. Sum of introns: 120+80=200.
  3. The first product holds both: 300+200=500.
  4. The mature mRNA holds only the exons, after the introns are cut out.
Answer: The first product is 500 nucleotides; the mature mRNA is 300 nucleotides.
The non-template strand of a gene reads ATGGGCTAA. What is the mRNA?
Show the solution
  1. The non-template strand has the same sequence as the mRNA.
  2. Replace every T with U and leave the other bases unchanged.
Answer: mRNA: AUGGGCUAA.
Common mistakes
  • Writing thymine in RNA. Uracil always replaces it.
  • Thinking both strands are transcribed together. One gene is copied from one template strand.
  • Mixing up intron and exon. The intron is cut out and discarded; the exon stays and codes.
  • Believing transcription starts anywhere. RNA polymerase must first bind the promoter.
Summary
  • Transcription: RNA is built from a DNA template strand by RNA polymerase.
  • Transcription starts when RNA polymerase binds the promoter.
  • In RNA, uracil replaces thymine.
  • mRNA processing: cap and tail added, introns removed, exons joined.

Translation and the Genetic Code

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Read the genetic code and know the start and stop codons
  • Find the anticodon for any codon
  • Work out the length of the protein made from an mRNA

The genetic code and codons

A codon is three bases in a row on mRNA that specify one amino acid. RNA has four bases, so the number of possible codons is 43=64.

Of these, 61 code for the twenty amino acids and 3 are stop codons. The code is redundant: several codons can code for the same amino acid.

  • Start codon AUG: codes for methionine and marks where translation begins.
  • Stop codons UAA, UAG and UGA: code for no amino acid and end translation.
  • The code is universal: almost the same codons code for the same amino acids in all organisms, and it is read codon by codon without overlap.

tRNA and the anticodon

One end of a tRNA carries three bases called the anticodon, which pair with the codon on the mRNA: A with U, and G with C. The other end carries the amino acid that the codon specifies.

Example: the codon AUG pairs with the anticodon UAC.

The ribosome and the stages of translation

Translation is building a protein by reading mRNA codons. The ribosome has a small and a large subunit, both made of rRNA and protein. Several ribosomes can read the same mRNA at once, so many copies of the protein are made quickly. Translation has three stages:

  • Initiation: the small subunit binds the mRNA and finds AUG, the first tRNA enters, and then the large subunit joins.
  • Elongation: a matching tRNA enters for each codon, a peptide bond forms between the two amino acids, and the ribosome moves one codon along.
  • Termination: no tRNA matches a stop codon; a release factor binds instead, so the chain is released and the ribosome comes apart.

Protein length

The number of amino acids equals the number of codons before the stop codon, because the stop codon adds no amino acid. So if you count all nucleotides from AUG to the end of the stop codon, divide by 3 and subtract 1.

Laws and rules

Number of possible codonscodons=43=64Four bases at each of the three positions of a codon.
Protein lengthamino acids=3nucleotides​−1Nucleotides: the count from the start codon to the end of the stop codon. Subtract 1 because the stop codon adds no amino acid.

Worked examples

An mRNA contains AUGGCUUACUAA. How many amino acids does the protein have? What are the anticodons of the first three codons?
Show the solution
  1. Split into codons: AUG, then GCU, then UAC, then UAA.
  2. The last codon UAA is a stop, so 3 codons code for amino acids.
  3. Anticodons by pairing: AUG→UAC, GCU→CGA, UAC→AUG.
Answer: The protein has 3 amino acids; the anticodons are UAC, CGA and AUG.
An mRNA part has 90 nucleotides from the start codon to the end of the stop codon. How many amino acids are in the protein?
Show the solution
  1. Number of codons: 90÷3=30.
  2. Remove the stop codon: 30−1=29.
Answer: The protein has 29 amino acids.
The DNA template strand reads TACAAACCCATT. What is the mRNA, and how many amino acids does the protein have?
Show the solution
  1. Write the mRNA base by base: AUGUUUGGGUAA.
  2. Split into codons: AUG, UUU, GGG, UAA.
  3. The fourth codon UAA is a stop, so 3 codons code for amino acids.
Answer: mRNA: AUGUUUGGGUAA; the protein has 3 amino acids.
Common mistakes
  • Counting the stop codon as an amino acid. It is an ending signal and has no amino acid.
  • Mixing up the codon (on mRNA) with the anticodon (on tRNA). The anticodon is the complement.
  • Writing thymine in an anticodon. tRNA is RNA, so its anticodon has uracil.
  • Forgetting that AUG is a start codon that codes for methionine, so it does count as an amino acid.
Summary
  • A codon is three bases on mRNA; there are 64 codons.
  • Start codon: AUG. Stop codons: UAA, UAG, UGA.
  • The anticodon on tRNA pairs with the codon.
  • The ribosome reads mRNA codon by codon and joins the amino acids.
  • Amino acids = (nucleotides ÷ 3) − 1 when the stop codon is counted.

Mutations

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Tell the point mutations apart and know how each affects the protein
  • Understand frameshift and why it is so damaging
  • Know the mutagens and the four chromosomal mutations

What is a mutation and what causes it?

A mutation is a permanent change in the DNA sequence. It can occur spontaneously through a mistake in replication, or through agents called mutagens.

Mutagens include radiation (ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays), chemicals (such as tobacco smoke) and some viruses. For example, ultraviolet light makes two neighbouring thymines stick together, which disturbs replication there.

  • A mutation in sex cells (gametes) is passed to offspring.
  • A mutation in body cells is not inherited, but it can cause diseases such as cancer.

Gene (point) mutations

A point mutation changes one base or a few bases inside a gene. The types are:

  • Substitution: one base replaces another. It has three possible results: silent (the new codon codes for the same amino acid because the code is redundant, so the protein is unchanged), missense (one amino acid changes), or nonsense (next point).
  • Nonsense mutation: a substitution that turns a codon for an amino acid into a stop codon, so building stops early and the protein is short and usually useless.
  • Insertion and deletion: one or more bases enter or leave the sequence.

Frameshift

The ribosome reads mRNA in groups of three bases. If a number of bases not divisible by 3 (one or two, for example) is added or deleted, the reading frame shifts, so every codon after the mutation changes, and the stop codon may be lost or appear early.

Adding or deleting 3 bases does not shift the frame; it only adds or removes one amino acid.

Effects of mutations and sickle-cell anemia

A mutation can be harmful, neutral or beneficial, depending on where it is and which protein it changes. Mutations are the source of the new traits on which natural selection acts.

Sickle-cell anemia is an example of one substitution in the gene for the beta chain of hemoglobin: the codon GAG becomes GUG, so valine replaces glutamic acid. At low oxygen the abnormal hemoglobin clumps and bends red blood cells into a sickle shape, so they block small vessels and break down quickly. Carriers of one copy are more resistant to malaria.

Chromosomal mutations

These change the structure of the chromosome itself, and usually arise from errors in meiosis. The types are:

There is also a mutation in chromosome number: nondisjunction in meiosis gives a gamete with an extra or a missing chromosome, as in Down syndrome, where there are three copies of chromosome 21.

  • Deletion: loss of a piece of the chromosome.
  • Duplication: a piece is repeated.
  • Inversion: a piece breaks off and rejoins the other way round in its place.
  • Translocation: a piece moves to a non-homologous chromosome.

Worked examples

The codon GAG in an mRNA changes to GUG. What type of mutation is this, given that GAG codes for glutamic acid and GUG for valine?
Show the solution
  1. Only one base changed (A became U), so it is a point substitution.
  2. The amino acid changed from glutamic acid to valine, and the new codon is not a stop.
  3. The number of amino acids is unchanged.
Answer: A missense substitution, as in sickle-cell anemia.
An mRNA is AUGUCAGACUAA. The first base of the second codon (the base U) is deleted. What happens to the codons?
Show the solution
  1. Original: AUG, UCA, GAC, UAA.
  2. After deleting the fourth base the sequence is AUGCAGACUAA (one base shorter).
  3. Regroup: AUG, CAG, ACU, AA.
  4. The second and third codons changed, and the stop codon is lost because it is no longer a complete triplet.
Answer: A frameshift: everything after the deletion changes, and translation may not stop where it should.
A protein is 150 amino acids long. A mutation changes the codon UGG to UGA at codon number 40. What type of mutation is it, and how long is the resulting protein?
Show the solution
  1. UGA is a stop codon, so it is a nonsense substitution.
  2. Translation stops at codon 40, which gives no amino acid.
  3. The amino acids made are codons 1 to 39.
Answer: A nonsense mutation; the protein is only 39 amino acids long.
Common mistakes
  • Assuming every mutation changes the protein. A silent mutation changes the codon but not the amino acid.
  • Confusing nonsense (an early stop signal appears) with missense (one amino acid changes).
  • Thinking that deleting or adding 3 bases shifts the frame. A frameshift happens when the number is not divisible by 3.
  • Saying all mutations are harmful, or that a body-cell mutation is passed to offspring.
Summary
  • A mutation is a permanent change in DNA, from a spontaneous error or a mutagen.
  • Substitution: silent, missense or nonsense.
  • Insertion or deletion of a number not divisible by 3 causes a frameshift.
  • Sickle-cell anemia: one substitution turns glutamic acid into valine.
  • Chromosomal mutations: deletion, duplication, inversion, translocation.

Gene Regulation: Operons

What you will learn
  • Know the parts of an operon and the role of the repressor
  • Explain how the lac operon (inducible) works
  • Explain how the trp operon (repressible) works

What is an operon?

An operon is a group of neighbouring genes, mainly in bacteria, that are transcribed together under one control. Its advantage is that the cell makes enzymes only when needed, saving energy.

Its parts are the promoter, where RNA polymerase binds; the operator, a sequence where the repressor binds; and the structural genes, which code for enzymes of one reaction pathway. A regulatory gene makes a protein called the repressor. When the repressor binds the operator, it blocks RNA polymerase and transcription stops.

The lac operon: inducible

This operon codes for the enzymes that digest lactose. It is normally off:

  • No lactose: the repressor is active and binds the operator, so RNA polymerase cannot transcribe the genes. The operon is off.
  • Lactose present: lactose (in a closely related form called allolactose) binds the repressor, which changes shape and leaves the operator. RNA polymerase moves through, the genes are transcribed and the lactose-digesting enzymes are made. The operon is on.
  • When the lactose is used up, the repressor returns to the operator and the operon switches off again.

The trp operon: repressible

This operon codes for the enzymes that make the amino acid tryptophan. It is normally on:

  • Little tryptophan: the repressor is inactive and does not bind the operator, so the operon is transcribed and the enzymes are made.
  • Plenty of tryptophan: tryptophan binds the repressor and activates it; it binds the operator and switches the operon off, so the cell does not waste energy making what it does not need.

Comparing the two operons

The key difference: in the lac operon the substance (lactose) switches it on; in the trp operon the substance (tryptophan) switches it off.

Worked examples

A bacterial cell grows in a medium with no lactose. What is the state of the lac operon?
Show the solution
  1. Without lactose, nothing binds the repressor.
  2. The repressor is active, binds the operator and blocks RNA polymerase.
Answer: The operon is off and the lactose-digesting enzymes are not made.
A mutation in the operator stops the repressor from binding to it. What happens to the lac operon when there is no lactose?
Show the solution
  1. The repressor switches the operon off only when it binds the operator.
  2. The changed operator cannot bind the repressor, so RNA polymerase always passes.
Answer: The operon is always on and the enzymes are made even without lactose, wasting energy.
A mutation makes the repressor of the trp operon unable to bind tryptophan. What is the state of the operon when tryptophan is plentiful?
Show the solution
  1. Switching this operon off needs tryptophan to bind the repressor and activate it.
  2. The mutant repressor cannot bind tryptophan, so it stays inactive and does not bind the operator.
  3. The operon is not switched off, so transcription continues.
Answer: The operon stays on and the cell keeps making tryptophan despite its abundance.
Common mistakes
  • Saying lactose directly stimulates RNA polymerase. Lactose binds the repressor, which frees the operator.
  • Mixing up the two operons: lac is normally off, trp is normally on.
  • Thinking the repressor binds the promoter. It binds the operator.
  • Forgetting that the regulatory gene makes the repressor and is not one of the structural genes.
Summary
  • Operon: promoter + operator + structural genes; a regulatory gene makes its repressor.
  • A repressor on the operator switches the operon off.
  • The lac operon is inducible: off, and lactose switches it on.
  • The trp operon is repressible: on, and plentiful tryptophan switches it off.

Biotechnology

What you will learn
  • Know what restriction enzymes and ligase do and how recombinant DNA is made
  • Describe the steps of PCR and count the copies
  • Explain the result of gel electrophoresis of DNA fragments

Restriction enzymes, ligase and recombinant DNA

Restriction enzymes (from bacteria) cut DNA at a short specific sequence, often leaving two sticky ends: short single strands that complement each other. If DNA from two different sources is cut with the same enzyme, their ends stick together by base pairing.

The enzyme DNA ligase makes the join permanent by linking the sugar-phosphate backbone with covalent bonds. The result is recombinant DNA: DNA that combines parts from two different sources.

  • Cut the wanted gene and the plasmid (a ring of bacterial DNA) with the same enzyme.
  • Mix them so the sticky ends pair up.
  • Ligase makes the join permanent.
  • Put the recombinant plasmid into bacteria, (this is called transformation), which then make the gene's protein, such as human insulin.

Cloning

Cloning is producing genetically identical copies. It includes gene cloning, by multiplying a gene inside bacteria, and cloning a whole organism, by transferring the nucleus of a body cell into an egg whose nucleus was removed, as with Dolly the sheep. The nuclear DNA of the cloned organism is identical to that of the nucleus donor.

PCR (polymerase chain reaction)

PCR multiplies a chosen DNA segment from a very small sample. It needs template DNA, two short primers, nucleotides and a heat-resistant DNA polymerase. Each cycle has three steps:

  • Denaturation (about 95 °C): the two DNA strands separate.
  • Annealing (about 55 °C): the primers bind the wanted segment.
  • Extension (about 72 °C): the polymerase builds the complementary strands.

How fast the sample grows

Each cycle doubles the number of copies, so the sample grows very fast.

Gel electrophoresis

Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size. The fragments are placed in wells in a gel and an electric current is applied. DNA is negatively charged, so it moves toward the positive pole. Small fragments move faster and farther, large ones stay near the wells, so each size forms its own band. This is used in DNA fingerprinting: a person's DNA is cut with a restriction enzyme and gives a band pattern of its own.

Laws and rules

Copies after n cyclescopies=2nn: the number of PCR cycles, starting from one molecule.
Number of fragmentsfragments=cut sites+1For linear DNA. For a circular plasmid the number of fragments equals the number of cut sites.

Worked examples

PCR starts with one DNA molecule. How many copies are there after 5 cycles? After 10?
Show the solution
  1. Each cycle doubles the number, so after 5 cycles: 25=32.
  2. After 10 cycles: 210=1024.
Answer: 32 copies after 5 cycles and 1024 copies after 10 cycles.
A sample has DNA fragments of 200, 500 and 1000 base pairs, and electrophoresis is run. Order the fragments from nearest to the wells to farthest, and say which way they move.
Show the solution
  1. DNA is negatively charged, so it moves toward the positive pole.
  2. Small fragments move faster and farther, large ones slower.
  3. From the wells: the largest fragment first, then the smaller ones.
Answer: Nearest to farthest: 1000, then 500, then 200. They move toward the positive pole.
A linear DNA has three sites for a restriction enzyme, and a circular plasmid has three sites for the same enzyme. How many fragments does each give after complete cutting?
Show the solution
  1. Linear: each cut adds one piece, so 3+1=4.
  2. Circular: the first cut only opens the ring, so the number of fragments equals the number of sites.
Answer: Linear: 4 fragments. Circular: 3 fragments.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking ligase cuts DNA. Ligase joins; the restriction enzyme cuts.
  • Saying large fragments travel farther in electrophoresis. Small ones are faster and go farther.
  • Forgetting that DNA is negatively charged and moves to the positive pole.
  • Counting the fragments of a circular plasmid with +1 as for linear DNA.
Summary
  • A restriction enzyme cuts at a specific sequence and leaves sticky ends; ligase joins.
  • Recombinant DNA: a gene and a plasmid cut with the same enzyme and then joined.
  • Cloning produces genetically identical copies.
  • PCR: denaturation, annealing, extension; copies =2n.
  • Electrophoresis: DNA goes to the positive pole and small fragments travel farther.
