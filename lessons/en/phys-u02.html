Inertia, the First Law and Net Force

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Explain why an object resists a change in its motion
  • State and apply Newton's first law
  • Find the net force of forces on one line and of two perpendicular forces

Inertia

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist any change in its motion: a resting object tends to stay at rest, and a moving object tends to keep moving at the same speed in the same direction.

Inertia is measured by mass alone. An iron ball of mass 2kg is harder to start or stop than a huge balloon of mass 0.1kg, even though the balloon is bigger.

Newton's first law

An object at rest stays at rest, and a moving object keeps moving at constant velocity in a straight line, unless a non-zero net force acts on it.

The new idea: motion does not need a force to continue. A force is needed only to change motion, that is, to cause acceleration. A piece of ice sliding on a perfectly smooth surface keeps its speed forever because nothing slows it.

When a bus stops suddenly the passengers lurch forward. Nothing pushes them; they simply keep their old motion while the bus stops.

Net force

Usually several forces act on an object at once. The net force is the single force that has the same effect as all of them together.

On one line, forces in the same direction add, opposite forces subtract, and the net force points the way of the larger force.

If the two forces are perpendicular (a right angle between them) we neither add nor subtract them; the net force is the hypotenuse of the right triangle whose sides are the forces (Pythagoras). For example 3N east and 4N north give a net force of 5N.

  • If the net force is zero the object is in equilibrium: at rest or moving at constant velocity.
  • If the net force is not zero the object accelerates in the direction of the net force.

Laws and rules

Newton's first lawif Fnet​=0 then a=0Velocity stays constant in size and direction: a resting object stays at rest and a moving object keeps its velocity.
Net force on one lineFnet​=Fright​−Fleft​Forces in newtons; a positive result means the net force points right, a negative one means left.
Two perpendicular forcesFnet​=F12​+F22​​Forces in newtons with a right angle between them; for example 32+42​=5N.

Worked examples

A force of 30N to the right and a force of 10N to the left act on an object. What is the net force?
Show the solution
  1. The forces are opposite, so subtract the smaller from the larger.
  2. Fnet​=30−10=20N.
  3. The direction is that of the larger force: to the right.
Answer: 20N to the right.
A resting object feels 8N east, 3N west and a third force. Find the third force in size and direction.
Show the solution
  1. The object stays at rest, so the net force is zero.
  2. The two known forces give 8−3=5N east.
  3. To make the total zero, the third force must be 5N west.
  4. Check: 8−3−5=0.
Answer: 5N west.
A crate is pulled at constant speed over rough ground by a horizontal force of 20N. What is the friction? If the pull suddenly rises to 35N, what is the net force and what happens to the speed?
Show the solution
  1. The speed is constant, so the acceleration and the net force are zero (first law).
  2. Pull and friction are opposite and equal: friction =20N against the motion.
  3. With a 35N pull the friction stays 20N, so the net force is 35−20=15N along the motion.
  4. The net force is not zero, so the crate accelerates and speeds up.
Answer: Friction is 20N; then the net force is 15N and the speed increases.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking motion needs a force to continue. Correct: a force only changes motion; with no net force the speed stays the same.
  • Thinking a resting object has no forces on it. Correct: many forces may act, but their net is zero.
  • Mixing up inertia with size or speed. Correct: inertia is set by mass alone.
  • Adding two opposite forces instead of subtracting. Correct: opposite forces subtract and the net points the way of the larger one.
Summary
  • Inertia is resistance to a change in motion, measured by mass.
  • First law: with no net force the velocity stays constant.
  • Opposite forces on one line subtract; forces in the same direction add.
  • Zero net force means equilibrium: no acceleration.

Newton's Second Law, Weight and Mass

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Link net force, mass and acceleration
  • Tell mass from weight and compute weight
  • Solve acceleration problems in tidy steps

The second law

A net force gives an object an acceleration: a bigger force gives a bigger acceleration, and a bigger mass gives a smaller one. The acceleration points the way of the net force.

The relation: net force = mass × acceleration. So acceleration = net force ÷ mass. Always use the net force, not one force; if there is friction or resistance, subtract it first.

The unit of force is the newton: 1N is the force that gives a 1kg mass an acceleration of 1m/s2.

Mass and weight

Mass is the amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms, and it does not change with place. Weight is the pull of a planet on the object, measured in newtons, and it changes with place.

Weight = mass × gravitational acceleration g. On Earth we take g≈10m/s2 in problems (more precisely 9.8m/s2), and on the Moon it is about one sixth of that (1.6m/s2).

So an astronaut moving to the Moon keeps the same mass and the same inertia, but the weight becomes smaller.

Laws and rules

Newton's second lawFnet​=maForce in newtons, mass m in kg, acceleration a in m/s2.
Accelerationa=mFnet​​The direction is that of the net force.
WeightW=mgWeight W in newtons, mass m in kg, g in m/s2.

Worked examples

A net force of 20N acts on an object of mass 5kg. What is its acceleration?
Show the solution
  1. The unknown is acceleration, so write a=mFnet​​.
  2. Substitute: a=520​=4m/s2.
  3. Check: 5×4=20N, the given force.
Answer: 4m/s2.
An astronaut has mass 60kg. Find the weight on Earth (g=10m/s2) and on the Moon (g=1.6m/s2). What is the mass on the Moon?
Show the solution
  1. Weight on Earth: 60×10=600N.
  2. Weight on the Moon: 60×1.6=96N.
  3. Mass does not change with place, so it stays 60kg.
Answer: Weight 600N on Earth and 96N on the Moon; mass 60kg.
A 1000kg car starts from rest with an engine force of 5000N and accelerates at 3m/s2. Find the resisting force and the speed after 4s.
Show the solution
  1. Net force =1000×3=3000N.
  2. Net force is engine minus resistance: 5000−R=3000.
  3. The resistance is R=5000−3000=2000N.
  4. Speed after 4s from rest: 3×4=12m/s.
Answer: Resistance 2000N and speed 12m/s.
Common mistakes
  • Using one force instead of the net force. Correct: subtract friction and resistance first, then apply the law.
  • Multiplying force by mass to get acceleration. Correct: acceleration = force ÷ mass.
  • Mixing mass and weight, or giving weight in kg. Correct: mass in kg, weight in newtons.
  • Thinking weight on the Moon equals weight on Earth. Correct: mass stays fixed, weight becomes smaller.
Summary
  • Net force = mass × acceleration.
  • Acceleration points along the net force; more force raises it, more mass lowers it.
  • Weight = mass × g, measured in newtons.
  • Mass is the same everywhere; weight changes.

Normal Force and Friction

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Find the normal force in different situations
  • Tell static friction from kinetic friction
  • Solve pushing and sliding problems on a rough surface

Normal force

The normal force is the push of a surface on an object, always perpendicular to the surface. It is not always equal to the weight; it comes from balancing the vertical forces.

A book at rest on a table: normal force = weight. If you also press down on it, the surface must push harder: normal force = weight + pressing force. If you pull it up with a vertical rope, the normal force is smaller: normal force = weight − pull.

On a slope at angle θ to the horizontal only a part of the weight presses into the surface, so the normal force is smaller than the weight.

Static friction

Friction is a force that opposes the sliding of two surfaces, or the attempt to slide, so it points against the motion (or the possible motion).

Before the object moves, static friction equals the pushing force exactly and grows with it, keeping the net force zero. But it has a maximum = coefficient of static friction × normal force. Once the push exceeds this limit, sliding begins.

Kinetic friction

When the object slides, the friction is kinetic: its constant value = coefficient of kinetic friction × normal force, which is smaller than the static maximum. That is why it takes a bigger force to start a crate moving than to keep it moving.

Friction does not depend on contact area or speed; it depends on the two surfaces and on the normal force.

Laws and rules

Normal force (pressing down on a horizontal surface)N=W+Fpress​All in newtons; with no extra press, N=W.
Normal force on a slopeN=Wcosθθ is the angle of the slope to the horizontal; N and W in newtons.
Maximum static frictionfs,max​=μs​Nμs​ has no unit. The actual static friction equals the applied force and never exceeds this limit.
Kinetic frictionfk​=μk​NPoints against the sliding; its value is constant.

Worked examples

Take g=10m/s2. You press down with 20N on a 5kg crate on a table. What is the normal force on it?
Show the solution
  1. Weight =5×10=50N.
  2. The crate does not move vertically, so upward forces equal downward forces.
  3. N=50+20=70N.
Answer: 70N.
Take g=10m/s2. A 10kg crate has μs​=0.5 and is pushed horizontally with 30N. What is the friction force?
Show the solution
  1. Normal force = weight =10×10=100N.
  2. Maximum static friction =0.5×100=50N.
  3. The push 30N is below 50N, so the crate does not move.
  4. Static friction equals the push: 30N (not 50).
Answer: 30N, and the crate stays at rest.
Take g=10m/s2. A block slides at 10m/s on level ground with μk​=0.5 and no pushing force. Find its deceleration and the distance until it stops.
Show the solution
  1. N=m×10, so friction =0.5×m×10=5m.
  2. Deceleration =m5m​=5m/s2 (the mass cancels).
  3. Use v2=2ad: 100=2×5×d.
  4. d=10100​=10m.
Answer: Deceleration 5m/s2 and distance 10m.
Common mistakes
  • Taking the normal force as always equal to the weight. Correct: find it from the force balance; pressing down raises it, while pulling up or a sloping surface lowers it.
  • Always using coefficient × normal for static friction. Correct: that is the maximum; the actual value equals the push while the object is at rest.
  • Thinking kinetic friction is bigger than the static maximum. Correct: it is smaller, which is why moving is easier than starting.
  • Thinking friction grows with contact area or speed. Correct: it depends on the surfaces and the normal force.
Summary
  • The normal force is perpendicular to the surface and comes from the vertical balance.
  • Static friction equals the push up to a maximum = coefficient × normal force.
  • Kinetic friction is constant = coefficient × normal force, below the static maximum.
  • Friction always opposes motion or attempted motion.

Newton's Third Law: Action and Reaction

What you will learn
  • State Newton's third law
  • Identify an action–reaction pair correctly
  • Explain why the two forces do not cancel

Forces come in pairs

When object A pushes on object B, B must push back on A. One is called the action and the other the reaction.

The third law: the two forces are equal in size and opposite in direction, of the same type, and appear at the same instant. When you kick a ball, the ball pushes back on you with the same force.

Why do they not cancel?

Because each force of the pair acts on a different object. Cancelling happens only between forces on the same object. So both objects can accelerate even though the forces are equal.

The forces are equal but the accelerations differ if the masses differ: a=mF​. A truck hits a small car and the car is affected much more, although the two forces are equal.

How to find the partner force

Reverse the sentence: the partner of «Earth pulls the book» is «the book pulls the Earth». The partner of «the table pushes the book up» is «the book presses down on the table».

Careful: the weight and the normal force on a book are not a pair; they act on one object and are different types of force.

Laws and rules

Newton's third lawFA on B​=−FB on A​Equal size, opposite direction, and they never act on the same object.

Worked examples

A player kicks a 0.5kg ball with a force of 100N. What is the force of the ball on the foot? What is the ball's acceleration?
Show the solution
  1. By the third law the ball pushes the foot with 100N in the opposite direction.
  2. The ball's acceleration =0.5100​=200m/s2.
Answer: The ball pushes the foot with 100N against the kick; the ball accelerates at 200m/s2.
Take g=10m/s2. A 2kg book rests on a table. What is the partner of the Earth's pull on the book? And the partner of the normal force?
Show the solution
  1. Weight of the book =2×10=20N (Earth pulls the book down).
  2. Its partner: the book pulls the Earth up with 20N.
  3. The normal force is 20N up (table on book); its partner: the book presses down on the table with 20N.
  4. Weight and normal force are equal because the book is balanced (first law), not because they are a pair.
Answer: Partner of the weight: the book pulling the Earth. Partner of the normal force: the book pressing on the table.
On smooth ice a 60kg skater pushes a 40kg skater with a force of 120N. Find the acceleration of each.
Show the solution
  1. The second skater pushes the first with 120N in the opposite direction (third law).
  2. The 40kg skater: 40120​=3m/s2.
  3. The 60kg skater: 60120​=2m/s2 in the opposite direction.
  4. Check: the forces are equal and the smaller mass has the larger acceleration.
Answer: The lighter skater accelerates at 3m/s2 and the heavier at 2m/s2, in opposite directions.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking the stronger or heavier object pushes harder (truck and car). Correct: the two forces are always equal.
  • Saying action and reaction cancel. Correct: they act on different objects, so they cannot cancel.
  • Treating weight and the normal force as an action–reaction pair. Correct: they act on one object; the partner of weight is the object pulling the Earth.
  • Thinking the reaction comes after the action. Correct: they occur at the same instant.
Summary
  • Every force has a matching force on the other object.
  • The pair is equal in size, opposite in direction and of the same type.
  • They do not cancel because they act on two different objects.
  • The accelerations differ when the masses differ.

Tension, Pulleys and the Elevator

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Find the tension in a rope at rest or accelerating
  • Solve a two-mass pulley system
  • Find the scale reading in an elevator

Tension

Tension is the force a taut rope exerts on the object tied to it, along the rope: it pulls the object toward the rope. We take the rope as light and the pulley as smooth, so the tension is the same all along the rope.

A hanging lamp at rest: tension = weight. If an object is lifted with an upward acceleration (up is positive): tension − weight = mass × acceleration, so the tension is greater than the weight. If it accelerates downward, the tension is smaller.

Pulleys and systems

Two masses tied by one rope over a pulley move together with the same acceleration: one goes down and the other goes up.

Treat the whole system as one object: driving force = difference of the weights, moving mass = sum of the two masses. Then find the tension by applying the second law to one mass alone.

The same idea holds when one mass sits on a smooth table and the other hangs: the driving force is only the weight of the hanging mass, and the moving mass is the sum of both; the tension is the only horizontal force on the table mass: tension = its mass × acceleration.

The elevator and the scale reading

A scale measures the force you press on it, which equals the normal force it pushes you with, not your true weight. Apply the second law vertically: reading − weight = mass × acceleration (up positive).

Elevator accelerating up: reading greater than weight (you feel heavy). Accelerating down: reading less than weight (you feel light). Constant speed: reading = weight. Free fall: reading is zero.

Laws and rules

Tension in a rope lifting an objectT−W=maUp is positive; T and W in newtons, m in kg, a in m/s2.
Acceleration of two masses over a pulleya=m1​+m2​(m1​−m2​)g​m1​ is the heavier mass; masses in kg, g and a in m/s2.
Mass on a smooth table joined to a hanging massa=m1​+m2​m2​g​m1​ is the mass on the table and m2​ the hanging mass, in kg; a in m/s2.
Scale reading in an elevatorR=m(g+a)a is positive upward and negative downward; R in newtons.

Worked examples

Take g=10m/s2. A 5kg object is lifted by a rope with an upward acceleration of 2m/s2. What is the tension?
Show the solution
  1. Weight =5×10=50N.
  2. Up is positive: T−50=5×2=10.
  3. T=50+10=60N.
  4. Check: the tension exceeds the weight because the acceleration is upward.
Answer: 60N.
Take g=10m/s2. Masses of 3kg and 2kg hang from a rope over a smooth pulley. Find the acceleration and the tension.
Show the solution
  1. Driving force =30−20=10N.
  2. Moving mass =3+2=5kg.
  3. a=510​=2m/s2.
  4. Take the 2kg mass (going up): T−20=2×2, so T=24N.
  5. Check with the 3kg mass (going down): 30−T=3×2=6, so T=24N.
Answer: Acceleration 2m/s2 and tension 24N.
Take g=10m/s2. A 50kg person stands on a scale in an elevator. What does the scale read if the elevator accelerates at 2m/s2 upward? Downward?
Show the solution
  1. Weight =50×10=500N.
  2. Upward: R−500=50×2=100, so R=600N.
  3. Downward (acceleration −2): R=50×(10−2)=400N.
  4. Check: 500−400=100=50×2.
Answer: 600N when accelerating up and 400N when accelerating down.
Common mistakes
  • Saying tension always equals weight. Correct: it equals the weight only when there is no vertical acceleration.
  • Dividing the driving force by just one mass in a pulley system. Correct: divide by the sum of both masses since they move together; in a table system the driving force comes from the hanging weight alone.
  • Thinking the elevator scale always reads the true weight. Correct: it reads the normal force, which changes with the acceleration.
  • Getting the sign of the elevator acceleration wrong. Correct: upward is positive and raises the reading; downward is negative and lowers it.
Summary
  • Tension acts along the rope; a light rope over a smooth pulley has the same tension throughout.
  • When lifting: tension − weight = mass × acceleration.
  • Pulley system: acceleration = weight difference ÷ sum of masses.
  • Elevator scale reading = mass × (g + a), up positive.

Drag and Terminal Velocity

What you will learn
  • Understand where drag comes from and how it changes
  • Explain why a falling object ends up at constant speed
  • Compute the acceleration before terminal velocity

Drag

When an object moves through air or water, the fluid pushes back against the motion with a force called drag. Drag grows with speed and with the area facing the fluid.

At the start of a fall the speed is zero, so the drag is zero. Only the weight acts, giving the largest acceleration.

How terminal velocity is reached

As the speed rises, the upward drag grows while the downward weight stays the same. The net force shrinks, so the acceleration falls, but the speed still increases.

When drag equals weight the net force is zero and so is the acceleration, so the speed stays constant: this is the terminal velocity (first law).

A heavier object of the same shape needs a higher speed before the drag reaches its larger weight, so its terminal velocity is higher; the larger the area facing the air, the lower the terminal velocity.

The object does not stop and the forces do not vanish: it falls at constant speed with balanced forces.

Opening the parachute

Opening the parachute increases the area, so the drag jumps above the weight. The net force points up, so the skydiver slows down, and as the speed drops the drag drops until it equals the weight again.

Then a new, much smaller constant speed is reached: the terminal velocity with the parachute.

Laws and rules

Acceleration of a falling object in aira=mW−D​W is weight and D is drag in newtons, m in kg; a in m/s2.
Condition for terminal velocityD=WThen the net force is zero and the acceleration is zero.

Worked examples

Take g=10m/s2. A 50kg skydiver is falling and the drag at some moment is 300N. What is the acceleration?
Show the solution
  1. Weight =50×10=500N.
  2. Net force downward =500−300=200N.
  3. a=50200​=4m/s2 downward.
Answer: 4m/s2 downward.
Take g=10m/s2. A 60kg skydiver falls at constant terminal velocity. What are the drag and the net force?
Show the solution
  1. The speed is constant, so the acceleration and the net force are zero.
  2. So the upward drag equals the downward weight.
  3. Weight =60×10=600N, so the drag is 600N.
Answer: Drag 600N and net force 0.
Take g=10m/s2. An 80kg skydiver falling at terminal velocity opens a parachute and the drag suddenly becomes 2000N. Find the acceleration at that moment and the drag when the new terminal velocity is reached.
Show the solution
  1. Weight =80×10=800N downward.
  2. Net force =2000−800=1200N upward.
  3. a=801200​=15m/s2 upward, so the skydiver slows down.
  4. At the new terminal velocity the speed drops until the drag is 800N again.
Answer: Acceleration 15m/s2 upward; final drag 800N.
Common mistakes
  • Thinking terminal velocity means no forces. Correct: the forces exist but are balanced: drag = weight.
  • Thinking the object stops at terminal velocity. Correct: it keeps falling at constant speed.
  • Thinking drag equals weight at every moment of the fall. Correct: only at terminal velocity.
  • Thinking the acceleration stays equal to g during the whole fall. Correct: it falls from g to zero as drag grows.
Summary
  • Drag opposes motion and grows with speed and area.
  • The acceleration shrinks during the fall because the net force shrinks.
  • Terminal velocity: drag = weight, net force zero.
  • Opening a parachute raises drag, so the skydiver slows to a smaller terminal velocity.
