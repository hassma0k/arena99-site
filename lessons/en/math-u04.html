Sequences as Functions

What you will learn
  • Understand a sequence as a function whose domain is the positive integers
  • Find any term from the explicit rule (nth term)
  • Find terms with a recursive rule, one after another
  • Write a rule for a sequence from its first terms

What is a sequence?

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers: a1​,a2​,a3​,… Each number is called a term, and the small number under the letter is the term position.

A sequence is really a function: we input the term number n (which is 1,2,3,…) and the term value an​ comes out. So its domain is the positive integers, and its graph is separate points, not a connected line.

The explicit rule

It gives the nth term directly, such as an​=3n−2. To find the 5th term, put 5 in place of n and calculate in order: powers, then multiplication, then addition and subtraction.

A factor (−1)n in the rule makes the signs alternate: an​=(−1)n×n gives −1,2,−3,4,…

The recursive rule

It gives the first term and the step from one term to the next, such as a1​=4 and an+1​=an​+3. Here we do not jump far ahead; we calculate the terms one after another.

Note: an+1​ is the term right after an​, not an​ plus 1.

Writing a rule from the terms

Look at how the terms change. If they grow by a constant amount d, the rule starts with dn, and then we fix the constant using the first term. Test your rule on at least two terms before you trust it.

Not every rule is linear. If the differences are not constant, compare the terms with the squares 1,4,9,16: in 2,5,10,17 each term is 1 more than the square of its position, so the rule is an​=n2+1.

Laws and rules

Explicit rulean​=f(n)an​ is the nth term, n is the term number (a positive integer), and f(n) is an expression in n.
Recursive rulean+1​=f(an​)Each term is found from the one before it, and the first term a1​ must be known.

Worked examples

The nth term of a sequence is an​=2n2−3. Find its 4th term.
Show the solution
  1. The 4th term means n=4.
  2. Substitute: a4​=2×42−3.
  3. Power first: 42=16, then multiply: 2×16=32.
  4. Then subtract: 32−3=29.
Answer: a4​=29
A sequence has a1​=2 and an+1​=2an​−1. Find its 4th term.
Show the solution
  1. Start from the first term: a1​=2.
  2. Second term: a2​=2×2−1=3.
  3. Third term: a3​=2×3−1=5.
  4. Fourth term: a4​=2×5−1=9.
Answer: a4​=9
The first terms of a sequence are 5,8,11,14,… Write its explicit rule, then find which term equals 59.
Show the solution
  1. The terms grow by 3 each time, so the rule starts with 3n.
  2. At n=1, 3n gives 3 but the first term is 5, so we add 2: an​=3n+2.
  3. Test: at n=2 we get 3×2+2=8, and at n=4 we get 14. The rule works.
  4. Set it equal to 59: 3n+2=59, so 3n=57.
  5. Then n=19, a positive integer, so 59 is the 19th term.
Answer: an​=3n+2, and 59 is the 19th term.
Common mistakes
  • Order of operations: in 2n2 at n=4 square 4 first and then multiply by 2 to get 32, not (2×4)2=64.
  • Confusing an+1​ with an​+1: the first is the next term, the second is the current term plus one.
  • With a recursive rule, trying to jump straight to a far term. Correct thinking: find each term from the one before it, in order.
  • Assuming every rule is linear. In 2,5,10,17 the differences 3,5,7 are not constant, so the rule is not linear; try n2+1.
Summary
  • A sequence is a function whose domain is the positive integers.
  • Explicit rule: put the term number in place of n and calculate.
  • Recursive rule: find each term from the one before, starting at the first.
  • When searching for a term number, n must be a positive integer.
  • If the differences are not constant, compare the terms with squares.

Arithmetic Sequences

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Recognise an arithmetic sequence and find its common difference
  • Use the nth-term rule to find any term
  • Find the number of terms, or the first term and difference from two known terms

What is an arithmetic sequence?

It is a sequence in which the difference between any two consecutive terms is constant. We call this difference the common difference, write it d, and find it with d=a2​−a1​.

If d is positive the terms rise; if it is negative they fall. The sequence 12,9,6,3 has d=9−12=−3.

Where does the rule come from?

We start at a1​ and add d at every step. To reach the nth term we take only n−1 steps, because the first term needs no step. So an​=a1​+(n−1)d.

Cases that keep appearing

  • Number of terms: put the last term in place of an​ in the rule and solve for n; it must be an integer.
  • Difference from two non-adjacent terms: divide the difference of the terms by the difference of their positions.
  • Arithmetic mean: the middle term between two terms is their average.
  • Three consecutive terms x,y,z of an arithmetic sequence: the two differences are equal, y−x=z−y, so 2y=x+z.
  • Inserting several arithmetic means between two numbers: number of steps = number of means + 1. Between 2 and 18 with three means there are 4 steps, d=416​=4, and the means are 6,10,14.
  • Real-life problems: savings that start at 20 riyals and rise by 5 riyals each week form an arithmetic sequence, and week 8 is the 8th term: 20+7×5=55 riyals.

Laws and rules

Common differenced=an+1​−an​The later term minus the earlier term; it is constant in an arithmetic sequence.
nth terman​=a1​+(n−1)da1​ is the first term, d the common difference, n the term number.
Arithmetic meany=2x+z​x,y,z are three consecutive terms of an arithmetic sequence.

Worked examples

Find the 20th term of the arithmetic sequence 5,8,11,…
Show the solution
  1. Difference: d=8−5=3, first term a1​=5.
  2. Substitute in an​=a1​+(n−1)d with n=20: a20​=5+19×3.
  3. 19×3=57, so a20​=5+57=62.
Answer: a20​=62
In an arithmetic sequence a4​=17 and a9​=42. Find a1​ and d.
Show the solution
  1. From the 4th term to the 9th is 5 steps, and each step adds d.
  2. Difference of the terms: 42−17=25, so 5d=25 and d=5.
  3. From the 1st term to the 4th is 3 steps: a4​=a1​+3×5, so 17=a1​+15 and a1​=2.
  4. Check: a9​=2+8×5=42. Correct.
Answer: a1​=2 and d=5
How many terms are in the arithmetic sequence 7,11,15,…,95?
Show the solution
  1. Difference: d=11−7=4, first term 7.
  2. Let the last term 95 be the nth term: 95=7+(n−1)×4.
  3. Subtract 7 from both sides: 88=4(n−1), then divide by 4: n−1=22.
  4. So n=23, a positive integer. Check: 7+22×4=95.
Answer: 23 terms
Common mistakes
  • Using n instead of n−1: for the 10th term multiply d by 9, not by 10.
  • Reversing the subtraction when finding the difference: it is later minus earlier. For 12,9,6 the difference is 9−12=−3, not 3.
  • When solving for the number of terms, not checking that n is an integer. If it comes out as a fraction, the given number is not a term of the sequence.
  • Given two non-adjacent terms, dividing the difference of their values by 1 or by one of the positions. The correct divisor is the number of steps between the positions: from a4​ to a9​ there are 5 steps because 9−4=5.
Summary
  • An arithmetic sequence has a constant difference d.
  • nth term: an​=a1​+(n−1)d; we take n−1 steps.
  • Number of terms: put the last term in the rule and solve for n.
  • Difference from two terms = difference of terms ÷ number of steps between them.

Arithmetic Series

What you will learn
  • Tell a sequence from a series
  • Find the sum of the first n terms of an arithmetic sequence with both formulas
  • Choose the right formula and find the number of terms when needed
  • Read and evaluate sigma notation

A series is the sum of the terms

A sequence is a list: 3,7,11. A series is the sum of its terms: 3+7+11. We write the sum of the first n terms as Sn​, and it is one number.

Gauss's idea

Write the sum twice, once in order and once reversed, then add each pair of columns. Every column gives the same amount a1​+an​, and there are n columns.

The result is double the sum, so we halve it and get Sn​=2n​(a1​+an​). In words: sum = number of terms × average of the first and last term.

Sigma notation

A sum is also written with the symbol ∑. The expression ∑k=14​(2k+1) means: substitute k=1,2,3,4 into 2k+1 and add, that is 3+5+7+9=24.

The start of k is written below the symbol and its end above it. The terms 3,5,7,9 are arithmetic, so we check with the formula: 24​(3+9)=24.

Which formula do I use?

  • If the last term is known, use the first formula.
  • If it is not, substitute an​=a1​+(n−1)d and you get the second formula.
  • If n is not known, find it first from the last term, as with arithmetic sequences.

Laws and rules

Sum using the last termSn​=2n​(a1​+an​)n is the number of terms, a1​ the first term, an​ the last term.
Sum using the differenceSn​=2n​(2a1​+(n−1)d)Used when d is known and the last term is not.

Worked examples

Find the sum of the first 20 terms of 2,5,8,…
Show the solution
  1. a1​=2, d=3, n=20.
  2. Last term: a20​=2+19×3=59.
  3. Substitute: S20​=220​(2+59)=10×61.
  4. S20​=610. Check with the second formula: 10(4+57)=610.
Answer: 610
Find the sum of the series 4+10+16+…+64.
Show the solution
  1. Difference d=6, first term 4, last term 64.
  2. Find the number of terms: 64=4+(n−1)×6, so 60=6(n−1) and n=11.
  3. Substitute: S11​=211​(4+64)=211​×68.
  4. 268​=34 and then 11×34=374.
Answer: 374
An arithmetic sequence has first term 6 and difference 4. How many terms from the start must be added to get 96?
Show the solution
  1. Use Sn​=2n​(2×6+(n−1)×4).
  2. Simplify inside the bracket: 12+4n−4=4n+8, so Sn​=n(2n+4)=2n2+4n.
  3. Set it equal to 96: 2n2+4n−96=0, then divide by 2: n2+2n−48=0.
  4. Factor: (n+8)(n−6)=0, so n=6 or n=−8. Reject the negative since n is positive.
  5. Check: 6+10+14+18+22+26=96.
Answer: 6 terms
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting to halve: the sum is 2n​(…), not n(…).
  • Putting the value of the last term in place of n. The symbol n is the number of terms, so find it from the last term first.
  • Mixing up sequence and series: Sn​ is one number, the sum, not a list of terms.
  • In the quadratic for n, keeping a negative or fractional root. n is always a positive integer.
Summary
  • A series is the sum of the terms of a sequence; Sn​ is the sum of the first n terms.
  • Sn​=2n​(a1​+an​).
  • If the last term is unknown use 2a1​+(n−1)d.
  • Find the number of terms n first if it is not given.
  • The symbol ∑ means add the values of the expression as the index runs from its start to its end.

Geometric Sequences

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Recognise a geometric sequence and find its common ratio
  • Use the nth-term rule to find any term
  • Find the ratio and the first term from two known terms

What is a geometric sequence?

It is a sequence in which every term is multiplied by a fixed number to get the next term. We call this number the common ratio, write it r, and find it by dividing: r=a1​a2​​.

To test a sequence, divide each term by the one before it; if the result is always the same, it is geometric. In 1,3,9,27 every ratio is 3, while 2,4,6,8 has different ratios so it is not geometric.

How the ratio behaves

  • r>1: positive terms grow fast.
  • 0<r<1: terms shrink toward zero.
  • r<0: the terms alternate between positive and negative.

The nth-term rule

We start from a1​ and multiply by r at each step. To reach the nth term we take n−1 steps, so an​=a1​rn−1.

Geometric mean of two numbers: the number that makes the three terms geometric. Between 3 and 27 it is ±3×27​=±9, and questions usually ask for the positive one.

Growth problems

Anything that doubles or shrinks by a fixed ratio forms a geometric sequence. A colony of 100 bacteria doubles every hour: the starting number is the first term, so after 4 hours we reach the 5th term, not the 4th: 100×24=1600.

Laws and rules

Common ratior=an​an+1​​The later term divided by the earlier term; it is constant in a geometric sequence.
nth terman​=a1​rn−1a1​ is the first term, r the ratio, n the term number.
Geometric meany2=x×zx,y,z are three consecutive terms of a geometric sequence.

Worked examples

Find the 7th term of the geometric sequence 3,6,12,…
Show the solution
  1. Ratio: r=6÷3=2, first term 3.
  2. Substitute in an​=a1​rn−1 with n=7: a7​=3×26.
  3. 26=64, so a7​=3×64=192.
Answer: a7​=192
Find the 6th term of the sequence 81,27,9,…
Show the solution
  1. Ratio: r=27÷81=31​.
  2. Substitute with n=6: a6​=81×(31​)5.
  3. (31​)5=2431​, so a6​=24381​=31​.
  4. Check by writing them out: 81,27,9,3,1,31​.
Answer: a6​=31​
In a geometric sequence a2​=6 and a5​=162. Find r and a1​.
Show the solution
  1. From the 2nd term to the 5th is 3 steps, each multiplying by r.
  2. So a5​=a2​×r3, that is 162=6r3 and r3=27.
  3. Hence r=3.
  4. First term: a1​=6÷3=2. Check: a5​=2×34=162.
Answer: r=3 and a1​=2
Common mistakes
  • Finding a difference instead of a ratio: in a geometric sequence we divide a term by the one before it; we do not subtract.
  • Writing rn instead of rn−1: the first term is not multiplied by r, so there are n−1 steps.
  • Flipping the division: r is later ÷ earlier. For 81,27 the ratio is 8127​=31​, not 3.
  • Getting the sign of a power wrong with a negative ratio: (−2)5=−32 because an odd power of a negative number is negative.
Summary
  • In a geometric sequence the ratio between consecutive terms is a constant r.
  • r = later term ÷ earlier term.
  • an​=a1​×rn−1.
  • A negative ratio makes the signs of the terms alternate.

Geometric Series and Infinite Series

★ Key
What you will learn
  • Find the sum of the first n terms of a geometric sequence
  • Know when an infinite series has a sum
  • Find the infinite sum and turn a repeating decimal into a fraction

A sum of a limited number of terms

A geometric series is the sum of the terms of a geometric sequence. To find Sn​ we multiply the sum by r and subtract: most terms cancel and Sn​−rSn​=a1​−a1​rn remains, which gives the formula below (provided r=1).

If n is not given, find it from the last term: for example in 1+2+4+…+64 we write 2n−1=64=26, so n=7.

What if the terms never end?

When ∣r∣<1 the power rn shrinks toward zero as n grows, so the sum settles near a fixed number. We say the infinite series has a sum.

If ∣r∣≥1 the terms do not shrink and the sum never settles, so we do not use the formula. For example 2+4+8+… has no sum.

Repeating decimals

The number 0.333… equals 0.3+0.03+0.003+…, an infinite geometric series with first term 0.3 and ratio 0.1, so we can find its sum as a fraction.

Laws and rules

Sum of the first n termsSn​=1−ra1​(1−rn)​a1​ is the first term, r the ratio (r=1), n the number of terms.
Infinite sumS=1−ra1​​Valid only when ∣r∣<1; a1​ is the first term.

Worked examples

Find the sum of the series 2+6+18+54+162.
Show the solution
  1. First term a1​=2, ratio r=6÷2=3, number of terms n=5.
  2. Substitute: S5​=1−32(1−35)​.
  3. 35=243, so the numerator is 2(1−243)=2×(−242)=−484 and the denominator is −2.
  4. S5​=−2−484​=242. Check by adding: 2+6+18+54+162=242.
Answer: 242
Find the sum of the infinite series 12−6+3−…
Show the solution
  1. Ratio: r=−6÷12=−21​, and ∣r∣=21​<1, so the sum exists.
  2. Substitute: S=1−(−21​)12​.
  3. Denominator: 1+21​=23​.
  4. S=12÷23​=12×32​=8.
Answer: S=8
Write the repeating decimal 0.2727… as a fraction.
Show the solution
  1. Split it: 0.2727…=0.27+0.0027+0.000027+…
  2. This is a geometric series with first term 10027​ and ratio 1001​ (the repeating block has two digits, so we divide by 100 each step), and ∣r∣<1.
  3. S=1−1001​10027​​=10099​10027​​.
  4. Divide: 10027​×99100​=9927​=113​.
  5. Check: 3÷11=0.2727…
Answer: 113​
Common mistakes
  • Using the infinite-sum formula when ∣r∣≥1. In 3+6+12+… the ratio is 2 and the series has no sum.
  • A sign slip with a negative ratio: 1−(−21​)=23​, not 21​. For the sum of 5 terms with ratio −2 we get (−2)5=−32, so 1−(−32)=33.
  • Writing rn−1 in the sum formula. The sum uses 1−rn; rn−1 belongs to the nth term only.
  • Taking n as the largest exponent instead of the number of terms: in 1+2+…+64 the exponents run 0 to 6, so there are 7 terms, not 6.
Summary
  • Sn​=1−ra1​(1−rn)​, provided r=1.
  • Find the number of terms n from the last term using powers.
  • An infinite series has a sum only if |r| < 1.
  • S=1−ra1​​; use it to turn a repeating decimal into a fraction.

The Binomial Theorem

What you will learn
  • Expand a power of a sum of two terms using Pascal's triangle
  • Know the number of terms and the powers in each term
  • Find a specific term or coefficient with the general term

The shape of the expansion

When we expand (x+y)n we pick x or y from each bracket. So the powers of x fall from n to 0, the powers of y rise from 0 to n, and the two powers in every term add up to n.

So the expansion has n+1 terms. In (x+y)6 the powers of x are 6,5,4,3,2,1,0: seven terms.

Coefficients: Pascal's triangle

Each number in the triangle is the sum of the two numbers above it, and each row begins and ends with 1. Row number n (counting starts at row 0) gives the coefficients of (x+y)n.

The general term

To find one term without writing the whole expansion we use the general term. The term in position k+1 (starting from k=0) is its coefficient times xn−kyk.

The sign ! means the product of the numbers from the number itself down to 1, for example 4!=4×3×2×1=24.

If n is even, the expansion has a single middle term in position 2n​+1. In (2x+1)4 the middle term is the third (k=2): 6×(2x)2×1=24x2.

With signs or coefficients

Put the first and second terms in brackets with their signs before raising them to the power, such as (2x)3=8x3 and (−1)3=−1.

Laws and rules

General termTk+1​=k!(n−k)!n!​xn−kykn is the power of the bracket, k starts at 0, and the fraction is the coefficient in row n of Pascal's triangle.
Number of termsn+1n is the power of the binomial.

Worked examples

Expand (x+2)3.
Show the solution
  1. Row 3 of Pascal's triangle: 1,3,3,1.
  2. Powers of x falling: x3,x2,x,1 and powers of 2 rising: 1,2,4,8.
  3. Multiply: 1×x3+3×2x2+3×4x+1×8.
  4. Result: x3+6x2+12x+8. Check at x=1: 27=1+6+12+8.
Answer: x3+6x2+12x+8
Find the coefficient of x3 in the expansion of (x+2)5.
Show the solution
  1. The power of x is 5−k, and we want 5−k=3, so k=2 (the third term).
  2. The coefficient in row 5 at k=2: row 5 is 1,5,10,10,5,1, so the value is 10. Check: 2!×3!5!​=12120​=10.
  3. The power of 2 is k=2: 22=4.
  4. The coefficient: 10×4=40.
Answer: 40
Expand (2x−1)4.
Show the solution
  1. Row 4: 1,4,6,4,1. The two terms are 2x and −1.
  2. k=0: 1×(2x)4=16x4.
  3. k=1: 4×(2x)3×(−1)=4×8x3×(−1)=−32x3.
  4. k=2: 6×(2x)2×1=24x2. And k=3: 4×2x×(−1)=−8x. And k=4: 1.
  5. Check at x=1: 1=16−32+24−8+1.
Answer: 16x4−32x3+24x2−8x+1
Common mistakes
  • Counting n terms instead of n+1: the power starts at 0, which adds one term.
  • Raising only x to the power and leaving the coefficient: (2x)3=8x3, not 2x3.
  • Forgetting the negative sign: when the second term is negative, the signs of the expansion alternate.
  • Wrong position of a term: the third term has k=2, not k=3, because counting starts at k=0.
Summary
  • (x+y)n has n+1 terms, and the two powers in each term add to n.
  • Row n of Pascal's triangle gives the coefficients.
  • The term in position k+1 has xn−kyk.
  • Put each term in brackets before raising it to a power, and watch the signs.

Mathematical Induction

What you will learn
  • Know the steps of a proof by mathematical induction
  • Prove a sum formula by induction
  • Prove a divisibility statement by induction

The row-of-dominoes idea

We want to prove a statement true for every positive integer n. Testing many numbers cannot do it, because the numbers never end. Induction solves this like falling dominoes: the first falls, each one knocks over the next, so the whole row falls.

The steps

  • Base: check the statement at n=1.
  • Assumption: suppose it is true at n=k.
  • Step: prove, using the assumption, that it is true at n=k+1.
  • Conclusion: so the statement is true for every positive integer.

The secret of the third step

With sums: the sum of the first k+1 terms = the sum of the first k terms + term number (k+1). Replace the sum of k terms by what we assumed, then simplify until you reach the same formula at k+1.

With divisibility: write the new power in terms of the old one, then pull out the required factor.

Laws and rules

Sum of natural numbers1+2+…+n=2n(n+1)​A well-known example proved by induction; n is a positive integer.

Worked examples

Prove by induction that 1+2+…+n=2n(n+1)​ for every positive integer n.
Show the solution
  1. Base n=1: left side 1, right side 21×2​=1. True.
  2. Assumption: 1+2+…+k=2k(k+1)​.
  3. At n=k+1 add the term (k+1) to both sides: the sum =2k(k+1)​+(k+1).
  4. Take out the factor (k+1): (k+1)(2k​+1)=(k+1)×2k+2​.
  5. This is 2(k+1)((k+1)+1)​, the same formula at n=k+1. So the statement is true for every n.
Answer: Proved.
Prove by induction that 1+3+5+…+(2n−1)=n2.
Show the solution
  1. Base n=1: left side 1 and right side 12=1. True.
  2. Assumption: 1+3+…+(2k−1)=k2.
  3. The next term at n=k+1 is 2(k+1)−1=2k+1. Add it: k2+2k+1.
  4. k2+2k+1=(k+1)2, the formula at n=k+1. So the statement is true for every n.
Answer: Proved.
Prove by induction that 4n−1 is divisible by 3 for every positive integer n.
Show the solution
  1. Base n=1: 41−1=3 is divisible by 3.
  2. Assumption: 4k−1=3m for some integer m, that is 4k=3m+1.
  3. At n=k+1: 4k+1−1=4×4k−1=4(3m+1)−1.
  4. Simplify: 12m+4−1=12m+3=3(4m+1), a multiple of 3. So the statement is true for every n.
Answer: Proved.
Common mistakes
  • Skipping the base step. Without it the first domino may never fall, so knowing each one knocks over the next is useless.
  • Assuming the statement at n=k+1 instead of n=k. That is circular reasoning; the assumption is made at k only.
  • Adding the term k instead of the term (k+1) when moving on in a sum. The new term always has number k+1.
  • Being satisfied with testing a few values of n. Being true at 1,2,3 does not prove it for every n.
Summary
  • Induction proves a statement for every positive integer n.
  • Base: check at n = 1.
  • Assume at n = k, then prove at n = k + 1 using the assumption.
  • In sums, add the term (k + 1) to the sum of k terms.
  • For divisibility, write the new power in terms of the old one, then pull out the required factor.
